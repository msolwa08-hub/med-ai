# Emergency Medicine — depth reference

*Scope: the undifferentiated adult and paediatric patient presenting to a South African
state-hospital emergency centre (EC). Written to the DEPTH-BAR six-part structure. The ED lives
on undifferentiated presentations, so every syndrome LEADS with the differential and the
must-not-miss list before management.*

*Sources reached: South African Triage Scale (SATS) + TEWS training material and validation
studies (EMSSA; Twomey et al. paediatric SATS; PMC validation cohorts); SA snakebite guideline
(Hardcastle/SASS SAMJ CME 2023; Müller et al. CME); SA/Western Cape Poisons Information Helpline
(0861 555 777) & AfriTox; WFSA organophosphate management; paracetamol/NAC nomogram literature.
Global anchors [EK]: ATLS 10th ed; ACLS/AHA 2020; Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021; Wells/PERC,
Ottawa, Canadian C-spine & CT-head, NEXUS, Parkland, Centor, CURB-65, HEART. Full source list at
end. Items marked [EK] rest on established knowledge, not a fetched source.*

## 1. The consultant's mental model

The EC consultant does not think in diagnoses first — they think in **threats to life, in ABCDE
order, on a clock**. Three questions run continuously behind every encounter:

1. **"Is this patient about to die in front of me, and from what airway/breathing/circulation/
   disability problem?"** — the resuscitation reflex. Triage colour and TEWS set the tempo before
   a history is taken. A "sick vs not sick" gestalt in the first 10 seconds (work of breathing,
   colour, mentation, whether they can speak in full sentences) overrides any number.
2. **"Of the killers that present *exactly like this benign thing*, which have I actively
   excluded?"** — the undifferentiated mindset. Chest pain is ACS *until* the 6 killers are
   addressed; the young woman with "gastro" has an ectopic until βhCG says otherwise; the drunk
   with a headache has a subarachnoid until the story and exam say otherwise. The consultant is
   *always* secretly ruling out: **hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, ectopic pregnancy, sepsis, ACS,
   subarachnoid, and occult haemorrhagic shock.**
3. **"What is the reversible cause, and what is the time window?"** — glucose-first in every
   altered patient, βhCG in every woman of reproductive age, a lactate/gas in anyone who looks
   unwell, and the clock on STEMI (door-to-needle <30 min / door-to-balloon <90 min), stroke
   thrombolysis (<4.5 h), and sepsis antibiotics (<1 h).

**Tempo and re-triage.** The waiting room is where patients die. A "yellow" TEWS trending toward
"orange" in the queue is a re-triage event. The consultant treats the trajectory of serial vitals
as data, not the single snapshot. **Occult (compensated) shock** — normal BP with a rising heart
rate, narrowing pulse pressure, and a climbing lactate — is the single most dangerous pattern a
junior misses because "the blood pressure was fine."

**Resuscitate as you find problems.** You do not complete the survey and then act; you fix the
airway before you assess breathing. Every intervention is followed by **reassessment** ("has the
chest drain improved the sats?"). Anchoring on the first plausible label — and abandoning the
systematic ABCDE — is the classic killer.

**The SA overlay is constant.** HIV/TB colours every differential (meningitis, PJP, TB pericardial
effusion, disseminated sepsis, cryptococcus). The trauma and interpersonal-violence load is
enormous, so **penetrating trauma, GSW/stab, and the J88 medico-legal obligation** are part of
routine ED cognition, not a subspecialty. Resource reality (no CT at the district level, limited
troponin, antivenom stock-outs, no cath lab) means the consultant thinks in **thrombolysis not PCI,
clinical scores not CT-first, and transfer decisions**.

### 2.1 Undifferentiated CHEST PAIN — the 6 killers

**Differential.**
- *(a) Common:* musculoskeletal/costochondritis, GORD/oesophageal spasm, anxiety/hyperventilation,
  pneumonia/pleurisy, stable angina, herpes zoster, biliary/pancreatic referred pain.
- *(b) Must-not-miss — THE SIX KILLERS:*
  1. **Acute coronary syndrome (STEMI/NSTEMI/unstable angina).**
  2. **Pulmonary embolism.**
  3. **Aortic dissection.**
  4. **Cardiac tamponade.**
  5. **Tension pneumothorax.**
  6. **Oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave).**
- *(c) Zebras/juniors-forget:* TB or uraemic pericarditis/effusion (common in SA), myocarditis
  (often viral/HIV), cocaine/methamphetamine-induced coronary vasospasm, sickle-cell acute chest
  syndrome, Takotsubo, oesophageal foreign body, coronary dissection in a peripartum woman.

**The discriminators (what actually splits the six):**

| Killer | Pain character | Key discriminator | Bedside finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| ACS | Central, heavy, radiates arm/jaw, exertional, diaphoresis, nausea | Risk factors + ECG dynamic + troponin delta | Often normal exam; sweaty, grey |
| PE | Pleuritic, sudden, dyspnoea dominant | Risk factors (immobility, malignancy, OCP, post-partum, HIV), unilateral leg swelling | Tachycardia, hypoxia, ↑RR out of proportion |
| Dissection | **Tearing/ripping**, maximal at onset, **migrates** front→back | **BP/pulse differential** >20 mmHg between arms; new AR murmur; radiates interscapular | Pulse deficit, focal neuro (carotid), diastolic murmur |
| Tamponade | Dull, positional (better sitting forward) | **Beck's triad**: hypotension + muffled sounds + raised JVP; **pulsus paradoxus** >10 mmHg | Quiet heart, distended neck veins, tachycardia |
| Tension pneumo | Sudden pleuritic + severe dyspnoea | **Tracheal deviation away, absent breath sounds, hyper-resonance** on affected side; distended neck veins + HYPOtension | Clinical diagnosis — DO NOT wait for CXR |
| Boerhaave | Severe, after **forceful vomiting**, radiates to back | **Subcutaneous emphysema (crepitus in neck)**, Mackler triad (vomiting + chest pain + surgical emphysema) | Odynophagia, crepitus, unwell/septic |

**Discriminating history:** onset (maximal-at-onset = dissection/PE/pneumo; crescendo = ACS);
character (tearing vs pressure vs pleuritic); radiation (interscapular = dissection; arm/jaw =
ACS); relation to breathing/position/swallowing; risk factors for VTE; preceding vomiting
(Boerhaave); cocaine/tik use; HIV status (pericarditis, PE risk).

**Discriminating exam:** **BP in BOTH arms** (dissection), JVP + heart sounds + pulsus paradoxus
(tamponade), tracheal position + breath sounds (tension pneumo), surgical emphysema (Boerhaave),
calves (DVT), and a focused neuro (dissection can cause stroke/limb ischaemia).

**Investigations & interpretation:**
- **12-lead ECG within 10 min** — the single most time-critical test. STEMI criteria: ≥1 mm ST
  elevation in ≥2 contiguous limb leads, or ≥2 mm (men <40: ≥2.5 mm) / ≥1.5 mm (women) in V2–V3.
  New LBBB in an ischaemic presentation → treat as STEMI. **Always look for the ECG that hides an
  MI** (see §4.1: posterior, RV, de Winter, Wellens, aVR elevation).
- **Troponin (hs) 0/1h or 0/3h delta** — a *single* normal troponin at <3 h does NOT exclude ACS;
  it is the **delta** (rise/fall) that rules in. A flat, non-dynamic mildly-raised troponin fits
  renal failure, PE, sepsis, myocarditis — not necessarily ACS.
- **CXR** — widened mediastinum (>8 cm) in dissection, pneumomediastinum/effusion (Boerhaave),
  small/absent lung markings (pneumo — but tension is clinical). Often normal in PE.
- **D-dimer** — only useful in a **low-probability (Wells) + PERC-negative** patient to rule out PE;
  useless (and harmful) if ordered indiscriminately. Age-adjusted cutoff (age×10 µg/L over 50).
- **POCUS/eFAST** — pericardial effusion + tamponade physiology (RV diastolic collapse), lung
  sliding absent + no B-lines (pneumo), RV strain/dilatation (massive PE), aortic flap.

**Management anchors (SA STG):** MONA is dead — give **aspirin 300 mg chewed** + a P2Y12 inhibitor
(clopidogrel 300–600 mg loading) for ACS; oxygen only if sats <90%; nitrates for ongoing pain/
hypertension (NOT if inferior/RV infarct or hypotensive). **STEMI → thrombolysis (tenecteplase
weight-banded, or streptokinase 1.5 MU over 1 h) within 30 min door-to-needle** where PCI is
unavailable (the district reality); refer for rescue PCI if reperfusion fails. Tension pneumo →
**immediate needle/finger thoracostomy** then intercostal drain. Tamponade → **pericardiocentesis**.
Dissection → **BP control (target SBP 100–120, HR <60; labetalol/esmolol first, then vasodilator)**
+ urgent surgical/vascular referral. Boerhaave → NBM, broad-spectrum antibiotics, surgical referral.

**Traps & pearls:**
- **Never give a thrombolytic (or full anticoagulation) to undiagnosed "ACS" that is actually
  dissection** — check both arms and think about the tearing pain FIRST.
- Inferior STEMI: get a **right-sided ECG (V4R)** — RV infarction means nitrates/morphine can
  crash the BP; these patients are preload-dependent and need **fluid**, not GTN.
- A normal ECG and one normal troponin does **not** discharge a high-risk chest pain — use HEART
  score + serial troponin.
- Cocaine/tik chest pain: benzodiazepines first, **avoid pure beta-blockers** (unopposed alpha).
- The "gastritis" that is really an inferior MI, and the "anxiety attack" that is really a PE — the
  ECG and the risk factors, not the affect, decide.

### 2.2 BREATHLESSNESS (undifferentiated dyspnoea)

**Differential.**
- *(a) Common:* asthma, COPD exacerbation, community-acquired pneumonia, LVF/pulmonary oedema,
  anaemia, anxiety.
- *(b) Must-not-miss:* PE, tension pneumothorax, acute pulmonary oedema (flash/cardiogenic),
  anaphylaxis, DKA (Kussmaul — it's a *metabolic* dyspnoea), severe sepsis, upper-airway
  obstruction/angio-oedema, massive pleural/pericardial effusion.
- *(c) Zebras/SA:* **PJP (HIV — desaturation on exertion, weeks of dry cough)**, TB (miliary,
  large effusion, pericardial), cardiomyopathy (HIV, peripartum, alcohol), salicylate toxicity
  (tachypnoea + mixed acid-base), methaemoglobinaemia (saturation gap), Guillain-Barré/organophosphate
  (neuromuscular respiratory failure — falling vital capacity with "normal" lungs).

**Discriminating history:** speed of onset (minutes = pneumo/PE/anaphylaxis/pulmonary oedema; days
= pneumonia/effusion/PJP); orthopnoea/PND (LVF); wheeze vs stridor (lower vs upper airway); fever
(infection); exertional desaturation + HIV (PJP); chest pain (PE/pneumo); leg swelling (VTE/CCF).

**Discriminating exam:** **work of breathing and ability to speak** (the single best severity
sign); stridor (upper airway — emergency); silent chest in asthma (peri-arrest); crepitations +
raised JVP + gallop (LVF); unilateral absent breath sounds (pneumo/effusion); Kussmaul breathing
(metabolic acidosis); saturation that doesn't fit the picture (methaemoglobinaemia, CO).

**Investigations & interpretation:** SpO₂ + **VBG/ABG** (the crashing patient — see §4.2: a rising
CO₂ in a tiring asthmatic is peri-arrest; a metabolic acidosis in a "breathless" patient means DKA/
sepsis/toxin, not lung); **peak flow** (asthma severity vs personal best); CXR; **POCUS lung**
(B-lines = oedema, absent sliding = pneumo, consolidation, effusion); ECG + troponin + BNP for
cardiac; D-dimer/CTPA pathway for PE.

**Management anchors:** treat the physiology while you diagnose — sit up, high-flow O₂ (target
88–92% in COPD, 94–98% otherwise), nebulised salbutamol±ipratropium + steroid for obstructive;
**GTN + furosemide + CPAP/NIV for cardiogenic pulmonary oedema** (CPAP is the workhorse); IM
adrenaline for anaphylaxis; needle decompression for tension pneumo; antibiotics + fluids for
pneumonia/sepsis.

**Traps & pearls:** a "normal" or falling RR and rising CO₂ in a previously tachypnoeic asthmatic
is **exhaustion = imminent arrest**, not improvement. Don't be reassured by a normal CXR in PE.
The breathless HIV patient with clear-ish lungs and profound exertional desaturation is **PJP until
proven otherwise** — start co-trimoxazole + steroid. Kussmaul breathing = **check a glucose and a
gas** before you reach for a nebuliser.

### 2.3 COLLAPSE / SYNCOPE

**The core split: cardiac vs neurological vs mechanical/reflex — because cardiac syncope kills.**

**Differential.**
- *(a) Common/benign:* vasovagal (reflex) syncope, orthostatic hypotension (drugs, dehydration,
  autonomic), situational (micturition, cough).
- *(b) Must-not-miss (cardiac & catastrophic):* **arrhythmia** (VT, complete heart block, SVT,
  pauses), **structural** (aortic stenosis, HOCM, tamponade), **massive PE**, **ruptured AAA/
  ectopic (haemorrhagic)**, **subarachnoid haemorrhage**, MI, GI bleed. Also **hypoglycaemia**.
- *(c) Zebras/ECG channelopathies:* **long QT, Brugada, WPW, ARVC**, HOCM in the young athlete,
  aortic dissection with syncope, subclavian steal, carotid sinus hypersensitivity.

**Distinguishing syncope from seizure:** true syncope = brief, rapid recovery, pallor, sometimes a
few myoclonic jerks (convulsive syncope — do not mislabel as epilepsy); seizure = aura, tongue-
biting (**lateral** tongue is specific), post-ictal confusion >5 min, incontinence (non-specific).

**Discriminating history:** **posture and trigger** (exertional syncope = cardiac/HOCM/AS until
proven; syncope lying down or with palpitations = arrhythmia); prodrome (nausea/sweating = vasovagal;
none = arrhythmia); **family history of sudden death <40** (channelopathy); chest pain (MI/PE/
dissection); pregnancy (ectopic); melaena (GI bleed); medications (QT-prolonging, antihypertensives,
insulin).

**Discriminating exam:** postural BP (drop >20 systolic / >10 diastolic), **ejection systolic
murmur radiating to carotids (AS)**, signs of PE/DVT, PR + abdominal exam (AAA, GI bleed), focal
neuro.

**Investigations & interpretation:** **ECG is mandatory** — hunt the red flags (see §4 and §5):
QTc >500 ms, delta wave (WPW), Brugada morphology, bifascicular/trifascicular block, Mobitz II/
complete heart block, Epsilon wave, deep S in III/inverted T (PE), ischaemia. **Glucose. βhCG.**
Hb (occult bleed). Troponin if cardiac suspected. Consider the **San Francisco / Canadian Syncope**
risk tools for disposition.

**Management anchors:** admit/monitor any syncope with an abnormal ECG, exertional syncope, syncope
+ chest pain/dyspnoea, family history of sudden death, structural heart disease, age with red
flags, or an unexplained event. Discharge the classic young vasovagal with a normal ECG and normal
exam, safety-netted.

**Traps & pearls:** **exertional syncope is cardiac until proven** — never discharge it as
vasovagal. Convulsive syncope is mislabelled as a first fit and worked up as epilepsy — get the
ECG. Syncope in an older woman with abdominal/back pain = **ruptured AAA**; in a young woman =
**ectopic**. Always take a **collateral** history — the patient's account of a syncopal event is
unreliable.

### 2.4 THE SHOCKED PATIENT — the shock-type algorithm

**Shock = inadequate tissue perfusion (rising lactate, altered mentation, oliguria, cool/mottled
or warm/flushed periphery), which may exist with a normal BP (compensated).** The algorithm sorts
by mechanism because the treatments diverge:

| Type | Mechanism | Bedside pattern | JVP / preload | Key SA causes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypovolaemic / haemorrhagic** | Lost volume | Cold, tachycardic, flat veins, narrow pulse pressure | Low | Trauma/GSW/stab, GI bleed, ruptured ectopic, DKA |
| **Distributive (septic / anaphylactic / neurogenic)** | Vasodilation | Warm, bounding early → cold late | Low/normal | Sepsis, anaphylaxis, spinal injury |
| **Cardiogenic** | Pump failure | Cold, raised JVP, crepitations, gallop | High | MI, arrhythmia, cardiomyopathy, myocarditis |
| **Obstructive** | Mechanical block | Cold, raised JVP, ± tracheal shift / muffled sounds | High | Tension pneumo, tamponade, massive PE |

**Discriminating history/exam:** trauma/bleeding source; fever/source of sepsis; allergen exposure;
chest pain/ECG (cardiogenic/PE); the **JVP is the great divider** — flat in hypovolaemic/septic,
raised in cardiogenic/obstructive. **POCUS (RUSH protocol)** answers "pump, tank, pipes": pericardial
effusion, IVC collapse, LV function, free fluid, aortic aneurysm, RV strain, pneumothorax.

**Investigations & interpretation:** **lactate + base deficit** as the resuscitation gauge (lactate
>2 = occult hypoperfusion; >4 = severe; **clearance** = adequate resuscitation); Hb (may be normal
early in acute haemorrhage — trend it); VBG; **shock index (HR/SBP >0.9 = significant, >1.0 flags
occult haemorrhage/PE)** — often abnormal before the BP falls.

**Management anchors:** control catastrophic haemorrhage FIRST (C-ABC); IV access ×2 wide-bore;
crystalloid boluses **250–500 mL and reassess** (not reflex litres, which harm in cardiogenic and
haemorrhagic shock — permissive hypotension in trauma, target SBP ~90 / palpable radial pulse until
bleeding controlled); **blood, not crystalloid, for haemorrhage**; adrenaline for anaphylaxis;
early antibiotics + source control for sepsis; needle decompression/pericardiocentesis for
obstructive; noradrenaline once filled but still hypotensive.

**Traps & pearls:** normal BP does not exclude shock (compensated — watch HR, pulse pressure,
lactate). Don't flood the cardiogenic or the actively-bleeding patient. The young trauma patient
maintains BP until ~30–40% blood loss then crashes precipitously (Class III–IV). Neurogenic shock =
hypotension **with bradycardia** (loss of sympathetic tone) — the exception to "shock is tachycardic."

### 2.5 ALTERED MENTAL STATUS / reduced GCS

**Glucose first, always. Then the reversible causes.**

**Differential — mnemonic AEIOU-TIPS overlaid on reversibles:**
- *(a) Common:* hypoglycaemia, alcohol intoxication, post-ictal, sepsis/delirium, hypoxia/CO₂
  narcosis, drugs.
- *(b) Must-not-miss:* **hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, meningitis/encephalitis, intracranial haemorrhage/
  stroke, raised ICP, DKA/HHS, hyponatraemia, hypertensive encephalopathy, opioid/sedative
  overdose, non-convulsive status epilepticus, Wernicke's encephalopathy**.
- *(c) Zebras/SA:* **cryptococcal or TB meningitis, HIV encephalopathy, cerebral malaria,
  neurosyphilis, hepatic/uraemic encephalopathy, thyroid storm/myxoedema coma, adrenal crisis,
  CO poisoning, carbon-monoxide/toxin**, thiamine deficiency, hypercalcaemia.

**Discriminating history (collateral essential):** speed of onset, fever, headache, trauma, drug/
alcohol, diabetes, HIV/TB, psychiatric history, access to poisons.

**Discriminating exam:** **glucose + temperature + SpO₂ first**; pupils (pinpoint = opioid/pontine/
organophosphate; fixed dilated = herniation/anticholinergic; unequal = mass/herniation); neck
stiffness (meningism); focal signs (stroke/mass); needle marks; **fundi** (papilloedema, subhyaloid
haemorrhage in SAH); asterixis (metabolic); smell (ketones, alcohol, fetor).

**Investigations & interpretation:** glucose, VBG (pH, CO₂, lactate, Na⁺, K⁺), U&E (Na⁺, urea/
creatinine, Ca²⁺), FBC + septic screen, ammonia/LFTs, **paracetamol + salicylate levels + osmolar
gap** (toxic alcohols), TFTs/cortisol if indicated, blood cultures + malaria smear, **CT brain**
before LP if focal signs/papilloedema/immunocompromised, then **LP** (meningitis; in SA send for
TB, cryptococcal antigen/India ink, cell count, glucose, protein).

**Management anchors:** the empirical **"coma cocktail" as indicated** — **glucose 50 mL of 50%
dextrose** if hypoglycaemic (with **thiamine 100 mg IV BEFORE glucose** in the malnourished/
alcoholic to prevent Wernicke); **naloxone 0.4–2 mg** for opioid toxidrome; **oxygen**. Treat
meningitis empirically within the hour (ceftriaxone ± aciclovir ± TB cover, + dexamethasone) — do
not delay antibiotics for the LP. Protect the airway (GCS ≤8 → intubate).

**Traps & pearls:** **never give glucose to a malnourished/alcoholic patient without thiamine**.
Every altered patient gets a **glucose and a temperature** before anything cerebral is blamed.
Non-convulsive status hides in the "post-ictal" patient who doesn't wake up — consider EEG/
empirical benzodiazepine. In SA, cryptococcal and TB meningitis are subacute and easily missed —
low threshold for LP in HIV.

### 2.6 THE FITTING PATIENT / STATUS EPILEPTICUS

**Differential.**
- *(a) Common:* known epilepsy (missed meds, sleep deprivation, alcohol), febrile convulsion
  (child), alcohol withdrawal, hypoglycaemia.
- *(b) Must-not-miss:* **hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, eclampsia (pregnant/post-
  partum), meningitis/encephalitis, intracranial haemorrhage/mass, hypoxia, toxin (TCA, INH,
  tramadol, organophosphate)**.
- *(c) Zebras/SA:* **neurocysticercosis (leading cause of adult-onset seizures in SA)**, TB/
  cryptococcal meningitis, cerebral malaria, HIV mass lesion (toxoplasmosis), isoniazid overdose
  (needs **pyridoxine**), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, PRES/hypertensive encephalopathy.

**Discriminating history/exam:** known epilepsy + compliance; **pregnancy/post-partum → eclampsia**;
fever/immunosuppression; head injury; drugs/withdrawal; focal onset (structural). Check glucose,
Na⁺, Ca²⁺, βhCG, temperature, BP (eclampsia/hypertensive).

**Management anchors — status = seizure >5 min or recurrent without recovery:**
1. ABC, O₂, **glucose** (treat if low), IV access.
2. **Benzodiazepine:** IV lorazepam 4 mg (or diazepam 10 mg IV, or **midazolam 10 mg IM/buccal**
   if no IV) — repeat once at 5–10 min.
3. **Second-line load:** phenytoin 20 mg/kg IV (max 50 mg/min, cardiac monitor) OR sodium valproate/
   levetiracetam.
4. **Refractory:** RSI + intubate + infusion (midazolam/propofol/thiopentone) → ICU.
- **Eclampsia:** **magnesium sulphate** (loading 4 g IV over 20 min + 1 g/h infusion, or the
  Pritchard IM regimen), control BP, deliver — benzodiazepines are NOT the primary drug.
- **Isoniazid seizures:** **pyridoxine** gram-for-gram of INH ingested (empirical 5 g).

**Traps & pearls:** any seizing woman of reproductive age is **eclampsia until excluded** — give
magnesium, not just a benzo. Check glucose in EVERY fit. Don't forget pyridoxine in the INH/TB
context. Prolonged "post-ictal" state = consider **non-convulsive status**.

### 2.7 HEADACHE — the thunderclap workup

**Differential.**
- *(a) Common:* tension-type, migraine, viral/sinusitis, medication-overuse, cervicogenic.
- *(b) Must-not-miss:* **subarachnoid haemorrhage, bacterial/TB/cryptococcal meningitis,
  cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, raised ICP/space-occupying lesion, giant cell arteritis
  (>50 y), acute angle-closure glaucoma, carbon monoxide, hypertensive emergency, pituitary
  apoplexy, carotid/vertebral dissection**.
- *(c) Zebras/SA:* **cryptococcal meningitis with raised ICP (HIV)**, TB meningitis, cerebral
  toxoplasmosis, neurocysticercosis, CVST in the peripartum/dehydrated/OCP patient, pre-eclampsia.

**Thunderclap red flags (SNOOP):** **S**udden/Systemic (fever, weight loss, HIV), **N**eurological
deficit/reduced consciousness, **O**nset sudden ("worst ever," maximal <1 min — SAH), **O**lder
(new headache >50 — GCA/tumour), **P**apilloedema/Positional/Precipitated by exertion/valsalva/
Pattern change.

**Discriminating history:** **time to peak intensity** (instantaneous = SAH/dissection/CVST vs
gradual); worst-ever/first-ever; fever + neck stiffness (meningitis); positional/worse lying or on
valsalva (raised ICP/CVST); visual halos + red painful eye (glaucoma); jaw claudication + scalp
tenderness (GCA); pregnancy/post-partum (CVST/pre-eclampsia).

**Discriminating exam:** neck stiffness/Kernig/Brudzinski, focal neuro, **fundoscopy** (papilloedema,
subhyaloid haemorrhage), BP, temperature, red eye + fixed mid-dilated pupil (glaucoma), temporal
artery tenderness.

**Investigations & interpretation:** **non-contrast CT brain** — near-100% sensitive for SAH within
6 h of onset, falling thereafter; a **negative CT after 6 h does NOT exclude SAH → LP** (looking for
xanthochromia; RBCs that don't clear tube-1-to-tube-4). LP also for meningitis (send TB, CrAg, cell
count/protein/glucose). CT **venogram** for CVST (raised D-dimer supports). ESR/CRP for GCA (start
steroids empirically if suspected — don't wait). Malaria smear where relevant.

**Management anchors:** empirical meningitis treatment within the hour; nimodipine + neurosurgical
referral for SAH; anticoagulation for CVST; high-dose steroid for GCA; urgent ophthalmology +
pressure-lowering for glaucoma.

**Traps & pearls:** a normal CT does **not** exclude SAH beyond 6 h — LP for xanthochromia. Do not
LP before CT if focal signs/papilloedema/immunocompromised (coning risk). In HIV, cryptococcal
meningitis presents with raised-ICP headache and a near-normal CT — **measure and drain CSF
pressure** (therapeutic LPs). The "migraine" that is the worst-ever and sudden is a SAH.

### 2.8 ABDOMINAL PAIN (ED lens)

**Differential.**
- *(a) Common:* gastroenteritis, non-specific/constipation, biliary colic, renal colic, PID,
  urinary retention, gastritis/PUD.
- *(b) Must-not-miss:* **ruptured AAA, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, mesenteric ischaemia,
  perforated viscus, bowel obstruction/strangulation, acute pancreatitis, ascending cholangitis,
  MI (inferior — epigastric), DKA, testicular/ovarian torsion, ruptured spleen (trauma/malaria)**.
- *(c) Zebras/SA:* **abdominal TB (mesenteric nodes, TB peritonitis, ileal), typhoid perforation,
  amoebic liver abscess, sickle-cell crisis, lead colic, adrenal crisis, DKA masquerading as an
  acute abdomen, HIV enteropathy**.

**Discriminating history:** **βhCG in every reproductive-age woman** (ectopic); pain out of
proportion to exam (mesenteric ischaemia); back/flank + collapse (AAA); referred shoulder tip
(diaphragmatic/blood — Kehr's sign); vomiting/absolute constipation (obstruction); alcohol/gallstones
(pancreatitis).

**Discriminating exam:** peritonism (guarding, rebound, rigidity), pulsatile mass (AAA), hernial
orifices, PR (melaena, mass), external genitalia (torsion — a missed torsion is a lost testis),
signs of shock.

**Investigations:** **βhCG, VBG + lactate (mesenteric ischaemia, shock), amylase/lipase, U&E, FBC,
LFTs, urine dip**; erect CXR/AXR (free air, obstruction); **POCUS/eFAST + AAA scan**; CT where
available. Raised lactate + severe pain + AF/vascular risk = **mesenteric ischaemia**.

**Traps & pearls:** ectopic and AAA are the classic ED misses — βhCG and a POCUS of the aorta cost
minutes. Mesenteric ischaemia = pain out of proportion + a rising lactate + a "soft" abdomen early.
Inferior MI presents as epigastric pain — ECG the older diabetic with "indigestion." DKA and adrenal
crisis both mimic a surgical abdomen.

### 2.9 PALPITATIONS

**Differential.**
- *(a) Common:* sinus tachycardia (anxiety, fever, anaemia, dehydration, salbutamol/caffeine),
  ectopics, AF/flutter, SVT.
- *(b) Must-not-miss:* **VT, WPW with pre-excited AF, long-QT/torsades, complete heart block,
  thyrotoxicosis/storm, electrolyte (K⁺/Mg²⁺), PE, ACS, digoxin toxicity, phaeochromocytoma**.
- *(c) Zebras:* Brugada, catecholaminergic polymorphic VT, cocaine/tik, TCA toxicity.

**Discriminating history/exam:** regular vs irregular; abrupt onset/offset (SVT); syncope/family
sudden death (channelopathy/VT); chest pain; thyroid symptoms; drugs. Haemodynamic stability is the
first branch point.

**Investigations:** **12-lead ECG** (rate, regularity, QRS width, delta wave, QTc, ischaemia);
U&E/Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺, TFTs, troponin, FBC (anaemia), βhCG.

**Management anchors [EK ACLS]:** **unstable (shock, chest pain, pulmonary oedema, ↓GCS) → synchronised
DC cardioversion**. Stable narrow-complex regular SVT → vagal + adenosine 6→12→12 mg. Stable AF →
rate control (avoid AV-nodal blockers in **pre-excited AF** — use flecainide/amiodarone/cardioversion).
**Broad-complex tachycardia = VT until proven otherwise** (do not give verapamil). Torsades → **MgSO₄
2 g IV** + correct K⁺, stop offending drugs.

**Traps & pearls:** treat regular broad-complex tachycardia as VT — misdiagnosing it as SVT with
aberrancy and giving verapamil can be fatal. Adenosine in pre-excited AF can precipitate VF.

### 2.10 THE POISONED PATIENT — toxidromes & antidotes

**Approach:** resuscitate (ABC, glucose, ECG), identify the **toxidrome**, decontaminate where
indicated, give the **antidote if one exists**, and phone the **Poisons Information Helpline
(0861 555 777, 24/7, AfriTox database)**. Assume co-ingestion; check **paracetamol level in every
deliberate self-poisoning** (silent, treatable).

**The toxidromes:**

| Toxidrome | Pupils | Skin | Other | Agents | Antidote |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cholinergic** | Pinpoint (miosis) | Wet/sweaty | SLUDGE + bradycardia, bronchorrhoea, fasciculations | **Organophosphates/carbamates**, nerve agents | **Atropine + pralidoxime** |
| **Anticholinergic** | Dilated | Dry, hot, flushed | "Mad, hot, dry, red, blind" — delirium, retention, tachycardia | TCAs, antihistamines, atropine, datura | Supportive ± physostigmine |
| **Sympathomimetic** | Dilated | Sweaty | Agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia | Cocaine, **methamphetamine (tik)**, amphetamines | Benzodiazepines |
| **Opioid** | Pinpoint | — | ↓RR, ↓GCS, ↓BP | Heroin, morphine, methadone, tramadol | **Naloxone** |
| **Sedative-hypnotic** | — | — | ↓GCS, ↓RR, normal pupils | Benzodiazepines, alcohol, barbiturates | Supportive (flumazenil rarely) |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | Dilated | Sweaty | Clonus (esp. lower limb), hyperreflexia, hyperthermia, agitation | SSRIs + tramadol/MAOI | Benzos, cyproheptadine, cooling |

**Specific antidotes & their timing:**
- **Paracetamol → N-acetylcysteine (NAC).** Plot the 4-h+ level on the **Rumack-Matthew nomogram
  (150 mg/kg "treatment line": 150 mg/L at 4 h, ~37.5 mg/L at 12 h)**; treat above the line, or if
  staggered/late/unknown-timing with a raised level or ALT. NAC most effective **<8 h** post-
  ingestion; give empirically if presentation >8 h while awaiting the level. Regimen: 3-bag IV
  (150 mg/kg over 1 h, 50 mg/kg over 4 h, 100 mg/kg over 16 h). Ingested dose ≥150 mg/kg (or ≥12 g)
  flags risk.
- **Opioids → naloxone** 0.4–2 mg IV/IM/IN, titrate to respiration (short half-life — beware
  re-narcosis, may need infusion, esp. methadone).
- **Organophosphates → atropine** (2 mg IV, **double every 5 min** until secretions dry/HR >80/
  clear chest — often huge cumulative doses) **+ pralidoxime** 1–2 g IV over 15–30 min then
  infusion. See §2.16.
- **TCA → sodium bicarbonate** 1–2 mmol/kg boluses for **QRS >100 ms**, arrhythmia, or hypotension
  (target pH 7.45–7.55). See §4.5.
- **Benzodiazepines → flumazenil** — generally AVOIDED (seizure/withdrawal risk, especially with
  co-ingested TCA).
- **Iron → desferrioxamine**; **methanol/ethylene glycol → fomepizole or ethanol + dialysis**
  (raised osmolar + anion gap); **digoxin → Digibind**; **beta-blocker/Ca-channel blocker →
  high-dose insulin euglycaemia + glucagon + calcium**; **methaemoglobinaemia → methylene blue**;
  **cyanide → hydroxocobalamin**; **warfarin → vitamin K + PCC/FFP**; **INH → pyridoxine**;
  **local anaesthetic → intralipid**; **sulphonylurea hypoglycaemia → octreotide + dextrose**.

**Decontamination:** **activated charcoal 1 g/kg within ~1 h** of a significant ingestion (airway
protected) — not for hydrocarbons/paraffin, corrosives, metals, alcohols. Gastric lavage almost
never indicated. **Whole-bowel irrigation** for iron, lithium, body-packers, sustained-release.

**Traps & pearls:** **check a paracetamol level on every deliberate overdose** regardless of story.
Pinpoint pupils = opioid OR organophosphate — the wet, bradycardic, fasciculating patient is the
latter and needs atropine. Naloxone wears off before the opioid does. A widening QRS after an
overdose = **give bicarbonate now** (TCA). Don't chase the toxin's name at the expense of ABC.

### 2.11 ANAPHYLAXIS

**Diagnosis (clinical):** acute onset (minutes–hours) with (i) skin/mucosa + airway or breathing or
circulation compromise, OR (ii) ≥2 organ systems after likely allergen, OR (iii) hypotension after
known allergen. Absence of skin signs does NOT exclude it (10–20% have none).

**Management anchors [EK]:**
- **IM adrenaline 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1:1000) into the anterolateral thigh — FIRST and immediately**;
  repeat every 5 min. Paediatric 0.01 mg/kg. **Delayed adrenaline is the leading cause of
  anaphylaxis death.**
- Remove trigger, high-flow O₂, lie flat + legs up (sit up only if breathing difficulty; upright
  posture in a hypotensive patient can cause "empty ventricle" arrest).
- **IV fluid bolus** (500 mL–1 L crystalloid) for hypotension.
- Adjuncts (AFTER adrenaline): salbutamol nebs for bronchospasm; antihistamine and steroid are
  **second-line and do not treat the acute reaction**.
- **Refractory** → IV adrenaline infusion, consider glucagon (if on beta-blocker), early airway.
- Observe 6–12 h for **biphasic reaction**; discharge with adrenaline auto-injector + allergy
  referral + avoidance advice.

**Traps & pearls:** IM (not IV bolus) adrenaline for the conscious patient; antihistamine/steroid
first is the classic fatal delay; sit-up-to-die — keep the hypotensive patient flat.

### 2.12 THE SEPTIC PATIENT

**Recognition:** infection + organ dysfunction. **qSOFA** (RR ≥22, SBP ≤100, altered mentation — ≥2
flags risk) as a bedside screen, but **do not use it to delay treatment**; lactate + the whole
clinical picture matter more. **Septic shock** = vasopressor-requiring hypotension + lactate >2
despite fluids.

**Surviving Sepsis "Hour-1 bundle" [EK, SSC 2021]:**
1. **Measure lactate** (remeasure if >2).
2. **Blood cultures before antibiotics** (don't delay antibiotics for them).
3. **Broad-spectrum antibiotics** within the hour (SA STG per source — e.g. ceftriaxone ± cover per
   focus; add cover for the SA context: **think TB, PJP, malaria, cryptococcus**).
4. **Fluid 30 mL/kg crystalloid** for hypotension/lactate ≥4 — but reassess responsiveness (passive
   leg raise/IVC/lactate clearance) rather than reflexively flooding.
5. **Vasopressors (noradrenaline first-line)** to keep **MAP ≥65** if fluid-refractory.

**SA-specific:** always ask **where is the source** (chest/PJP/TB, urine, skin/soft-tissue, abdomen,
meningitis, malaria); HIV status changes the differential and antibiotic choice; **source control**
(drain the abscess/empyema, remove the line) is as important as antibiotics.

**Traps & pearls:** normal temperature does not exclude sepsis (elderly, immunosuppressed can be
hypothermic). Lactate clearance is the resuscitation target. Over-resuscitation with fluid harms —
cumulative positive balance = organ oedema. Don't miss **malaria** in the returning traveller and
**TB/PJP** in HIV.

### 2.13 ENDOCRINE EMERGENCIES — DKA, HHS, thyroid storm, adrenal crisis

**DKA:** glucose usually >11, **ketonaemia ≥3 (or ketonuria ≥2+)**, **pH <7.3 or HCO₃ <15**. (Beware
**euglycaemic DKA** — SGLT2 inhibitors, pregnancy, starvation.) Management: **fluids first** (0.9%
saline, ~1 L stat then titrate), **fixed-rate insulin infusion 0.1 U/kg/h** (start AFTER fluids),
**replace K⁺ aggressively** (start K⁺ once <5.5 and passing urine — the "normal" or high K⁺ falls
fast once insulin starts and can cause fatal hypokalaemia), hourly glucose/ketones, add dextrose
when glucose <14 while continuing insulin until ketones clear. Find and treat the precipitant
(infection, MI, missed insulin — the "I"s). Traps: **potassium** (the killer), **cerebral oedema**
in children (cautious fluids), don't stop insulin when glucose normalises (ketosis persists),
euglycaemic DKA missed.

**HHS:** older type-2, glucose often >30, **osmolality >320**, minimal ketosis, profound dehydration
(often 8–10 L deficit) and altered mentation. Rehydrate **slowly** (avoid rapid osmotic shifts/
cerebral oedema and central pontine myelinolysis), lower glucose gradually, low-dose insulin only
after fluids; **high VTE risk → prophylactic anticoagulation**.

**Thyroid storm:** fever, tachyarrhythmia (AF), agitation/delirium, heart failure, GI upset;
precipitated by infection/surgery/iodine/withdrawal. **Burch-Wartofsky score** supports diagnosis.
Treat: **beta-blocker (propranolol), propylthiouracil/carbimazole, iodine (≥1 h after thionamide),
hydrocortisone, cooling** + treat precipitant.

**Adrenal (Addisonian) crisis:** hypotension resistant to fluids/pressors, **hyponatraemia +
hyperkalaemia + hypoglycaemia**, weakness, abdominal pain; SA precipitants include **TB adrenal
destruction, HIV, abrupt steroid withdrawal, sepsis (Waterhouse-Friderichsen)**. Treat: **hydrocortisone
100 mg IV immediately** (take a cortisol first if it won't delay), aggressive saline + dextrose,
treat precipitant. Do not wait for confirmation — treat empirically if suspected.

**Myxoedema coma:** hypothermia, bradycardia, hyponatraemia, ↓GCS — thyroxine + hydrocortisone +
supportive.

### 2.14 HYPERKALAEMIC (peri-)ARREST

**Suspect** in AKI/CKD, missed dialysis, crush/rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis, Addisonian crisis, and
the drug cocktail (**ACEi/ARB + spironolactone + trimethoprim/NSAID**). **The ECG evolves with
level:** peaked T waves → flattened P/PR prolongation → widened QRS → sine wave → VF/asystole. **A
peaked-T or wide-QRS in a dialysis patient is a resuscitation emergency — treat before the level
returns.**

**Management [EK]:** (1) **Calcium gluconate 10 mL 10% (or calcium chloride)** IV to stabilise the
myocardium — repeat if ECG unchanged (works in minutes, doesn't lower K⁺); (2) **shift**: insulin
10 U + 25 g dextrose (50 mL 50%) + salbutamol nebs 10–20 mg + bicarbonate if acidotic; (3)
**remove**: dialysis (definitive in anuric/refractory), ± potassium binders/frusemide if making
urine. In **hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest**, give calcium during CPR and arrange emergency dialysis.

**Traps & pearls:** a "normal" K⁺ on a haemolysed sample vs the real thing — but treat the ECG.
Don't forget to **stop the culprit drugs** and recheck. Calcium buys time, it doesn't lower K⁺ —
you must also shift and remove.

### TRAUMA

### 2.15a Primary survey (ATLS) and the reversible causes of traumatic arrest

**C-ABCDE** [EK ATLS 10th ed]: **C**atastrophic haemorrhage control first (direct pressure,
tourniquet, pelvic binder, haemostatic dressing) → **A**irway with C-spine control → **B**reathing
→ **C**irculation with haemorrhage control → **D**isability (GCS, pupils, glucose) → **E**xposure/
environment (log-roll, prevent hypothermia).

**Immediately life-threatening BREATHING injuries — the lethal six (secondary survey adds more):**
airway obstruction, **tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax (sucking chest wound), massive
haemothorax, flail chest, cardiac tamponade.** ("ATOM-FC": Airway, Tension pneumo, Open pneumo,
Massive haemothorax, Flail, Cardiac tamponade.)

**Reversible causes in traumatic cardiac arrest** (different from medical arrest — treat these,
prolonged CPR is often futile): **hypovolaemia (haemorrhage), tension pneumothorax, cardiac
tamponade, hypoxia** → so **bilateral finger thoracostomies, blood/haemorrhage control, and
consider resuscitative thoracotomy** for penetrating chest arrest with recent signs of life.

**The lethal trauma triad (the "diamond of death"):** **hypothermia + acidosis + coagulopathy**
(each worsens the others; hypocalcaemia now added). Prevent by warming, giving **blood not
crystalloid**, permissive hypotension, and early **tranexamic acid (1 g within 3 h)**.

**FAST/eFAST:** free fluid in Morrison's pouch, spleno-renal, pelvis, pericardium; extended for
pneumothorax (lung sliding) + haemothorax. A positive FAST + hypotension = **theatre, not CT**. A
negative FAST does NOT exclude significant injury (retroperitoneal, hollow viscus).

**Massive transfusion:** activate protocol for ongoing haemorrhage (e.g. **ABC score ≥2**, shock
index >1, obvious pattern). Give **balanced product 1:1:1 (RBC:FFP:platelets)**, **TXA 1 g bolus +
1 g/8 h**, correct **calcium** (citrate chelation → give calcium), keep warm, target **permissive
hypotension** (palpable radial/SBP ~90) until surgical control, then normalise. Monitor with
serial lactate/base deficit and Hb trend.

### 2.15b Head injury — the lucid interval & CT decision rules

**Extradural (epidural) haematoma:** classic **lucid interval** (brief LOC → recovery → deterioration)
from a middle meningeal artery tear; **lens/biconvex on CT**; a surgical emergency. **Subdural:**
crescentic, bridging veins, elderly/alcoholic, may be chronic. **Diffuse axonal injury:** low GCS
with little on CT.

**Signs of raised ICP / herniation:** falling GCS, **unequal/fixed dilated pupil (uncal herniation,
CN III)**, **Cushing's response (hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respiration)**, posturing.
→ head-up 30°, keep **PaO₂ >8 kPa / SpO₂ >90%, normocapnia (PaCO₂ 4.5–5.0)**, MAP to maintain
**CPP** (avoid hypotension — a single episode of SBP <90 worsens outcome), treat seizures, **mannitol
0.5–1 g/kg or hypertonic saline** for herniation, urgent neurosurgery.

**Canadian CT Head Rule** (GCS 13–15, LOC/amnesia/disorientation; high-risk = GCS <15 at 2 h,
suspected open/depressed skull #, any basal-skull-# sign, ≥2 vomits, age ≥65; medium-risk = amnesia
>30 min, dangerous mechanism). Sensitive for injuries needing intervention. Anticoagulated/HIV/low-
platelet patients lower the threshold. In the district ED **without CT**, GCS trajectory + these
rules drive the transfer decision.

**Traps:** the lucid interval that lulls — serial GCS; anticoagulated elderly with a "trivial" bump;
hypoxia/hypotension are the secondary insults that kill; **glucose and alcohol don't explain a
falling GCS in a head-injured patient** until the head is cleared.

### 2.15c C-spine clearance

**NEXUS (all five to clinically clear, no imaging):** no midline cervical tenderness, no focal
neuro deficit, normal alertness, no intoxication, no distracting injury. **Canadian C-Spine Rule**
(alert, stable, GCS 15): any high-risk factor (age ≥65, dangerous mechanism, paraesthesiae) →
image; if no high-risk and a low-risk factor allowing safe assessment (simple rear-end, sitting,
ambulatory, delayed pain, no midline tenderness) → test **active rotation 45° each way** — if able,
clear. Immobilise (collar/blocks/log-roll) until cleared; beware the distracting injury and the
intoxicated/head-injured patient who cannot be clinically cleared.

### 2.15d Chest, pelvic/long-bone, burns

**Chest trauma:** tension pneumo (clinical → decompress), open pneumo (three-sided occlusive
dressing → drain), massive haemothorax (>1500 mL initial or >200 mL/h → theatre), flail chest (treat
the underlying pulmonary contusion + analgesia ± ventilation), tamponade (pericardiocentesis/theatre),
tracheobronchial and aortic injury (widened mediastinum). **Chest drain:** 5th ICS anterior/mid-
axillary line, "safe triangle."

**Pelvic fracture:** high-energy, can exsanguinate into the retroperitoneum — **binder at the
greater trochanters**, do NOT "spring" the pelvis repeatedly, blood, and angioembolisation/external
fixation. **Long-bone/femur:** a closed femoral shaft # can lose 1–1.5 L — traction splint, analgesia,
neurovascular checks; **fat embolism** at 24–72 h.

**Burns — Parkland:** **4 mL × %TBSA × weight (kg)** of Ringer's lactate in 24 h, **half in the
first 8 h from the time of injury**, titrated to **urine output 0.5–1 mL/kg/h** (1–1.5 in children/
electrical). **%TBSA by Rule of Nines** (adult: head 9, each arm 9, each leg 18, front torso 18,
back 18, perineum 1; palm ≈1%). **Airway burn red flags:** facial/neck burns, singed nasal hair,
soot in mouth/sputum, hoarseness, stridor, enclosed-space fire → **early intubation before oedema
closes the airway**. Consider **carbon monoxide (give 100% O₂; SpO₂ reads falsely normal) and
cyanide** (smoke inhalation → hydroxocobalamin). Circumferential burns → escharotomy. Refer major
burns per criteria.

### 2.15e Paediatric trauma differences

Children have **greater physiological reserve then crash suddenly** (maintain BP until ~40% volume
loss). **Hypotension is a late, pre-terminal sign** — use age-adjusted HR/RR/cap-refill and the
paediatric TEWS. **Higher body-surface-area** → hypothermia and greater fluid losses in burns.
**Larger head** → head injury and airway obstruction risk; **more compliant chest** → significant
lung contusion without rib fracture; **horizontal ribs/mobile mediastinum**. **SCIWORA** (spinal
cord injury without radiographic abnormality). Fluid bolus **10–20 mL/kg**, then blood **10 mL/kg**.
Always weigh (or estimate — Broselow/APLS **weight = (age+4)×2**) for drug/fluid dosing. **Consider
non-accidental injury** (pattern injuries, inconsistent history, delayed presentation).

### SA-SPECIFIC EMERGENCIES

### 2.16 Interpersonal violence / penetrating trauma

SA carries one of the world's highest interpersonal-violence and penetrating-trauma burdens
(stabbings, GSW, assault, GBV). **Stab wounds:** the visible wound underestimates the tract; a
thoraco-abdominal stab crosses the diaphragm. Serial exam + FAST; selective non-operative management
for stable abdominal stabs, laparotomy for peritonitis/evisceration/haemodynamic instability.
**GSW:** entry/exit don't reveal the tract; assume trajectory crosses cavities; document (don't
label entry/exit — that's a forensic conclusion). **Neck zones** (I–III) guide management of
penetrating neck injury; hard signs (expanding haematoma, bruit, airway compromise, massive
bleeding) → theatre. **Tetanus prophylaxis + antibiotics + analgesia.** **J88** and forensic chain
of evidence for every assault/GSW/stab/GBV (see §6).

### 2.17 Snakebite — the antivenom decision

**Three envenomation syndromes (southern Africa) and the culprits** [SASS/Hardcastle 2023; Müller CME]:

| Syndrome | Culprit snakes | Clinical picture |
|---|---|---|
| **Cytotoxic** (commonest ~½) | **Puff adder, Mozambique spitting cobra (M'fezi), stiletto/burrowing asp, rinkhals (mixed)** | Progressive painful swelling, blistering, bruising, tissue necrosis, compartment syndrome, shock; ± AKI |
| **Neurotoxic** | **Black & green mambas, Cape cobra, snouted cobra, non-spitting cobras**; berg adder (mild, not covered) | Descending flaccid paralysis: ptosis → diplopia → bulbar → **respiratory failure**; mambas fast (30 min–a few h) |
| **Haemotoxic** | **Boomslang (*Dispholidus*), vine/twig snake (*Thelotornis*)** | Delayed (hours) coagulopathy: bleeding gums/venepuncture, haematuria, DIC; **NOT covered by polyvalent** |

**Bedside test:** **20-minute whole-blood clotting test (WBCT20)** — 2 mL fresh blood in a clean
dry glass tube; **no clot at 20 min = coagulopathy** (haemotoxic/consumptive). Also monitor
compartment (pain, CK), neuro (single-breath count, ptosis, vital capacity), and renal function.

**Polyvalent SAIMR/SAVP antivenom** covers 10 species (both mambas, most cobras, rinkhals, puff
adder, gaboon adder) — but **NOT boomslang, vine snake, or berg adder**. **Indications** (envenoming,
not the bite alone): **neurotoxicity** (any progressive paralysis), **significant/progressive
cytotoxicity** (rapidly advancing swelling past a joint, threatening airway from spitting-cobra
neck bites), and systemic effects/shock. **Dose (children and adults the same — venom load is fixed):**
- Neurotoxic (mamba/cobra): **≥8–12 vials** initial, repeat as needed.
- Severe cytotoxic (puff adder): **~5–6 vials**, repeat.
- Give **diluted IV, slowly**, with **adrenaline drawn up and monitoring ready** — anaphylaxis to
  antivenom is common (up to ~40–50% in some SA series, higher in children). Premedicate/manage
  reactions with **adrenaline** (routine subcut premed debated; adrenaline immediately available is
  mandatory). Stop the infusion and treat anaphylaxis, then cautiously resume.

**Boomslang bite → monovalent boomslang antivenom** (only for confirmed boomslang with coagulopathy);
supportive with FFP is inadequate alone if the antivenom is available.

**Adjuncts:** **neurotoxic** — early **intubation + ventilation** for respiratory failure is life-
saving even without/awaiting antivenom; a trial of **atropine + neostigmine** may help postsynaptic
(cobra) but not presynaptic (mamba) paralysis. **Cytotoxic** — elevate limb, analgesia, monitor for
compartment syndrome (**measure pressure; fasciotomy only for true compartment syndrome, not
swelling alone** — over-eager fasciotomy causes morbidity), tetanus, watch for AKI. **Do NOT** apply
tourniquets, cut/suck, or use electric shock; pressure immobilisation is for neurotoxic elapid bites
only. Antivenom does not reverse established tissue necrosis or fixed paralysis.

**Traps:** the bite that looks minor early but is a mamba (neurotoxicity is delayed then rapid —
admit and observe); giving antivenom for a dry bite (no envenoming = no antivenom); using polyvalent
for a boomslang (won't work); missing compartment syndrome vs treating swelling with the knife.

### 2.18 Organophosphate poisoning

Common in SA (agricultural pesticides, deliberate ingestion, "street pesticide"/aldicarb). **Cholinergic
crisis** = the muscarinic **SLUDGE/DUMBELS** (Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI
cramps, Emesis / Diarrhoea, Urination, Miosis, Bradycardia/Bronchorrhoea/Bronchospasm, Emesis,
Lacrimation, Salivation) + nicotinic (fasciculations, weakness, tachycardia) + CNS (agitation,
seizures, coma). **Bronchorrhoea and bronchospasm kill.**

**Management [WFSA/EK]:** protect yourself (PPE, remove contaminated clothes, wash skin). **Atropine**
2 mg IV, **double the dose every ~5 min** until **atropinised** (chest clear of secretions, HR >80,
SBP >80, dry axillae, pupils no longer pinpoint) — total doses can be very large; then an infusion.
**Pralidoxime (2-PAM)** 1–2 g IV over 15–30 min then infusion **10–20 mg/kg/h** — reactivates
acetylcholinesterase (nicotinic/muscle weakness), give early (ineptness once "ageing" occurs).
**Benzodiazepines** for seizures/agitation. **Secure the airway** (secretions + weakness); avoid
suxamethonium (prolonged block). Watch for the **intermediate syndrome (24–96 h: proximal/neck-
flexor/respiratory weakness — the patient who can't lift the head off the pillow)** needing
ventilation, and delayed neuropathy.

**Traps:** under-atropinising (titrate to the chest, not the pupils/HR alone); pinpoint pupils
misread as opioid; forgetting staff/decontamination exposure; discharging too early (biphasic +
intermediate syndrome).

### 2.19 Paraffin (kerosene) ingestion

The commonest childhood poisoning in SA (stored in cooldrink bottles). The danger is **aspiration →
chemical (hydrocarbon) pneumonitis**, NOT systemic absorption. Cough, choking, tachypnoea, fever,
hypoxia; CXR changes (often lower zones) may lag 6+ h.

**Management:** **do NOT induce emesis or give activated charcoal or lavage** (aspiration risk
outweighs benefit). Supportive: oxygen, observe for respiratory distress, CXR if symptomatic,
antibiotics **only if secondary bacterial infection** (not prophylactic), bronchodilators for
wheeze; **admit symptomatic/hypoxic children, observe asymptomatic 4–6 h and discharge if well**.
**Prevention counselling** (safe storage, original containers) and notify per poisoning surveillance.
Phone the **Poisons Information Helpline (0861 555 777)**.

### 2.20 Hanging / strangulation

Near-hanging is common in SA self-harm. Two mechanisms: **venous/arterial occlusion + airway** (most
non-judicial hangings — hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury) rather than cervical fracture (judicial
"long drop"). **Assess:** GCS, airway (laryngeal injury, oedema — **early intubation** if any airway
concern, as swelling progresses), C-spine (though fracture is uncommon in short-drop), neuro,
ligature marks, petechiae, subconjunctival haemorrhage. **Delayed airway obstruction and ARDS/
pulmonary oedema** develop over hours — admit and observe even the initially-well patient. Manage as
brain-injury (normoxia, normocapnia, avoid hypotension) if arrested/comatose. **Non-fatal
strangulation in GBV is a strong marker of future homicide** — screen, document (J88), and safeguard.

### 2.21 Obstetric emergencies presenting to the ED

Any woman of reproductive age with collapse/abdominal pain/PV bleeding is pregnant until βhCG says
otherwise.
- **Ruptured ectopic pregnancy:** amenorrhoea + abdominal pain + shock ± PV bleeding; positive βhCG +
  empty uterus/free fluid on POCUS → **theatre, resuscitate with blood**. A leading cause of first-
  trimester maternal death; the "gastro/UTI" that is an ectopic is a classic fatal miss.
- **Eclampsia:** seizures in pregnancy/post-partum with hypertension ± proteinuria → **magnesium
  sulphate** (loading 4 g IV over 20 min + 1 g/h, or Pritchard IM), control **BP (labetalol/
  hydralazine/nifedipine, target 140–150/90–100)**, left-lateral, protect airway, **deliver** (the
  cure); watch for HELLP. Magnesium toxicity (areflexia, respiratory depression) → **calcium
  gluconate**.
- **Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH):** >500 mL vaginal / >1000 mL CS, or any causing instability. **Four
  T's**: **Tone** (atony — commonest: uterine massage, **oxytocin, ergometrine, misoprostol,
  tranexamic acid, carboprost**, bimanual compression, balloon tamponade), **Trauma** (repair
  tears), **Tissue** (retained products → evacuate), **Thrombin** (coagulopathy → products). Blood
  + MTP; escalate to theatre (B-Lynch, hysterectomy).
- **Cord prolapse:** obstetric emergency — **relieve cord compression** (presenting part elevated by
  hand, knee-chest/Trendelenburg, fill bladder), **do not handle the cord**, and **immediate
  delivery** (usually CS). Fetal bradycardia after membrane rupture → check for cord.
- Also: **antepartum haemorrhage** (placenta praevia — painless; abruption — painful, tender rigid
  uterus, "silent" concealed bleeding with shock out of proportion), **uterine rupture**, **amniotic
  fluid embolism**, **ruptured ovarian cyst/torsion**.

## 3. The normals (what "normal" looks like in the EC)

**Adult vital signs:** RR 12–20/min; HR 60–100; SBP 100–140 (MAP ≥65 for perfusion); SpO₂ ≥95% (aim
88–92% in COPD/CO₂ retainers); temperature 36.5–37.5 °C; capillary refill <2 s; urine output ≥0.5
mL/kg/h; GCS 15.

**Paediatric age-banded norms (approx.):**

| Age | HR (bpm) | RR (/min) | SBP (mmHg, lower limit ≈70 + 2×age) |
|---|---|---|---|
| <1 y | 110–160 | 30–40 | >70–80 |
| 1–2 y | 100–150 | 25–35 | >70–85 |
| 2–5 y | 95–140 | 25–30 | >75–90 |
| 5–12 y | 80–120 | 20–25 | >85–100 |
| >12 y | 60–100 | 12–20 | >90–110 |

**Bloods/gas anchors:** glucose 4–7 mmol/L (hypoglycaemia <3.9; treat <3.0 or symptomatic); Na⁺
135–145; K⁺ 3.5–5.0 (ECG changes/arrest risk >6.5); pH 7.35–7.45; PaCO₂ 4.6–6.0 kPa; HCO₃ 22–26;
lactate <2 (>4 severe); base excess −2 to +2; anion gap 8–12; Hb ♂ 13–17 / ♀ 12–15 g/dL; troponin
below the assay's 99th percentile (know your lab's number); INR ~1.0; corrected QT <440 ms (♂)/
<460 ms (♀). **WBCT20:** a firm clot at 20 min = normal coagulation.

## 4. Cross-cutting investigation interpretation (deep)

### 4.1 The 12-lead in ACS — beyond obvious ST elevation
- **Posterior MI:** tall R + **ST depression V1–V3** (mirror image), upright T; confirm with
  **posterior leads V7–V9** (ST elevation ≥0.5 mm). Easily read as "anterior ischaemia" — it's a
  posterior STEMI needing reperfusion.
- **Right ventricular infarct:** with inferior STEMI, get **V4R** (ST elevation ≥1 mm). Preload-
  dependent → **avoid nitrates/opioids, give fluid**.
- **de Winter T waves:** upsloping ST depression + tall symmetric T at the J-point in precordials =
  proximal LAD occlusion equivalent — treat as STEMI.
- **Wellens' syndrome:** deep symmetric or biphasic T-wave inversion in V2–V3 in a pain-free patient
  = critical proximal LAD stenosis; a warning of imminent large anterior MI — don't stress-test,
  refer.
- **aVR ST elevation** with widespread ST depression = left main / triple-vessel / severe global
  ischaemia.
- **New LBBB** with ischaemic symptoms → treat as STEMI (Sgarbossa criteria refine concordant ST
  changes).
- **Hyperacute T waves** precede ST elevation. **Serial/repeat ECGs** in ongoing pain — a single
  ECG can miss an evolving occlusion.

### 4.2 The VBG in the crashing patient
A venous gas answers the resuscitation questions fast: **pH, PCO₂ (ventilation), HCO₃/base excess
(metabolic), lactate (perfusion), Na⁺/K⁺/glucose/Hb**. VBG **agrees closely with ABG for pH and
HCO₃**; it is unreliable for PaO₂ (use SpO₂ or ABG for oxygenation). Reading it: a **metabolic
acidosis with a high lactate** = shock/sepsis/ischaemia; **high anion gap** ("MUDPILES" — methanol,
uraemia, DKA, propylene glycol, INH/iron, lactate, ethylene glycol, salicylate); **normal anion gap
acidosis** = diarrhoea/RTA; a **rising CO₂ in a tiring asthmatic/COPD** = impending respiratory
arrest; **osmolar gap** flags toxic alcohols.

### 4.3 Serial troponin delta
High-sensitivity troponin uses the **change (delta) over 1 h or 3 h**, not a single value, to rule
in/out MI. A very low presentation value (below the assay limit) with a low-risk story can rule out;
a **significant rise/fall** rules in type-1 MI. A **stable, non-dynamic mild elevation** = chronic
(renal failure, HF, LVH) or type-2 (demand — sepsis, tachyarrhythmia, PE), not necessarily an acute
coronary occlusion. Interpret with the ECG and the pre-test probability (HEART score).

### 4.4 Shock index and occult shock
**Shock index = HR ÷ SBP** (normal 0.5–0.7). **>0.9–1.0** flags significant blood loss/haemodynamic
compromise **before the BP falls** — sensitive for occult haemorrhage, ruptured ectopic, and PE.
**Age-adjusted (age × SI)** and the **modified SI** refine it. Rising SI on serial obs = decompensation.

### 4.5 The wide QRS after overdose (sodium-channel blockade)
**QRS >100 ms** post-ingestion (TCA, and other Na-channel blockers) predicts seizures (>100 ms) and
ventricular arrhythmia (>160 ms); a **terminal R wave in aVR >3 mm** and a large S in I/aVL support
TCA. **Sodium bicarbonate 1–2 mmol/kg boluses** (narrows QRS, targets pH 7.45–7.55) is the antidote
principle; hypotension → fluids + noradrenaline; seizures → benzodiazepines; **avoid class Ia/Ic/III
antiarrhythmics and physostigmine**.

### 4.6 The toxicology screen — its limits
Urine drug screens are **qualitative, have false positives/negatives, detect the class not the
clinical effect, and don't change acute management** — treat the toxidrome and the patient, not the
screen. **Quantitative levels that DO matter:** **paracetamol, salicylate, iron, lithium, digoxin,
methanol/ethylene glycol (with osmolar gap), carboxyhaemoglobin, theophylline, valproate.** Always
send a **paracetamol level in deliberate self-poisoning** even if denied. Phone the Poisons
Information Helpline for interpretation.

## 5. Scores, charts & structured tools

**South African Triage Scale (SATS)** — national mandated tool. Colour → target time to be seen:
**Red = emergency (immediate)**, **Orange = very urgent (<10 min)**, **Yellow = urgent (<60 min)**,
**Green = routine (<240 min)**, **Blue = dead/no vital signs**. Priority = **the higher of the TEWS
band OR the clinical discriminator** (a single "red" discriminator overrides a low TEWS).

**Triage Early Warning Score (TEWS)** — sum of points for **mobility, respiratory rate, heart rate,
systolic BP, temperature, AVPU, and trauma (yes/no)** (0–3 each). **Total → colour: 0–2 green, 3–4
yellow, 5–6 orange, ≥7 red.** Adult band pattern (points rise as the variable deviates from normal):
mobility (walking 0 → with help → stretcher/immobile); RR (normal ~15–20 = 0, then rising points for
<9, 21–29, >29); HR (51–100 = 0, points for extremes <41 or >110/>129); SBP (101–199 = 0, points for
<100 and very low <71 = 3, and >199); temperature (35–38.4 = 0, points at extremes); AVPU (A 0, V 1,
P 2, U 3). *(Component point-values per the SATS manual; totals-to-colour confirmed.)*

**SATS clinical discriminators (override the TEWS):** examples that force **RED** — airway threat/
stridor, respiratory distress/SpO₂ very low, shock, active seizure, GCS/AVPU = P or U, threatened
limb, unstable trauma, chemical eye injury. **ORANGE** — chest pain, acute focal neurology, severe
pain, purpura/haemorrhage, GI bleed, poisoning, pregnancy with abdominal pain/bleeding, hypo/
hyperglycaemia. **Paediatric SATS**: two TEWS charts by height (**50–95 cm ≈ 1 wk–3 y**, and
**96–150 cm ≈ 3–12 y**) with age-appropriate HR/RR bands + the ABC-c-c-DO (Airway, Breathing,
Circulation, Coma, Convulsion, Dehydration, Other) discriminator approach.

**Glasgow Coma Scale (E/V/M, /15):** **Eye** (4 spontaneous, 3 to voice, 2 to pain, 1 none);
**Verbal** (5 oriented, 4 confused, 3 inappropriate words, 2 sounds, 1 none); **Motor** (6 obeys, 5
localises, 4 withdraws, 3 abnormal flexion, 2 extension, 1 none). **≤8 = coma/secure airway.** Report
the components (M is the most prognostic). **AVPU** ≈ A→GCS15, V→~13, P→~8, U→3.

**Wells score (PE):** clinical signs of DVT 3, PE most likely diagnosis 3, HR >100 1.5, immobilisation/
surgery <4 wk 1.5, previous DVT/PE 1.5, haemoptysis 1, malignancy 1. **≤4 "PE unlikely" → D-dimer;
>4 "likely" → CTPA.** (Two-tier.)

**PERC (rule-out, only if low pre-test probability):** all 8 must be negative to exclude PE without
D-dimer — age <50, HR <100, SpO₂ ≥95%, no haemoptysis, no oestrogen, no prior VTE, no recent surgery/
trauma, no unilateral leg swelling.

**Ottawa Ankle rules:** X-ray if pain in the malleolar zone AND (bony tenderness at posterior edge/
tip of lateral or medial malleolus, OR inability to weight-bear 4 steps both immediately and in ED).
**Ottawa Knee:** X-ray if age ≥55, isolated patellar tenderness, tenderness at fibular head, cannot
flex 90°, or cannot weight-bear 4 steps.

**Canadian C-Spine Rule / NEXUS:** see §2.15c (NEXUS 5 low-risk criteria; CCR high-risk/low-risk/
rotation algorithm).

**Canadian CT Head Rule:** high-risk (mandate CT) — GCS <15 at 2 h, suspected open/depressed skull #,
sign of basal skull #, ≥2 vomiting episodes, age ≥65; medium-risk — amnesia >30 min before impact,
dangerous mechanism. (Applies to minor head injury GCS 13–15 with LOC/amnesia/disorientation.)

**Parkland (burns):** 4 mL × %TBSA × kg RL over 24 h (½ in first 8 h), titrate to urine output.
**Rule of Nines** for %TBSA (palm ≈1%).

**Centor / McIsaac (strep pharyngitis):** fever >38, tender anterior cervical nodes, tonsillar
exudate, absence of cough — 1 each; McIsaac adds +1 age 3–14, 0 for 15–44, −1 for ≥45. ≥3 → consider
antibiotics/test.

**CURB-65 (pneumonia severity):** Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, RR ≥30, BP <90/≤60, age ≥65 — 1 each.
0–1 outpatient, 2 admit/short stay, ≥3 severe/consider ICU.

**HEART (chest pain):** History, ECG, Age, Risk factors, Troponin (0–2 each). **0–3 low** (~1–2%
MACE — consider discharge with serial trop), **4–6 moderate**, **≥7 high**. (TIMI/GRACE also used for
risk stratification in confirmed ACS.)

**Other structural tools:** **NIHSS** (stroke severity), **CHA₂DS₂-VASc** (AF stroke risk), **ABCD²**
(TIA), **San Francisco/Canadian Syncope** rules, **Burch-Wartofsky** (thyroid storm), **Alvarado**
(appendicitis), **Rockall/Glasgow-Blatchford** (GI bleed), **ABC/shock index** (massive transfusion),
**Rumack-Matthew** nomogram (paracetamol), **Wells DVT**.

## 6. SA-specific reality

**SATS as the national instrument.** SATS (TEWS + discriminators + colour) is the mandated,
validated triage backbone of every state EC, in adult and paediatric versions. It is designed for a
task-shifted, nurse-led, high-volume, under-resourced front door. The **re-triage of the waiting
patient** is a governance and safety issue: overcrowded ECs mean a "yellow" can deteriorate unseen —
serial TEWS and queue re-triage are the safety net.

**The trauma & interpersonal-violence burden.** SA has an exceptional load of penetrating trauma
(stab, GSW), assault, road traffic, burns, and GBV. Trauma resuscitation, damage-control principles,
permissive hypotension, TXA, and the transfer pathway to a trauma centre are daily bread. Alcohol is
a massive contributor (weekend "trauma tsunami").

**J88 and forensic obligations.** The **J88** is the statutory medico-legal form documenting injuries
in assault/GSW/stab/GBV/sexual-assault cases. Document **objectively** (site, size, shape, type of
each wound; do NOT interpret entry/exit or assign a weapon), preserve **chain of evidence**
(clothing, projectiles handled with care and documented), and complete it contemporaneously —
clinicians are frequently subpoenaed. **Sexual-assault care:** HIV **PEP** (start <72 h), **emergency
contraception**, STI prophylaxis, hepatitis B, forensic evidence kit + J88, counselling, and referral
to a **Thuthuzela Care Centre** where available.

**Notifiable medical conditions** (report to the NMC system): among ED-relevant ones — **rabies (and
suspected rabies exposure), cholera, meningococcal disease, measles, acute flaccid paralysis,
viral haemorrhagic fever, food-borne/poisoning outbreaks, plague, diphtheria, tetanus (neonatal),
and certain others.** Snakebite and acute poisonings feed surveillance via the **Poisons Information
Helpline (0861 555 777) / AfriTox**. **Rabies PEP** (wound care + vaccine ± immunoglobulin) for dog/
bat/mongoose bites in endemic areas is a routine ED decision.

**HIV/TB integration.** Every ED differential carries an HIV/TB overlay: **TB or cryptococcal
meningitis** (subacute headache/altered mental status), **PJP** (breathless HIV patient with clear-
ish lungs), **TB pericardial effusion → tamponade**, **disseminated TB sepsis**, **immune
reconstitution**, higher VTE and stroke risk, and adrenal TB → Addisonian crisis. Know the patient's
status, CD4, and ART; start co-trimoxazole/steroid empirically for suspected PJP; treat meningitis
broadly (bacterial + TB + cryptococcal cover) while awaiting the LP.

**District-ED resource reality.** Much of SA state emergency care happens at **district hospitals
without CT, cath lab, on-site specialists, or reliable troponin/blood-bank turnaround, and with
antivenom/antidote stock-outs.** This changes decisions: **thrombolysis (streptokinase/tenecteplase)
not primary PCI** for STEMI; **clinical decision rules (NEXUS/CCR/Canadian CT-Head) to select the
few CT transfers**; **clinical toxidrome-led management** with the Poisons Helpline rather than
levels; **early, safe inter-facility transfer** as a core skill; and explicit, honest
**ceiling-of-care / resuscitation-status** conversations in a scarce-resource setting. **Blood
products are precious** — permissive hypotension and MTP stewardship matter. Analgesia and
safety-netting on discharge are quality markers that are easy to drop under volume pressure.
