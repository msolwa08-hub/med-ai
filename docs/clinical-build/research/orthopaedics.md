## 1. The consultant's mental model

**The order of questions is fixed, and it is never "what fracture is this".** An orthopaedic
registrar or consultant approaching any trauma patient runs a strict hierarchy: **(1) is this
patient dying — primary survey first, fractures don't kill in the first ninety seconds, catastrophic
hidden blood loss does** (pelvis, femur, multiple long bones — a closed femoral shaft fracture can
hide 1–1.5 L, a pelvis can hide the patient's entire circulating volume); **(2) is this limb dying —
the six limb-threatening emergencies that override the clock and the ward round**: open fracture,
compartment syndrome, a fracture/dislocation with vascular compromise, a dislocation needing urgent
reduction (hip, knee — the popliteal artery), septic arthritis (the joint dies in hours, not days),
and cauda equina syndrome (the only "orthopaedic" diagnosis that is also a spinal cord emergency).
**(3) then, and only then, the fracture itself** — reduce, hold, rehabilitate, in that order, and
decide operative vs conservative.

**The two things a trauma/ortho clinician is always secretly ruling out** [EK]:
- **A second injury they haven't looked for yet.** The patient with an obvious calcaneal fracture
  (axial load — fell/jumped from height) has a spinal fracture until the spine is examined and
  imaged; the patient with a femur fracture has an ipsilateral hip fracture/dislocation until the
  hip is specifically checked (a missed floating-hip injury is a career-defining miss). Expose the
  whole patient, log-roll, examine every joint, not just the one that hurts.
- **Compartment syndrome developing under their own cast/splint/dressing.** Pain out of proportion,
  increasing analgesia requirement, and a tense limb after *any* intervention (reduction, casting,
  IM nailing, tight bandaging) is compartment syndrome until serial exam/pressures say otherwise —
  this is not a diagnosis made once at triage, it is a diagnosis actively hunted for on every review.

**Neurovascular status distal to EVERY injury, documented before AND after any manipulation, is
not optional — it is the single most litigated omission in orthopaedic practice** [EK]. Reduce a
dislocated/displaced fracture without a pre-reduction neurovascular exam and you cannot say whether
you caused the deficit or inherited it. This applies to every closed reduction, every cast
application, every splint, every theatre positioning change.

**Reduce → hold → rehabilitate — the universal fracture algorithm** [EK]. *Reduce* (restore
length, alignment, rotation — closed manipulation or open reduction). *Hold* (cast, splint,
traction, or fixation — K-wires, plate, intramedullary nail, external fixator — chosen by
soft-tissue state, fracture pattern, patient physiology, and *implant/theatre access*, which in SA
state hospitals is a real and constant constraint, not a footnote). *Rehabilitate* (weight-bearing
status, physiotherapy, return of function — the part juniors forget to plan for on day one but
which determines the patient's actual outcome).

**Operative vs conservative is a physiology-and-pattern decision, not a reflex.** Displaced,
unstable, intra-articular, open, neurovascularly compromised, or in a patient who needs early
mobilisation to survive (the elderly hip fracture — see §2.5) → operate. Undisplaced, stable,
extra-articular, in a patient unfit for anaesthesia, or where the soft-tissue envelope is not ready
(degloving, blisters, compartment swelling still settling) → conservative first, or "damage
control" external fixation as a bridge to definitive fixation once the patient/limb is fit.

**The SA trauma load is not the exception, it is the Tuesday-night baseline** [EK/grounded]. A
South African trauma unit sees interpersonal violence (stab, gunshot, assault) and road-traffic
collisions as the *default* case mix, not the occasional outlier — SA's interpersonal-violence
mortality runs at roughly seven times the global age-standardised rate, and road-traffic injury at
roughly double the global average (see §6). This changes the mental model in a specific way: every
intern must be fluent in **penetrating limb trauma with a hard/soft sign vascular exam**, in
**multiple simultaneous fractures**, in **triaging who goes to theatre first when three trolleys
need it at once**, and in **damage-control orthopaedics** (external fixation, wound washout,
staged definitive fixation) as the default pathway rather than the exception.

**Tempo.** Hyperacute (minutes): exsanguinating pelvis/femur, open fracture with vascular injury,
compartment syndrome, cauda equina, a dislocated hip/knee with an ischaemic foot, septic arthritis.
Acute (hours): most fractures needing theatre within the admission, closed reduction of
dislocations, open-fracture debridement (<24h, ideally <12h for definitive debridement even though
antibiotics start <1h). Subacute (days–weeks): elective fixation of stable patterns, cast
follow-up, non-union/malunion surveillance. Chronic (months): TB spine/joints, osteomyelitis,
diabetic-foot Charcot/chronic ulceration, degenerative disease.

**The ward-round problem list is the same discipline as medicine, translated**: not "tibia
fracture" but "closed, displaced, distal-third tibia/fibula fracture — awaiting theatre, neuro-
vascularly intact, plan ORIF, VTE prophylaxis started, watch compartments." Every ortho problem
gets a *plan*, a *trigger to escalate* (usually neurovascular deterioration or pain out of
proportion), and an explicit weight-bearing/mobility instruction.

### 2.1 The open fracture

**Pattern spectrum — this is a classification, not a differential, but the tiers still matter:**
- **Gustilo–Anderson (assessed definitively only in theatre after debridement, not in casualty):**
  **Type I** — wound <1 cm, low energy, minimal contamination/muscle damage. **Type II** — wound
  >1 cm but <10 cm, moderate soft-tissue damage, no extensive flap/avulsion. **Type III** — high
  energy, wound >10 cm OR any segmental fracture, farmyard/highly contaminated wound, or any
  fracture requiring soft-tissue coverage regardless of wound size; subdivided **IIIA** (adequate
  soft-tissue coverage of bone achievable), **IIIB** (extensive soft-tissue loss, periosteal
  stripping, bone exposure requiring flap coverage), **IIIC** (any open fracture with an arterial
  injury requiring repair — the worst prognosis category regardless of wound size). **[grounded]**
- **The mimics/associated patterns to exclude:** a "closed" fracture with a nearby puncture wound
  from a bone spike that has retracted (still an open fracture — treat as such, do not be fooled by
  a small clean-looking punctum), a gunshot fracture (treat as open regardless of entry/exit wound
  size — high-velocity soft-tissue cavitation is invisible on the skin), a degloving injury with an
  intact fracture underneath (the skin, not the bone, is now the limb-threatening problem).

**Discriminating history.** Mechanism and energy (low-energy fall vs MVC/pedestrian/GSW —
predicts Gustilo grade before you even see the wound). Time since injury (drives the antibiotic and
debridement clock). Environment (farm/soil/water — anaerobic and unusual organism risk, e.g.
clostridial, marine organisms). Tetanus immunisation history. Comorbidities affecting healing
(diabetes, HIV, smoking, PVD). Neurovascular symptoms distal to the injury *before* any handling.

**Discriminating exam.** Wound size/contamination documented and **photographed once, then
covered with a saline-soaked dressing and NOT re-exposed repeatedly** (every extra look is an
infection risk and delays theatre without changing management). Neurovascular status distal to the
injury — pulses, capillary refill, sensation, motor — **before** any splinting/reduction, and
again **after**. Compartment assessment (an open fracture does **not** exclude compartment
syndrome — the fascia can still be intact even with a skin wound). Look for a second wound (through-
and-through, entry/exit).

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** Plain X-rays (joint above and below); do not delay the antibiotic
dose to get the X-ray. Hb/group & save (occult blood loss with long-bone fractures — a closed femur
1–1.5 L, tibia 0.5–1 L, pelvis potentially the entire volume [EK]). CRP/WCC baseline (for later
infection-trend comparison, not diagnostic acutely). CT angiogram if any hard or soft sign of
vascular injury (see §2.3). In a gunshot fracture, plain films to localise the bullet/fragments and
assess bone loss, not to "clear" the vascular status — that is a clinical/CTA decision.

**Management anchors (SA STG/EML-grounded framework).**
1. **Antibiotics as fast as possible, target <1 hour from arrival, and never delayed for imaging
   or transfer** — this is the single highest-yield intervention and the most litigated time
   target in ortho-trauma. **First-generation cephalosporin (cefazolin) IV** is the SA EML backbone
   for bone/joint cover [grounded via 2017–9 NEMLC musculoskeletal review: cloxacillin IV → cefazolin
   IV]; internationally-grounded dosing is **cefazolin 2 g IV stat then 2 g IV 8-hourly** for Types
   I/II. For **Type III**, add **gentamicin** (broad Gram-negative cover) and, where local
   protocol/severe contamination dictates, add **benzylpenicillin/metronidazole** for anaerobic/
   clostridial cover in soil-contaminated or farmyard wounds [EK — apply local unit protocol, doses
   above are internationally grounded and structurally consistent with SA EML antibiotic classes].
   Continue **24 h** post-wound-closure for Types I/II, and up to **72 h** or 24 h after definitive
   soft-tissue coverage for Type III [grounded]. Re-dose intraoperatively if the case runs long or
   there is major blood loss.
2. **Tetanus prophylaxis** per wound/immunisation status: tetanus toxoid-containing vaccine for
   any incompletely immunised patient or if >5–10 years since last booster for a contaminated/high-
   risk wound; **tetanus immunoglobulin** added for unknown/incomplete immunisation status with a
   contaminated or devitalised wound — give **within the first hours**, alongside the antibiotic
   dose, not as an afterthought at discharge [EK].
3. **Debridement** — thorough surgical washout and excision of all devitalised tissue; the
   historic "6-hour rule" for debridement has been superseded by evidence that **antibiotic
   timing matters more than the debridement clock**, but debridement should still happen as soon as
   feasibly possible (same-day, ideally <12–24 h) and is mandatory before any definitive fixation
   in a contaminated wound [EK/grounded via EAST-era literature].
4. **Fracture stabilisation** — provisional (splint, external fixator) if the soft-tissue envelope
   or the patient's physiology is not ready for definitive fixation ("damage control orthopaedics");
   definitive fixation (IM nail, plate) once the wound is clean and the patient stable. **In SA
   district/regional settings, external fixation is frequently the *definitive* strategy for weeks
   because of implant and theatre-slot backlog** — plan for this reality, not the textbook ideal.
5. **Wound left open or loosely closed initially** in higher-grade injuries, with planned re-look
   at 48–72 h; do not primarily close a heavily contaminated Type III wound under tension.
6. **VTE prophylaxis** once haemostasis secured (see §6/§4).

**Traps & pearls.** The "clean-looking small wound" is not a Type I fracture until the surgeon has
looked at the energy of the mechanism — a gunshot exit wound can be 8 mm and still represent
massive internal soft-tissue destruction; **grade by mechanism and by what you find in theatre, not
by the skin wound alone.** Do not repeatedly unwrap the dressing in casualty to "have a look" —
one photograph, one saline dressing, then cover and move to theatre; each exposure adds
contamination risk and delays antibiotics/theatre. **Antibiotics before X-ray if there is any
conflict for time** — a fracture pattern can wait fifteen minutes, established infection cannot be
undone. Never assume a fracture is closed just because the overlying skin looks intact in a
high-energy mechanism — examine circumferentially for a posterior/plantar/occult wound.

### 2.2 Compartment syndrome

**Differential of "pain out of proportion" in a limb (three tiers):**
- **Common cause of the pain-out-of-proportion picture:** the fracture itself (expected pain, but
  should *respond* to analgesia and immobilisation — if it doesn't, escalate the suspicion), a
  tight cast/splint/dressing (release it immediately as the first diagnostic-and-therapeutic step).
- **The emergency it must be distinguished from — true acute compartment syndrome:** classically
  tibial/forearm fractures (highest risk), crush injury, prolonged limb compression (the "found
  down" patient — a SA-relevant scenario after collapse/intoxication/assault), reperfusion after
  vascular repair, tight circumferential burns, snake bite (venom-induced), and — critically —
  **it occurs in a proportion of closed fractures with no wound at all**, so an open fracture does
  not protect against it and neither does the absence of one.
- **Zebras/consultant-considers:** exertional compartment syndrome (chronic, athletic, resolves
  with rest — a different disease, not an emergency), compartment syndrome from IV infiltration/
  extravasation, compartment syndrome of the hand/foot/thigh/gluteal region (less taught, easily
  missed because clinicians default to thinking "leg/forearm only"), abdominal compartment syndrome
  as a distractor mimicking the terminology but a different disease entirely.

**Discriminating history.** The **6 Ps are taught but the first two are the ones that actually
change management** — the rest are late and mean the window is closing: **Pain** out of
proportion to the injury and **increasing** despite adequate analgesia/immobilisation — the single
most reliable early symptom; **Pain on passive stretch** of the muscles in the compartment (passive
dorsiflexion of the toes for the deep posterior/anterior leg compartments) — the single most
reliable early *sign*. Then, late and unreliable for early diagnosis: **P**araesthesia, **P**allor,
**P**ulselessness, **P**aralysis — by the time these appear, muscle necrosis may already be
underway; **do not wait for pulselessness to diagnose compartment syndrome — the pulse is usually
still present because compartment pressure exceeds venous/capillary pressure long before it exceeds
arterial pressure.**

**Discriminating exam.** The compartment is **tense and firm** to palpation (compared to the
contralateral limb). Rising analgesia requirement on serial review is itself a vital sign — chart
it. In an **obtunded, intubated, sedated, or regional-blocked patient** (a common SA trauma-bay
scenario), the pain history is unavailable — this is when **compartment pressure measurement
becomes mandatory rather than optional**, because clinical exam alone cannot be trusted.

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** **Intracompartmental pressure measurement** (needle manometry/
Stryker device) where available: absolute pressure **>30 mmHg** is concerning, but the more
physiologically accurate threshold is the **delta pressure (ΔP) = diastolic BP − compartment
pressure; ΔP ≤30 mmHg (i.e., compartment pressure within 30 mmHg of diastolic) is the indication
for fasciotomy**, because it accounts for the patient's own perfusion pressure rather than a fixed
number (a hypotensive polytrauma patient can develop compartment syndrome at a lower absolute
compartment pressure than a hypertensive one) [grounded]. **CK and renal function** — rising CK
with myoglobinuria (dipstick blood-positive, no RBCs on microscopy) signals muscle
necrosis/rhabdomyolysis and the risk of AKI; trend it, don't single-point it. Serial exams are
themselves "the investigation" — document timed, dated neurovascular checks, not a single note.

**Management anchors.** **Release all constrictive dressings/casts first** (split the cast fully,
including the padding, down to skin) — this alone can be diagnostic and therapeutic and buys time.
If clinical suspicion persists (or pressures confirm), **emergency fasciotomy — the definitive and
only real treatment.** **The outcome is time-critical: fasciotomy within 6 hours of symptom onset
gives near-complete functional recovery in the large majority of patients; delay to 12 hours or
beyond drops good functional outcomes to roughly two-thirds or worse, and delay beyond ~8–12 hours
risks irreversible muscle/nerve necrosis, contracture (Volkmann's), and limb loss** [grounded]. Do
not elevate the limb above heart level (reduces arterial inflow and worsens perfusion pressure —
elevate to heart level only). Do not rely on ice/analgesia to "buy time" — they mask the pain sign
without treating the pathology.

**Traps & pearls.** **The classic error is waiting for the late signs (pulselessness, pallor) before
acting — by then the window has often closed.** Escalating analgesia requirement in a splinted limb
*is* the alarm, not a nuisance to be medicated away — treat rising opioid requirement as a
diagnostic sign, not just a symptom to suppress. Compartment syndrome can and does occur in an
**open** fracture — never use the open wound as false reassurance that pressure can "escape."
Bilateral lower-limb compartment syndrome occurs in the prolonged "found down" patient (collapse,
overdose, alcohol intoxication lying on a limb for hours) — a scenario the SA trauma/medical
interface sees regularly; have a low threshold in any patient found down for an unknown period.
Document neurovascular status **before and after** every fasciotomy, cast change, and reduction —
this is the medico-legal backbone of the whole condition.

### 2.3 Neurovascular injury — the pulseless or nerve-deficit limb

**Pattern spectrum by mechanism/site (this is where "differential" becomes "which structure, by
which injury"):**
- **Fracture-associated nerve injury by site** — a closed-reduction/consultant-level pattern
  recognition list [EK]: **shoulder dislocation → axillary nerve** (regimental-badge sensation,
  deltoid); **humeral shaft fracture → radial nerve** (wrist drop, first dorsal webspace
  sensation — classically at the spiral groove); **elbow dislocation/supracondylar fracture →
  median (anterior interosseous) or ulnar nerve**; **posterior hip dislocation → sciatic nerve**
  (especially the peroneal division — foot drop); **knee dislocation → peroneal nerve** (foot
  drop, dorsal foot sensation) **and popliteal artery** (see below); **fibular neck fracture/
  tight cast → common peroneal nerve** (foot drop — the most commonly compressed nerve in a
  cast).
- **Fracture/dislocation-associated vascular injury — the "hard sign, soft sign" framework**
  (adapted from penetrating-trauma vascular assessment and equally applicable to blunt
  fracture-dislocation): **hard signs (mandate immediate theatre/exploration, no time for
  imaging)** — absent distal pulse, expanding/pulsatile haematoma, audible bruit/palpable thrill,
  active pulsatile bleeding, obvious distal ischaemia (pale, cold, painful, paralysed). **Soft
  signs (warrant CT angiogram/further workup, not immediate theatre)** — reduced but present
  pulse, small non-expanding haematoma, peripheral nerve deficit in the same territory as a named
  vessel, history of significant bleeding at the scene, proximity of the wound/fracture to a major
  vessel.
- **The classic limb-threatening dislocation: knee (tibiofemoral) dislocation.** Even a dislocation
  that has spontaneously reduced before arrival carries a high rate of **popliteal artery injury**
  (intimal tear causing delayed thrombosis is common and can present hours later with a normal
  initial pulse) — **every knee dislocation gets serial neurovascular exam and a low threshold for
  CT angiography regardless of the initial pulse exam**, because a normal pulse at presentation
  does not exclude an evolving intimal injury.

**Discriminating history.** Mechanism (high-energy vs low-energy — a "simple" ankle sprain that was
actually a spontaneously-reduced dislocation), time since injury/reduction, any numbness/weakness
noticed by the patient before you even examine them (patients often localise nerve territory
accurately), prior vascular disease (a diabetic/PVD patient's "normal" pulse may already be
diminished at baseline — ask, and compare to the other side).

**Discriminating exam.** Pulses (palpate and, if in doubt, **Doppler and compare Ankle-Brachial
Index/segmental pressures to the contralateral limb** — an ABI <0.9 in the injured limb is
abnormal and warrants CTA). Capillary refill, skin colour/temperature, motor and sensory exam by
named nerve territory (not just "sensation intact" — test each nerve distribution specifically).
**Document before AND after every reduction, splint application, and positioning change** — this
is the theme that runs through this entire dossier because it is the theme that runs through
orthopaedic litigation.

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** **CT angiography** is the investigation of choice for any soft-sign
or equivocal vascular exam — fast, available in most SA regional/tertiary centres, and avoids the
delay of formal catheter angiography. **Do not image a hard-sign limb — go straight to theatre**;
imaging in a hard-sign presentation only delays definitive vascular repair and risks limb loss.
Nerve conduction studies/EMG have no role acutely — they are a subacute tool (weeks later) to
distinguish neurapraxia (recovers) from axonotmesis/neurotmesis (may not) once the acute picture has
settled.

**Management anchors.** Reduce the dislocation/fracture **immediately** if it is causing vascular
compromise — do not wait for imaging if pulses are absent and the limb is ischaemic; reduction
itself often restores perfusion and is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Re-check neurovascular
status immediately after reduction — if pulses remain absent post-reduction, this is now a vascular
surgical emergency (CTA if time allows and pulse is merely diminished, straight to theatre if
still absent/ischaemic — **ischaemia time to amputation risk rises sharply beyond 6 hours warm
ischaemia** [EK, standard vascular-surgery teaching]). Splint in the position of reduction/safety
and re-examine on a fixed schedule (not "as needed" — schedule it: e.g. hourly for the first 4–6
hours). Nerve injury without vascular compromise is usually managed expectantly (most closed
traction/stretch neurapraxias recover over weeks to months); document, splint to protect the nerve
(e.g. a wrist-drop splint), and refer early if no recovery by the expected window or if the nerve
injury is at a site more likely to need exploration (a laceration in direct proximity to the nerve).

**Traps & pearls.** **A palpable pulse does not exclude a vascular injury** — collateral flow can
maintain a distal pulse despite a significant proximal injury (the "pink pulseless hand" of
paediatric supracondylar fractures is the classic teaching case — see §2.8). **Reduce first, image
second** when the limb is frankly ischaemic — every minute spent obtaining imaging in a hard-sign
presentation is muscle and nerve dying. Never discharge a "sprain" with a nerve deficit without
considering that the joint may have spontaneously reduced from a dislocation. Document a pre- and
post-manipulation neurovascular exam **every single time**, with a timestamp — this is not
paperwork, it is the difference between "the deficit was pre-existing" and "we may have caused it."

### 2.4 The hip/neck-of-femur fracture in the elderly — the whole pathway

**Pattern spectrum (fracture location changes everything about management):**
- **Intracapsular (subcapital/transcervical) fractures** — blood supply to the femoral head (via
  the retinacular vessels along the femoral neck) is at risk; displacement predicts avascular
  necrosis risk. Classified by **Garden**: **Garden I** — incomplete/valgus impacted (stable);
  **Garden II** — complete, undisplaced (stable); **Garden III** — complete, partially displaced
  (unstable); **Garden IV** — complete, fully displaced, fragments not in contact (unstable, worst
  AVN risk) [grounded]. Garden I–II are often treated with internal fixation (screws) to preserve
  the native head; Garden III–IV in the elderly are usually treated with **hemiarthroplasty** (low-
  demand, limited mobility) or **total hip replacement** (higher-functioning, independently
  mobile) rather than fixation, because AVN and non-union rates are prohibitively high in displaced
  fractures [grounded].
- **Extracapsular fractures (intertrochanteric, subtrochanteric)** — blood supply is preserved
  (metaphyseal/diaphyseal bone, good vascularity); these are almost always treated with
  **fixation** (dynamic hip screw for intertrochanteric, intramedullary nail for
  subtrochanteric/reverse-oblique patterns) rather than replacement.
- **The mimics/must-not-miss in the "fell and can't weight-bear" elderly patient:** an
  **occult/undisplaced fracture with a normal-looking initial X-ray** (if clinical suspicion is
  high — groin pain, inability to weight-bear, pain on axial/log-roll of the leg — and the X-ray is
  normal, get an MRI or CT; do not discharge a clinically-suspicious hip on a normal plain film),
  a **pathological fracture** (metastatic disease, myeloma — look for a lytic lesion, ask about
  weight loss/known malignancy), a **pubic ramus fracture** (presents identically with groin pain
  and reduced mobility but does not need surgery — usually managed with analgesia and
  mobilisation), and — critically — **ask why they fell** (syncope, arrhythmia, hypoglycaemia,
  stroke, sepsis, postural hypotension, polypharmacy) because the fall is often a symptom of an
  underlying medical event that will kill the patient faster than the fracture will if missed.

**Discriminating history.** Mechanism (low-energy fall from standing height is the classic fragility
fracture in an osteoporotic elderly patient; a fall in a younger patient or high-energy mechanism
raises suspicion of a different pattern or associated injury). **The pre-fall history is as
important as the fracture** — collapse, chest pain, palpitations, a preceding infective illness,
new confusion, medication changes (sedatives, antihypertensives). Baseline mobility and cognitive
status (drives rehabilitation goals and delirium-risk stratification). Anticoagulant use (delays
theatre until reversed/safe — but delay itself carries mortality risk, so this becomes a genuine
balancing act with anaesthetics).

**Discriminating exam.** The classic **shortened, externally rotated leg** with groin pain and
inability to straight-leg-raise or weight-bear. Full secondary survey (a fall in the elderly
frequently causes more than one injury — wrist, humerus, ribs, head). **Cognitive screen at
baseline** (AMTS/similar) — essential both to detect delirium later and to establish a pre-op
baseline for consent and prognosis discussion. Pressure areas (a delayed-to-theatre elderly patient
lying on a trolley develops a sacral/heel pressure sore with frightening speed — check and
document skin at admission).

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** AP pelvis + lateral hip X-ray (compare both hips — Shenton's line
disruption). MRI or CT if clinical suspicion persists despite a normal plain film (occult fracture).
**FBC, U&E, glucose, group & save, ECG** — routine but purposeful: anaemia (occult blood loss into
the fracture site can be substantial even in a "closed" intracapsular fracture, more so
extracapsular), AKI/dehydration (common in elderly patients lying undiagnosed for hours after a
fall — the "long lie"), and the ECG/troponin screen for the arrhythmia or silent MI that may have
*caused* the fall. **CK** if there is any suspicion of a prolonged lie (rhabdomyolysis).

**Management anchors — this is a whole-patient pathway, not a fracture fixation decision alone.**
1. **Analgesia early and adequately** — a fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block is excellent,
   opioid-sparing, and reduces delirium risk; use it early in the ED/admission pathway rather than
   relying solely on systemic opioids (which worsen confusion in the elderly).
2. **Medical optimisation, not medical delay** — the modern standard is **surgery within 24–48
   hours** wherever physiologically possible; delay increases mortality, pressure sores,
   pneumonia, and delirium. "Awaiting medical clearance" should mean *rapid, targeted*
   correction of reversible physiology (anaemia, electrolyte derangement, rate-controlling a new
   arrhythmia, reversing anticoagulation) — not an open-ended wait for a "perfect" patient who will
   never arrive. **Every day of delay without a clear medical reason is a mortality risk**, and
   this tension is acute in SA state hospitals where theatre-slot and implant backlog routinely
   push beyond 48 hours — flag it, escalate it, don't accept it silently.
3. **VTE prophylaxis** from admission (mechanical ± pharmacological once any bleeding risk from
   surgery has settled) — hip fracture patients are among the highest VTE-risk orthopaedic
   patients (see §6).
4. **Delirium prevention bundle** — orientation, early mobilisation, hearing/visual aids, adequate
   analgesia (avoid over-sedation), correction of constipation/urinary retention, sleep hygiene,
   avoiding unnecessary catheters and lines, involving family. Delirium in this population is
   common, under-recognised (especially the hypoactive form), and independently worsens mortality
   and long-term cognitive outcome.
5. **Nutrition, pressure-area care, and early physiotherapy** from day one post-op — weight-bearing
   as tolerated is the modern default (fixation/arthroplasty chosen specifically to allow this).
6. **Osteoporosis work-up and secondary-fracture prevention** before discharge — calcium/vitamin D,
   bisphosphonate consideration, falls assessment — this is a fragility fracture and, without
   secondary prevention, the contralateral hip is a recurring statistic.

**Traps & pearls.** **Never accept "normal X-ray" as the end of the investigation in a clinically
convincing hip fracture** — occult fractures are common and delay to surgery worsens outcome even
in the undisplaced/occult group once finally diagnosed. **The fall is a symptom** — a hip fracture
worked up without asking "why did you fall" misses the arrhythmia/MI/stroke/sepsis/hypoglycaemia
that caused it and may recur and kill the patient in the peri-operative period. Anaemia from a
"closed" intracapsular fracture is real — check and cross-match, don't assume intracapsular
fractures don't bleed much. Delirium is not "just old age" — it is a medical emergency and a
marker of an unaddressed problem (pain, infection, electrolyte, drug). **The mortality benefit of
early surgery is one of the best-established outcomes in all of orthopaedics** — protect the <48h
window as a system priority, not a nice-to-have.

### 2.5 Distal radius fracture

**Pattern spectrum.** **Colles' fracture** — dorsally displaced/angulated distal radius (classic
"dinner-fork deformity"), fall onto an outstretched, dorsiflexed hand (FOOSH) — the commonest
fracture pattern seen in emergency departments generally. **Smith's fracture** — volarly
displaced/angulated (the reverse pattern), fall onto a flexed wrist or direct blow to the dorsum —
inherently less stable, more often needs fixation. **Barton's fracture** — an intra-articular
fracture-dislocation of the distal radius (dorsal or volar rim) with carpal subluxation — unstable,
almost always needs fixation. **Distal radius fracture with distal ulnar styloid fracture** — check
distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) stability. In children, this same mechanism more often produces a
**Salter-Harris physeal injury** or a greenstick/torus (buckle) fracture rather than the adult
patterns — different classification, different healing potential, different threshold for
manipulation.

**Discriminating history.** Mechanism (FOOSH — dorsally angulated Colles pattern is the overwhelming
majority) and hand dominance (functional priority in fixation decisions). Osteoporosis risk factors
(post-menopausal woman with a low-energy fall — this is a fragility fracture and, like the hip, a
sentinel event warranting osteoporosis screening). Occupation (manual labourer vs sedentary changes
the threshold for accepting residual deformity vs pursuing surgical fixation).

**Discriminating exam.** Deformity, swelling, dinner-fork appearance. **Median nerve
(specifically anterior interosseous nerve, then full median) status** — the distal radius fracture
at risk of acute carpal tunnel syndrome from swelling/displacement; test thumb-index pinch (AIN) and
palmar sensation. Assess the whole forearm and elbow (a distal radius fracture with an associated
proximal injury — e.g. Galeazzi/Monteggia patterns higher up the forearm — is missed if you only
X-ray the wrist).

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** AP and lateral wrist X-ray — **describe the fracture systematically:
site (distal radius metaphysis), pattern (transverse/comminuted/intra-articular), displacement
(dorsal/volar, % of shaft width), angulation (degrees, direction), shortening, and intra-articular
extension (does the fracture line enter the radiocarpal or DRUJ joint surface)** — this description
language is the universal grammar of fracture reporting and drives the reduction target. Assess
**radial height, radial inclination, and volar tilt** against normal reference values (normal
volar tilt ~11°, radial inclination ~22°, radial height ~11 mm [EK]) — the post-reduction film is
compared against these, not just "looks better."

**Management anchors.** Closed reduction under haematoma block/procedural sedation for displaced
fractures, then immobilisation in a below-elbow (or above-elbow, per unit preference for
stability) backslab/cast with the wrist in slight flexion and ulnar deviation for dorsally-
displaced patterns — **followed by a mandatory post-reduction X-ray to confirm alignment**, and
neurovascular re-check. Indications for surgical fixation (K-wires, volar locking plate, external
fixator): failure to achieve/maintain acceptable reduction, significant intra-articular
displacement/step-off, marked comminution/instability, an active/high-demand patient, or an open
fracture. Undisplaced/stable/well-reduced fractures — cast immobilisation, serial X-rays at 1 and
2 weeks to check for redisplacement (the fracture can slip in the cast as swelling reduces).

**Traps & pearls.** **A tight cast on a swelling forearm is a compartment-syndrome and acute-
carpal-tunnel risk** — split the cast fully if pain escalates, do not just "loosen the bandage."
Never treat a distal radius fracture in isolation without examining the elbow and the DRUJ —
missed proximal forearm injuries are a classic trap. An elderly patient's distal radius fracture is,
like the hip, a fragility-fracture sentinel event — flag for osteoporosis assessment, this is
frequently forgotten because the fracture itself "isn't dangerous."

### 2.6 Ankle fracture

**Pattern spectrum — the Weber classification (based on the level of the fibular fracture relative
to the syndesmosis, which predicts stability):**
- **Weber A** — fibular fracture **below** the syndesmosis (a pure avulsion-type injury,
  syndesmosis intact) — usually stable, often managed conservatively.
- **Weber B** — fibular fracture **at the level of** the syndesmosis — syndesmosis may or may not
  be disrupted; stability depends on the medial side (deltoid ligament/medial malleolus) — the
  "it depends" category needing careful assessment of the medial structures and mortise congruity.
- **Weber C** — fibular fracture **above** the syndesmosis — syndesmosis is disrupted by
  definition; these are unstable and almost always need surgical fixation to restore the mortise
  and stabilise the syndesmosis [grounded]. **(Lauge-Hansen classification, based on mechanism —
  supination-adduction, supination-external rotation, pronation-abduction, pronation-external
  rotation — is the alternative system, more mechanistic, less commonly used day-to-day but useful
  for predicting the pattern of associated injury.)**
- **The must-not-miss associated injury:** a **Maisonneuve fracture** — a Weber-C-equivalent
  pattern where the fibular fracture is at the **proximal** fibula (near the knee) with a disrupted
  syndesmosis and often a medial malleolar/deltoid injury at the ankle — **always palpate the full
  length of the fibula, including the fibular head, in any ankle injury with medial tenderness or
  an unexplained mortise widening**, because an isolated ankle X-ray will miss a proximal fibula
  fracture that is not in the film.

**Discriminating history.** Mechanism (twisting/rotational vs axial load vs direct blow — predicts
Lauge-Hansen pattern). Ability to weight-bear (drives the Ottawa Ankle Rule decision — see §5).
Point of maximal tenderness (lateral malleolus alone vs medial + lateral vs proximal fibula/
"high ankle" pain radiating up the leg — the last raises Maisonneuve suspicion).

**Discriminating exam.** Palpate the **entire fibula from lateral malleolus to fibular head** —
non-negotiable in any ankle injury. Medial malleolar tenderness and deltoid ligament laxity.
**Syndesmosis stress tests** — the squeeze test (compressing tibia and fibula together mid-calf
reproduces distal syndesmotic pain) and external rotation stress test. Neurovascular status distal
(dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial pulses, sensation). Assess for an associated dislocation
(grossly deformed ankle — reduce clinically before imaging if neurovascularly compromised, exactly
as for any other dislocation — do not wait for an X-ray to reduce an ischaemic, dislocated ankle).

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** **Ottawa Ankle Rule** to decide who needs an X-ray at all (see
§5). AP, lateral, and mortise-view ankle X-rays. Assess **mortise congruity** (the talus should sit
symmetrically in the mortise — widening of the medial clear space >4 mm suggests deltoid
ligament/syndesmotic injury even without a visible medial malleolar fracture). If Maisonneuve is
suspected clinically, **X-ray the full length of the tibia/fibula, not just the ankle** — this is a
distinct imaging request, the standard ankle series will not include the fibular head. CT for
complex intra-articular (pilon, trimalleolar) patterns to plan fixation.

**Management anchors.** Weber A/stable Weber B with a congruent mortise and no medial injury —
conservative: cast/boot immobilisation, weight-bearing per unit protocol, follow-up X-ray to
confirm maintained reduction. Weber C, unstable Weber B (widened mortise, medial injury),
bimalleolar/trimalleolar fractures, and any fracture-dislocation → **surgical fixation (ORIF ±
syndesmotic screw/suture-button for syndesmosis disruption).** A dislocated ankle with skin
compromise or neurovascular deficit → **reduce immediately in the emergency department**, splint,
re-X-ray, then definitive fixation once swelling allows (often after a delay of days for a high-
energy pattern — "spanning" external fixation may bridge this gap).

**Traps & pearls.** The Maisonneuve fracture is missed when the clinician X-rays "the ankle" without
examining/imaging the proximal fibula — always palpate the fibular head. A widened mortise with no
visible fracture is still an unstable injury (deltoid ligament rupture behaves like a medial
malleolar fracture functionally) — do not be reassured by "no fracture seen" if the mortise is
incongruent. Reduce a dislocated, skin-tenting or neurovascularly compromised ankle before imaging,
exactly as for any other limb-threatening dislocation.

### 2.7 The paediatric supracondylar humerus fracture

**Pattern spectrum — Gartland classification (extension-type, the overwhelming majority):**
- **Type I** — undisplaced (may be subtle — look for a posterior fat pad sign and the anterior
  humeral line failing to intersect the capitellum's middle third on a true lateral).
- **Type II** — displaced with an intact posterior cortex (hinged, angulated but not fully
  displaced).
- **Type III** — completely displaced, no cortical continuity — the highest-risk group for
  neurovascular injury and the group where the **"pink pulseless hand"** phenomenon is best
  described [grounded]: the radial pulse is absent (brachial artery kinked/spasmed/entrapped by
  the proximal fragment) **but the hand remains pink, warm, and well-perfused via collateral flow**
  — this is a genuinely different clinical entity from a **white, cold, pulseless hand** (true
  ischaemia), and the two are managed differently.

**Discriminating history.** Mechanism — a fall onto an outstretched, hyperextended elbow (extension
type, >95% of cases) vs a fall onto a flexed elbow (flexion type, uncommon, different displacement
direction). Age (peak 5–7 years). **Any mechanism inconsistent with the injury pattern, a delayed
presentation, or a story that changes between carers/re-tellings is a non-accidental injury (NAI)
red flag** — mandatory in every paediatric fracture assessment, and supracondylar fractures in a
non-mobile or very young child especially warrant this scrutiny.

**Discriminating exam.** Swelling, deformity, an S-shaped deformity/skin puckering anteriorly
(suggests the proximal fragment has buttonholed through brachialis — a sign that closed reduction
may be difficult and that soft tissue is at risk). **Full neurovascular exam of all three nerves**
at risk — **anterior interosseous nerve (AIN)** (the nerve most commonly injured in extension-type
supracondylar fractures — test by asking for an OK-sign/pincer between thumb and index tip, pure
motor, no sensory component so cannot be tested by sensation alone), **median nerve**, **radial
nerve**, and **ulnar nerve** (more often injured in flexion-type or during closed reduction/pinning
itself) — document each individually, not just "moves fingers." Pulse and capillary refill, and
critically — **classify the hand as pink-and-perfused-but-pulseless vs white-and-cold-and-
pulseless**, because this distinction changes the urgency and pathway.

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** AP and true lateral elbow X-ray. **Anterior humeral line** (a line
drawn along the anterior humeral cortex on a true lateral should pass through the middle third of
the capitellum — if it passes through the anterior third or misses it entirely, there is posterior
displacement even if subtle). **Posterior fat pad sign** (a fat pad visible posteriorly on the
lateral view is abnormal and implies an intra-articular effusion/occult fracture — a Type I
injury) — the anterior fat pad can be a normal finding, the posterior fat pad is not. **Baumann's
angle** for carrying-angle assessment in displaced fractures.

**Management anchors.** Type I — immobilise in a collar-and-cuff/above-elbow backslab, no reduction
needed, follow-up X-ray. Type II — closed reduction and percutaneous K-wire fixation in most units
(some stable Type II managed in a cast alone per local protocol). **Type III — this is a time-
sensitive orthopaedic emergency**: closed reduction and percutaneous pinning under anaesthesia,
urgently. **The pink pulseless hand**: current consensus favours **urgent closed reduction and
pinning first** (the vascular kink is usually mechanical and resolves with reduction); if the pulse
does not return after reduction but the hand remains well-perfused (pink, warm, capillary refill
normal), **close observation is reasonable** rather than mandatory vascular exploration, though
practice and evidence remain debated and this decision should involve a senior/vascular
consultation, not a junior call alone [grounded — genuine area of ongoing surgical debate]. **The
white, cold, pulseless hand is a true vascular emergency** — reduce immediately; if perfusion does
not return, urgent vascular exploration/repair is required — there is no observation period for a
truly ischaemic hand.

**Traps & pearls.** The **AIN palsy is easy to miss** because it has no sensory component — test the
OK-sign specifically, do not just check "can move fingers." **Never assume pink = safe** — a pink
pulseless hand still needs urgent reduction, it is simply not the same "minutes count" emergency as
a white pulseless hand, and the distinction must be made explicitly and documented. **Every
paediatric fracture gets an NAI screen** — mechanism-injury congruence, developmental stage
consistency (can a non-mobile infant really have sustained a "fall"?), delayed presentation, and a
check for other occult injuries/bruising patterns (see clinical-foundations.md's NAI flag — this
dossier extends it: specifically for supracondylar fractures, a spiral or metaphyseal pattern
inconsistent with the stated mechanism, or bilateral/differently-aged fractures, raises suspicion
further).

### 2.8 The open-book pelvic fracture and pelvic haemorrhage

**Pattern spectrum.** **Lateral compression (LC)** — the commonest pattern, pelvis "closes" —
usually less haemorrhagic. **Anteroposterior compression (APC)/"open-book"** — pubic symphysis
diastasis with posterior sacroiliac disruption, the pelvic volume **increases**, allowing massive
retroperitoneal haemorrhage to accumulate with less tamponade effect — the highest-risk pattern for
exsanguination. **Vertical shear** — a high-energy fall/MVC pattern with vertical displacement of
a hemipelvis, associated with the highest rates of major vascular and neurological injury.
**Combined mechanism** patterns also occur. Regardless of the formal Young-Burgess subtype, the
unifying danger is the same: **pelvic fractures can hide the patient's entire circulating blood
volume in the retroperitoneal space with no external sign of it whatsoever.**

**Discriminating history.** High-energy mechanism (MVC, pedestrian vs vehicle, fall from height,
crush) — this is essentially never a low-energy injury in an adult (except the elderly
osteoporotic pubic ramus fracture from a simple fall, a very different, low-risk entity — see
§2.4). Associated symptoms — inability to void, blood at the urethral meatus, perineal/scrotal
bruising (urethral injury), abdominal pain (intra-abdominal associated injury), rectal
bleeding (open pelvic fracture communicating with the rectum — devastatingly high mortality).

**Discriminating exam.** **Do NOT spring/rock the pelvis repeatedly to "test for instability"** —
this is outdated teaching and each manipulation can dislodge a stabilising clot and provoke further
haemorrhage; a single gentle assessment of gross instability (or simply the mechanism and imaging)
is sufficient — **apply a pelvic binder empirically in any high-energy mechanism with suspected
pelvic injury, before confirmatory imaging, as part of the primary survey circulation step.**
Inspect the perineum, scrotum/labia, and do a rectal exam (blood, high-riding prostate — urethral
injury) and a vaginal exam in women (occult open fracture communicating with the vagina). Look for
leg-length discrepancy/rotational deformity (suggests a vertically unstable pattern). Full secondary
survey for associated injuries (pelvic fractures rarely occur in isolation in high-energy trauma).

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** AP pelvis X-ray as part of the trauma series (with CXR, C-spine) —
rapid, in the resuscitation bay. **CT pelvis with contrast** once the patient is stable enough to
transfer — identifies the fracture pattern in detail and, critically, **contrast extravasation
(a "blush") localising active arterial bleeding** that may need angio-embolisation. FAST/eFAST to
assess for free intraperitoneal fluid (distinguishing purely retroperitoneal pelvic bleeding from
an additional intra-abdominal source). Serial Hb (a single value is misleadingly reassuring early —
trend it) and lactate/base deficit as resuscitation markers.

**Management anchors.** **Circulation control is the priority, ahead of definitive fracture
management**: (1) **pelvic binder** applied at the level of the greater trochanters (not the iliac
crests) immediately on suspicion, reducing pelvic volume and providing some tamponade; (2) **massive
transfusion protocol** activation early if haemodynamically unstable — blood products, not crystalloid,
as the primary resuscitation fluid in haemorrhagic shock, with permissive hypotension in the
absence of head injury until source control; (3) **source control** — angio-embolisation for
arterial bleeding (interventional radiology, where available — a resource-constrained bottleneck
in many SA settings, see §6), pre-peritoneal pelvic packing as a surgical damage-control option
where angio is unavailable/the patient is too unstable to leave the resuscitation bay, and external
fixation/pelvic C-clamp for mechanical stabilisation; (4) definitive internal fixation once the
patient is physiologically stable, often days later. **Urethral injury suspected → do not pass a
urethral catheter blind; get a retrograde urethrogram first or place a suprapubic catheter.**

**Traps & pearls.** The pelvis can look "fine" externally while the patient exsanguinates internally
— treat any high-energy mechanism with haemodynamic instability and no other obvious source as a
pelvic bleed until excluded. Repeated pelvic "springing" to test stability is harmful, not
diagnostic — stop teaching/doing it. Binder placement at the wrong level (too high, at the iliac
crests) fails to reduce the fracture and can even cause harm — trochanteric level is correct. An
open pelvic fracture (communicating with rectum/vagina/perineal wound) carries a mortality rate far
higher than a closed pattern of similar severity and needs the same open-fracture antibiotic/
tetanus discipline as any other open fracture (§2.1) plus faecal diversion consideration.

### 2.9 The acutely hot, swollen joint — septic arthritis

**Differential — three tiers:**
- **Common:** reactive arthritis (post-infective, e.g. post-GI/GU infection), gout/pseudogout
  (crystal arthropathy — can be clinically indistinguishable from sepsis and both can coexist),
  haemarthrosis (trauma, anticoagulation, haemophilia), osteoarthritis flare.
- **Dangerous / must-not-miss:** **native-joint septic arthritis** (the joint that is destroyed
  within **24–48 hours** of untreated infection — cartilage is avascular and has no capacity to
  clear bacteria/inflammatory enzymes, so delay is directly destructive, not just "the infection
  getting worse"), **prosthetic joint infection** (a hot swollen joint around a known
  arthroplasty is septic until proven otherwise and needs a different diagnostic/surgical pathway
  from a native joint), **gonococcal arthritis** (in the sexually active young adult — may present
  as a migratory polyarthralgia/tenosynovitis-dermatitis syndrome before localising to one joint),
  necrotising fasciitis overlying/adjacent to a joint (a surgical emergency distinct from but
  sometimes confused with septic arthritis).
- **Zebras/consultant-considers:** **TB arthritis** (subacute, less florid than pyogenic sepsis,
  a "cold" abscess picture — relevant and common in the SA HIV/TB-endemic context, see §2.14),
  reactive arthritis associated with HIV seroconversion itself, disseminated gonococcal infection
  with tenosynovitis, sickle-cell-associated joint crisis (mimics sepsis, and the two can co-occur
  — sickle patients get osteomyelitis/septic arthritis at higher rates too, particularly
  Salmonella osteomyelitis), Lyme arthritis (not endemic in SA, but relevant in a travel history),
  and rheumatological mimics (adult-onset Still's, acute sarcoid arthropathy).

**Discriminating history.** Acute onset over hours, fever, rigors, systemic unwellness (favours
sepsis over crystal/reactive, though the overlap is real and gout can also cause fever). Risk
factors — diabetes, immunosuppression (including HIV — a major SA-relevant risk factor for both
higher incidence and atypical/more severe presentation), IV drug use, recent joint injection/
arthroscopy/surgery, skin breach near the joint, known prosthetic joint, sexual history (gonococcal).
Prior episodes (recurrent mono-arthritis raises crystal arthropathy). Anticoagulation/bleeding
disorder (haemarthrosis).

**Discriminating exam.** Hot, red, exquisitely tender joint held in the position of maximum capsular
volume (e.g. hip flexed/abducted/externally rotated), **grossly restricted active AND passive range
of movement** (a joint that still moves through a reasonable range, even if painful, makes septic
arthritis somewhat less likely though never fully excludes it — passive movement is the more
reliable discriminator). Fever (may be absent, especially in the elderly/immunosuppressed —
absence does not exclude). Look for a source (skin breach, cellulitis tracking to the joint, a
distant infective focus that seeded haematogenously). Examine for a joint effusion versus
peri-articular swelling (a true joint effusion is central and limits movement in all planes; a
peri-articular process like cellulitis or bursitis is more focal and better tolerates some
movement).

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** **Joint aspiration BEFORE antibiotics, always, whenever safely
possible — this is the single highest-yield and most time-critical step**, because antibiotics
given first sterilise the fluid and can make the diagnosis unrecoverable while the joint continues
to be destroyed. Send synovial fluid for: **Gram stain and microscopy, culture, cell count with
differential (a WCC >50,000/mm³ with >90% neutrophils is highly suggestive of bacterial infection,
though lower counts do not exclude it — especially early or partially treated), crystal
examination (polarised light — negatively birefringent needle-shaped = gout, positively
birefringent rhomboid = pseudogout), and glucose/protein** if locally practised (low glucose,
high protein support infection but are non-specific). **Do not delay aspiration to "wait for the
X-ray" or "wait for bloods"** — it is the priority investigation. Blood cultures (often positive in
haematogenous seeding, and useful when the joint fluid Gram stain is negative). **FBC, CRP, ESR** —
raised but non-specific; **the trend of CRP/ESR over days is the useful monitoring tool for response
to treatment**, not the absolute admission value, which can be falsely reassuring-normal early or
elevated from an unrelated cause. Plain X-ray — usually unhelpful acutely (may show only soft-tissue
swelling/effusion in early disease) but establishes a baseline and can reveal pre-existing joint
disease or an adjacent osteomyelitis. Ultrasound to guide aspiration in a deep/difficult joint (hip)
where blind aspiration is unreliable. MRI where available and if osteomyelitis extension or a deep
joint (hip, shoulder) diagnosis is uncertain — the best modality for early bone/joint infection but
frequently a resource-limited/delayed test in SA state facilities, so clinical suspicion plus
aspiration should not wait for it.

**Management anchors (SA STG/EML-grounded).** **Empirical antibiotics targeted at *Staphylococcus
aureus*, the dominant pathogen in SA bone/joint sepsis series (with Pseudomonas aeruginosa a
distant second in some series)** — **cefazolin IV** is the current SA EML agent of choice
following the 2017–2019 NEMLC review (replacing IV cloxacillin), with **oral flucloxacillin**
substituted for cloxacillin where cloxacillin supply is unreliable [grounded]. Broaden empirically
for risk factors: add Gram-negative cover for the elderly/immunosuppressed/hospital-acquired
picture, consider gonococcal cover (ceftriaxone) in the sexually active young adult with a
compatible picture, and adjust once culture and sensitivity results return — **de-escalate to the
narrowest effective agent once organism/sensitivity known.** **Surgical/urgent joint washout
(arthrotomy or arthroscopic lavage) is the definitive source-control step for a confirmed or
strongly suspected septic joint — this is not "antibiotics and observe," this is a surgical
emergency analogous to an abscess that needs draining**; the hip in particular (a deep joint,
difficult to serially aspirate, at the highest risk of avascular necrosis in children) has a very
low threshold for surgical washout. Duration of antibiotics is typically weeks (IV transitioning to
oral once clinically improving and inflammatory markers trending down), guided by clinical response
and CRP trend rather than a fixed number in isolation.

**Traps & pearls.** **Aspirate before antibiotics — this is the single most repeated and most
violated rule in this entire section.** A normal or near-normal CRP/WCC does not exclude septic
arthritis, especially early or in an immunosuppressed patient — trust the joint exam and aspirate.
Gout and septic arthritis can and do coexist — a positive crystal finding does not fully exclude
infection, especially in a first presentation, systemically unwell, or high-risk patient; when in
real doubt, treat empirically for sepsis pending cultures rather than assume "it's just gout." A
prosthetic joint that becomes hot and painful is septic until an orthopaedic/infection specialist
says otherwise — do not aspirate a prosthetic joint in the general ward without specialist input
(different technique, different threshold, risk of seeding a previously sterile prosthesis). In
children specifically, use the **Kocher criteria** to help discriminate septic arthritis from
transient synovitis of the hip (see §5) — but treat it as a decision-support tool, not a rule that
overrides strong clinical concern.

### 2.10 Joint dislocation — reduction, post-reduction films, and the neurovascular re-check

**Pattern spectrum by joint (each with its own reduction urgency and nerve/vessel at risk):**
- **Shoulder (glenohumeral) dislocation** — **anterior** (~95% of cases, arm held abducted/
  externally rotated, mechanism usually a fall on an outstretched/abducted-externally-rotated arm
  or direct blow) — at risk: **axillary nerve** (test regimental-badge sensation and deltoid
  contraction — do this *before* reduction), less commonly the axillary artery in the elderly with
  atherosclerotic vessels. **Posterior** dislocation — much rarer, classically associated with
  seizures or electric shock, easily missed on a single AP shoulder film (the "lightbulb sign" on
  AP, and a true axillary or scapular-Y lateral view is needed to confirm) — a notorious source of
  delayed diagnosis.
- **Hip dislocation** — usually **posterior** (~90%, classic mechanism a dashboard injury in an MVC
  — knee striking the dashboard with the hip flexed/adducted), leg held shortened, flexed,
  internally rotated, adducted. **This is a true orthopaedic emergency — reduce within 6 hours to
  minimise avascular necrosis risk to the femoral head**, whose blood supply is tenuous and further
  compromised by the duration of dislocation. At risk: **sciatic nerve** (test before and after
  reduction — foot drop/peroneal division most commonly affected). Always assess for an associated
  acetabular fracture or femoral head fracture (post-reduction CT is frequently indicated to assess
  for this and for intra-articular loose fragments).
- **Knee dislocation** — see §2.3; the popliteal artery is the dominant concern and every
  presentation, spontaneously reduced or not, needs vascular surveillance.
- **Elbow dislocation** — usually posterior/posterolateral, a fall on an outstretched hand with the
  elbow extended; at risk: brachial artery, median and ulnar nerves — assess and document each.
- **Finger/patella dislocations** — lower acuity but still need pre/post neurovascular
  documentation and, for patella, assessment of the medial patellofemoral ligament and
  osteochondral fragment risk.

**Discriminating history.** Mechanism (predicts direction and associated structures at risk — see
above). Time since dislocation (drives urgency, particularly for the hip). First-time vs recurrent
(recurrent shoulder dislocation in a young patient raises the question of underlying instability/
labral pathology and a different long-term management conversation, but the acute reduction
principles are identical). Any neurological symptom noticed by the patient before you even touch
them.

**Discriminating exam.** The deformity is often visually diagnostic (squared-off shoulder with a
palpable void inferior to the acromion for anterior shoulder dislocation; shortened/flexed/
internally-rotated hip). **Neurovascular exam of the specific nerve(s)/vessel(s) at risk for that
joint, performed and documented BEFORE any reduction attempt** — this cannot be overstated; without
a pre-reduction baseline, any post-reduction deficit is medico-legally ambiguous.

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** Pre-reduction X-ray to confirm the direction of dislocation and
exclude an associated fracture that might change the reduction technique (e.g. a large associated
fracture may need reduction in theatre rather than a ward/ED manipulation) — **but do not delay
reduction for imaging if the limb is neurovascularly compromised; clinical reduction takes priority
in that scenario, exactly as for §2.3.** **Mandatory post-reduction X-ray** in every case to confirm
anatomical reduction and detect any fracture caused by or revealed by the reduction manoeuvre. CT
post-reduction where an associated fracture (acetabular, glenoid rim, tibial plateau) needs
detailed characterisation for surgical planning.

**Management anchors.** Adequate analgesia/sedation (procedural sedation, intra-articular local
anaesthetic, regional block, or general anaesthetic depending on the joint and setting) before
attempting reduction — a tense, guarding, unsedated patient makes reduction harder and more
traumatic to surrounding tissue. Use a recognised reduction technique for the joint (e.g. traction-
countertraction or scapular manipulation for anterior shoulder; standard Allis/Bigelow-type
manoeuvres for posterior hip). **Immediately after reduction: repeat the neurovascular exam,
obtain the post-reduction X-ray, and splint/immobilise in a position of stability** (e.g. a sling
and swathe for shoulder, in a position that does not re-provoke instability). Definitive management
depends on associated injury — an isolated, cleanly-reduced dislocation with no fracture may need
only a period of immobilisation and physiotherapy; a dislocation with a significant associated
fracture, an irreducible dislocation, or one with ongoing neurovascular compromise post-reduction
needs urgent orthopaedic/surgical involvement.

**Traps & pearls.** **Posterior shoulder dislocation is missed on a single AP view — always get an
axillary or scapular-Y view if there is any clinical suspicion** (seizure/electric-shock mechanism,
arm held adducted and internally rotated rather than the classic anterior posture). **A posterior
hip dislocation is a race against avascular necrosis** — do not let it wait behind "less urgent"
looking injuries on a busy trauma list; escalate it. Never attempt a joint reduction without
documenting the pre-reduction neurovascular exam first, and never consider the job finished until
the post-reduction X-ray and neurovascular re-check are both done and documented — an unconfirmed
reduction is not a completed reduction.

### 2.11 Cauda equina syndrome (and the broader red-flag back-pain differential)

**Differential of acute/severe back pain — three tiers:**
- **Common:** mechanical/musculoligamentous back pain, simple disc prolapse with radiculopathy
  (single nerve root — dermatomal pain/sensory change, myotomal weakness, reduced reflex — but
  bladder/bowel/perineal function intact), facet joint pain, muscle spasm.
- **Dangerous / must-not-miss:** **cauda equina syndrome (CES)** — compression of the lumbosacral
  nerve roots below the level of the spinal cord (typically from a large central disc prolapse, but
  also epidural abscess, epidural haematoma, tumour, or a fracture fragment), **spinal epidural
  abscess** (fever + back pain + escalating neurology — a genuine emergency in its own right and a
  cause of CES), **vertebral fracture with cord/cauda compromise**, **spinal metastatic disease
  with cord compression** (known or occult malignancy, night pain, weight loss), **discitis/
  vertebral osteomyelitis** (fever, severe localised pain, raised inflammatory markers).
- **Zebras/consultant-considers (and SA-critical):** **TB spine (Pott's disease)** — see §2.14,
  a leading cause of non-traumatic spinal cord compression in the SA TB/HIV-endemic setting,
  presenting subacutely rather than as a classic hyperacute CES but capable of producing the same
  end-organ neurological emergency; **spinal epidural haematoma** (anticoagulated patient,
  post-procedural — e.g. after spinal/epidural anaesthesia); **conus medullaris syndrome** (a
  closely related but anatomically distinct entity — upper motor neuron signs may be present, more
  symmetrical, earlier bladder involvement — the clinical distinction from CES is often academic in
  the acute setting: both need the same urgency of imaging and decompression).

**Discriminating history — the red flags that must be actively asked about, not waited for
spontaneously**: **bilateral leg pain/sciatica** (unilateral radiculopathy alone is common and
usually benign; bilateral involvement raises concern), **saddle (perineal, groin, inner thigh)
numbness** — the single highest-value red-flag symptom [grounded], **new bladder dysfunction**
(urinary retention is the most reliable objective sign, though early presentation may be urinary
*hesitancy* or loss of sensation of bladder fullness rather than frank retention — ask specifically,
patients often don't volunteer this), **bowel incontinence or loss of anal sensation/tone**, **any
new lower-limb weakness**, **sexual dysfunction/loss of genital sensation** (frequently omitted from
history-taking but important — ask directly). Fever/night sweats/weight loss (infective/malignant
cause). Known malignancy. Trauma mechanism. Anticoagulation or recent spinal procedure (haematoma).

**Discriminating exam.** Full lower-limb neurological exam (power, sensation by dermatome, reflexes,
plantar response) — bilateral. **Perianal (saddle) sensation testing** — explicitly, with the
patient's consent and explanation, testing light touch in the perianal/perineal distribution; this
is frequently the exam step junior clinicians skip out of discomfort, and it is the one that
matters most. **Digital rectal exam for anal tone** (reduced/absent tone is a significant finding)
and to assess for a palpable mass/other pathology. Post-void residual bladder volume (bladder scan
or catheter) if retention is suspected — an elevated post-void residual is objective evidence
supporting the diagnosis even before formal urinary retention develops. Gait, straight-leg-raise
(bilateral positive raises concern).

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** **MRI whole spine is the single most reliable and essential
investigation and should not be delayed** — plain X-rays and CT have very limited value for soft
tissue/cord compression (CT is useful only if MRI is genuinely unavailable, or to characterise a
bony fragment/fracture pattern) [grounded]. **The imaging must happen the same day the red flags
are recognised — this is one of the true "MRI now, not on the routine list" indications in
medicine**, because neurological recovery is time-and-compression-duration dependent. FBC/CRP/ESR
(raised in infective/malignant causes — a normal set does not exclude a mechanical/compressive
cause but should raise suspicion of abscess/discitis/TB if elevated with a compatible story). Blood
cultures if infective cause suspected. PSA/myeloma screen/tumour markers if malignancy suspected and
not previously known. In the SA context, a TB work-up (see §2.14) is a standard parallel
consideration when the presentation is subacute rather than hyperacute-traumatic.

**Management anchors.** **Urgent same-day MRI, urgent neurosurgical/orthopaedic-spine referral, and
surgical decompression as soon as feasible** — the literature and consultant consensus support
decompression as early as possible, with the practical understanding used in most units that
**outcomes are best with decompression within roughly 24–48 hours of red-flag onset**, and that
delay directly correlates with worse and more permanent neurological deficit, particularly bladder/
bowel/sexual function (which recovers least reliably of all the affected domains) [EK/grounded].
Catheterise if in retention. Treat the underlying cause in parallel — antibiotics for
abscess/discitis (after cultures, ideally, unless the patient is septic), steroids and oncology
referral for malignant cord compression (dexamethasone is a standard bridging measure while
definitive imaging/oncological management is arranged), TB treatment for Pott's-related
compression (§2.14), reversal of anticoagulation for epidural haematoma. In SA settings where MRI
access is delayed (a real resource constraint, especially at district level — see §6), the correct
action is **urgent transfer to a centre with same-day MRI and neurosurgical/spinal capacity**, not
a "wait and re-examine" strategy — this diagnosis does not tolerate observation as a first-line
approach once red flags are present.

**Traps & pearls.** **Saddle numbness and bladder symptoms are actively elicited, not passively
waited for** — many CES presentations are missed because the clinician asks "any weakness?" and
stops there without asking specifically about perineal sensation and bladder function. **A patient
who can still walk and has normal power can still have cauda equina syndrome** — motor/sensory
findings in the legs are not required for the diagnosis; sphincter/saddle involvement is the
defining feature and can occur with a relatively unimpressive limb exam. **Do not defer the MRI to
"the next routine slot"** — this is one of the few true same-day-imaging emergencies in
musculoskeletal medicine, on par with an open fracture or compartment syndrome in its time
sensitivity, even though the mechanism (compression, not ischaemia) is different. Document the
perineal/bladder exam explicitly in every "just a backache" presentation with any red-flag feature
— the medico-legal exposure of a missed CES is severe and the single most common reason this
diagnosis generates litigation is a failure to document that the sphincter/saddle exam was even
performed.

### 2.12 Traumatic spinal cord injury

**Pattern spectrum.** **Complete vs incomplete injury** — defined functionally by the **ASIA/
ISNCSCI Impairment Scale** (see §5), assessed properly only once **spinal shock has resolved**
(the bulbocavernosus reflex returning signals the end of spinal shock, typically within 24–48
hours — a "complete" injury assessed during spinal shock may in fact be incomplete once spinal
shock resolves, so **early ASIA grading should be treated as provisional**, not final). **Incomplete
cord syndromes** with distinct patterns [EK, standard neuro-anatomy]: **central cord syndrome**
(commonest — hyperextension injury in an older patient with pre-existing cervical spondylosis,
disproportionately greater weakness in the upper limbs than the lower limbs); **anterior cord
syndrome** (loss of motor/pain/temperature below the lesion with preserved proprioception/vibration
— worst prognosis of the incomplete syndromes, often from anterior spinal artery compromise);
**Brown-Séquard syndrome** (hemisection — ipsilateral motor/proprioceptive loss, contralateral
pain/temperature loss, classically from a penetrating injury — stab wound — highly relevant given
the SA penetrating-trauma burden); **posterior cord syndrome** (rare, proprioceptive loss
predominates).

**Discriminating history.** Mechanism (axial load/diving injury, hyperextension in the elderly with
pre-existing cervical canal narrowing, penetrating stab/gunshot to the back/neck, fall from height,
high-speed MVC). Any transient or persisting neurological symptom at the scene (numbness, weakness,
inability to move a limb, even if it seems to have resolved by arrival — transient deficits still
mandate full spinal imaging and precaution). Neck/back pain, priapism (a recognised sign of
significant cord injury).

**Discriminating exam.** **Full ASIA/ISNCSCI motor and sensory examination** (see §5) — key muscle
groups bilaterally, light touch and pinprick by dermatome, and the **sacral-sparing exam**
(perianal sensation, voluntary anal contraction, deep anal pressure sensation) — **sacral sparing
present means the injury is, by definition, incomplete, regardless of how little motor/sensory
function is preserved elsewhere, and carries a materially better prognosis.** Assess for **spinal
shock** (flaccid paralysis, absent reflexes below the level, including an absent bulbocavernosus
reflex) versus **neurogenic shock** (hypotension with paradoxical bradycardia — a distinct
haemodynamic entity from spinal shock, caused by loss of sympathetic tone below a high — typically
cervical/high-thoracic — injury, and easily confused with haemorrhagic shock, which instead causes
tachycardia; **treating "neurogenic shock" with aggressive fluid resuscitation alone, mistaking it
for haemorrhage, risks fluid overload — vasopressor support is often needed to restore sympathetic
tone**). Full secondary survey for a second, non-contiguous spinal injury (present in a meaningful
minority of significant trauma — always image the whole spine, not just the level of obvious
injury) and for associated injuries.

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** **CT spine** is the primary imaging modality in trauma (fast,
widely available, excellent for bony injury) — apply validated clinical decision rules (NEXUS,
Canadian C-spine — referenced in clinical-foundations.md) to decide who needs imaging at all in
lower-risk mechanisms, but image liberally in any high-energy mechanism or any neurological symptom.
**MRI** is added when there is a neurological deficit not fully explained by the CT bony findings,
to assess the cord itself, ligamentous injury, disc herniation, epidural haematoma, or cord oedema/
haemorrhage — again a resource-access question in SA district settings, warranting urgent transfer
where local MRI access is delayed, in the same way as for cauda equina syndrome (§2.11).

**Management anchors.** **Spinal immobilisation from the point of injury** (though modern practice
has moved away from prolonged rigid backboard use once the patient is off the board and log-rolled
onto a suitable surface, to reduce pressure-sore risk — immobilise the *spine*, not necessarily
with a rigid board for hours). **Maintain mean arterial pressure (typically targeted around
85–90 mmHg for the first several days) to optimise spinal cord perfusion** in the acute phase of a
significant cord injury — this is standard spinal-injury critical-care practice, using
vasopressors as needed rather than fluid alone, particularly relevant given the neurogenic-shock
haemodynamic pattern above. Urgent spinal surgical referral for decompression/stabilisation where
indicated (unstable fracture pattern, progressive neurological deficit, cord compression on
imaging) — timing follows similar urgency logic to cauda equina syndrome, earlier decompression
associated with better neurological recovery potential, though the evidence base and practical
access constraints (theatre, spinal-surgical capacity) shape real-world timing in SA more than in
well-resourced systems. Catheterise (neurogenic bladder), VTE prophylaxis (very high-risk group —
immobility plus, once past the acute bleeding-risk window, standard consideration), pressure-area
care (a paralysed patient cannot protect their own skin — turn regularly, from the first hour),
bowel regimen, temperature regulation (autonomic dysfunction impairs thermoregulation), and early
rehabilitation-team involvement.

**Traps & pearls.** **Do not grade a spinal cord injury as "complete" during the spinal-shock
period** — wait for spinal shock to resolve (bulbocavernosus reflex return) before finalising the
ASIA grade and prognosis conversation, and re-examine. **Distinguish neurogenic shock from
haemorrhagic shock by the heart rate** — a hypotensive trauma patient who is *bradycardic* rather
than tachycardic, with a plausible high spinal injury, has neurogenic shock; do not chase this with
unlimited crystalloid while missing an occult second haemorrhage source, and involve
vasopressor support early. Always examine and image the **whole spine**, not just the obviously
injured level. **Sacral sparing changes everything** — actively test perianal sensation and
voluntary anal tone in every cord-injury assessment, it is the single finding most likely to be
skipped under time pressure and the one with the greatest prognostic weight.

### 2.13 The diabetic foot and osteomyelitis

**Differential of the red, swollen, or ulcerated diabetic foot — three tiers:**
- **Common:** simple cellulitis (superficial, no ulcer/wound, responds to oral antibiotics),
  uninfected neuropathic ulcer (a wound with no clinical signs of infection — erythema, warmth,
  purulence, pain — despite tissue breakdown; infection is a clinical diagnosis, not implied by the
  ulcer's mere presence), tinea pedis/fissuring as a portal of entry.
- **Dangerous / must-not-miss:** **diabetic foot osteomyelitis** (bone infection underlying an
  ulcer — the presence of which fundamentally changes the treatment duration and often the surgical
  decision), **necrotising soft-tissue infection** (rapidly progressive, disproportionate pain,
  crepitus, skin discolouration/bullae, systemic toxicity — a surgical emergency, source control by
  debridement is the treatment, not antibiotics alone), **critical limb ischaemia** with
  superimposed infection (a foot that is both ischaemic and infected has a dramatically worse
  prognosis and a much lower threshold for major amputation if source control fails), **Charcot
  neuroarthropathy in its acute phase** (a hot, swollen, sometimes erythematous foot in a
  neuropathic diabetic that is frequently misdiagnosed as cellulitis or osteomyelitis — the
  discriminator is the absence of a skin breach/ulcer as a portal and a different natural history;
  when in doubt in an ulcer-free hot foot, Charcot must be actively considered, not defaulted past).
- **Zebras/consultant-considers:** gout in a diabetic foot (mimics cellulitis/sepsis), gas
  gangrene/clostridial myonecrosis (rapid, gas on X-ray, profound toxicity), TB osteomyelitis of the
  foot (uncommon but described in the SA TB-endemic context), atypical mycobacterial infection in
  the immunosuppressed.

**Discriminating history.** Duration of the ulcer/wound, prior episodes of foot infection/
osteomyelitis/amputation (a strong predictor of recurrence and of the causative organism profile),
glycaemic control (HbA1c), peripheral neuropathy symptoms (numbness — often the ulcer is painless,
which is itself diagnostically important: a painless, deep ulcer in a diabetic is neuropathic until
proven otherwise, and the absence of pain does not mean the absence of severe infection), peripheral
vascular disease symptoms (claudication, rest pain), smoking, renal function/dialysis status, HIV
status (an additional risk factor for infection severity and atypical organisms in the SA context),
recent trauma/foreign body/self-treatment (traditional remedies, home debridement).

**Discriminating exam.** **Probe-to-bone test** — probing the ulcer base with a sterile blunt
instrument; **contact with hard, gritty bone is a strong predictor of underlying osteomyelitis**
(reported positive predictive value in the 90%+ range in surgical series when the ulcer is deep and
the clinical suspicion is already at least moderate — though its accuracy is context/population-
dependent and it performs best, not in isolation, but combined with the overall clinical picture)
[grounded]. Assess **depth, size, base tissue (granulating vs necrotic vs exposed bone/tendon),
surrounding erythema/warmth/induration, purulent discharge, malodour**, and **systemic signs**
(fever, tachycardia — often blunted or absent in the diabetic due to autonomic neuropathy, so a
"normal" heart rate does not exclude severe infection). **Vascular exam** — pulses (dorsalis pedis,
posterior tibial), capillary refill, ankle-brachial index if pulses are diminished/absent (though
ABI can be falsely elevated by calcified, non-compressible vessels in longstanding diabetes —
toe-brachial index or formal vascular referral is more reliable in that scenario). **Neurological
exam** — 10 g monofilament testing, vibration sense (loss of protective sensation is the mechanism
underlying most diabetic foot ulcers). Classify infection severity clinically (mild — localised,
<2 cm surrounding erythema; moderate — more extensive local infection, or involving deeper
structures, without systemic toxicity; severe — with systemic inflammatory response/sepsis or
limb-threatening features) — this severity grading, not the ulcer appearance alone, drives the
inpatient-vs-outpatient and IV-vs-oral antibiotic decision.

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** **Plain X-ray first-line** — looks for cortical erosion, periosteal
reaction, bone destruction consistent with osteomyelitis, but has **limited sensitivity early**
(changes may take 2 weeks or more to appear on plain film) and can be difficult to distinguish from
Charcot changes. **MRI is the most sensitive and specific imaging modality for diabetic foot
osteomyelitis** when available, and is also the best modality to distinguish infection from Charcot
neuroarthropathy — but is frequently a delayed/limited-access investigation in SA state facilities
(see §6), so the clinical probe-to-bone test and plain film combination often has to carry more
diagnostic weight in practice than in a resource-rich setting. **Bone biopsy (culture and
histology) is the gold standard** for confirming osteomyelitis and, critically, for **directing
targeted antibiotic therapy rather than broad empirical cover for weeks** — pursue it whenever
feasible, especially before committing a patient to a prolonged antibiotic course. **Inflammatory
markers (WCC, CRP, ESR)** — a very high ESR (some series use >70 mm/h as a supportive threshold) in
the presence of a positive probe-to-bone test strengthens the diagnosis; trend CRP as a treatment-
response marker. **Blood cultures** if systemically unwell.

**Management anchors.** **Source control first, exactly as for any surgical infection** — surgical
debridement of infected/necrotic tissue and, where osteomyelitis is confirmed and the bone burden
is resectable, surgical resection of infected bone is often more effective and shortens antibiotic
duration compared to antibiotics alone; a purely medical approach (prolonged antibiotics without
debridement) is reserved for selected cases where surgery is not feasible/appropriate or the
disease is mild and well-localised. **Empirical antibiotics** targeted at the likely organism
profile — *S. aureus* remains dominant, but diabetic foot infections are frequently polymicrobial
(including Gram-negatives and anaerobes, especially in deep, chronic, or previously-treated
wounds) — broad empirical cover (e.g. amoxicillin-clavulanate for mild-moderate, escalating to
piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem plus consideration of MRSA cover for severe/limb-
threatening infection or a patient with prior resistant-organism isolates) [EK, apply SA STG
antibiotic-class equivalents locally], **narrowed to culture-directed therapy once bone/deep-tissue
culture results are available.** **Glycaemic control optimisation** is itself part of the
treatment, not a side issue — poor control impairs neutrophil function and wound healing.
**Offloading** (total contact casting, offloading footwear) is essential for ulcer healing and is
frequently under-prioritised relative to antibiotics. **Vascular assessment and revascularisation**
where significant peripheral arterial disease coexists — an infected ischaemic foot will not heal
on antibiotics/debridement alone if the blood supply is inadequate; involve vascular surgery early.
**Amputation** (minor, ray, or major) when source control cannot otherwise be achieved, when the
limb is non-salvageable, or when the patient's overall condition (sepsis, unreconstructable
ischaemia) makes limb salvage unsafe — this is a difficult, often late, but sometimes life-saving
decision, and the threshold discussion should involve the patient, not default silently to "keep
trying antibiotics."

**Traps & pearls.** **A painless deep ulcer is not a reassuring finding — it reflects neuropathy, not
mild disease**, and can conceal severe underlying infection; do not let the absence of pain lower
your suspicion. **A "normal" heart rate/temperature does not exclude severe diabetic foot sepsis** —
autonomic neuropathy blunts the expected tachycardic/febrile response; trust the local wound signs
and inflammatory markers over vital-sign reassurance. **Charcot neuroarthropathy is misdiagnosed as
osteomyelitis (and vice versa) more often than either textbook suggests** — a hot, swollen,
ulcer-free neuropathic foot deserves active consideration of Charcot, and imaging/specialist input
before committing to a prolonged antibiotic course intended for bone infection that may not be
present. Antibiotics alone, without debridement/source control, frequently fail — this is the same
surgical-sepsis principle as everywhere else in this dossier (and in `surgery.md`): **source control
is the treatment, antibiotics are the adjunct.**

### 2.14 TB spine (Pott's disease) and TB of joints

**Differential/pattern.** TB spine (Pott's disease) is the commonest site of osseous TB and a
disease every SA orthopaedic clinician must actively consider in the differential of **subacute
back pain with constitutional symptoms, progressive kyphotic deformity, a paravertebral/psoas
"cold" abscess, and — critically — progressive neurological deficit without a traumatic
mechanism.** TB joint disease (most often a large, weight-bearing joint — hip, knee) presents as a
**monoarthritis that is subacute rather than the florid, rapidly destructive picture of pyogenic
septic arthritis** — less pain and less systemic upset than the pyogenic pattern for a comparable
degree of joint destruction, which is exactly what makes it dangerously easy to under-triage.
Differential to actively exclude: pyogenic osteomyelitis/septic arthritis (faster tempo, more
systemic toxicity — though HIV co-infection can blur this distinction), malignancy (vertebral
metastasis, myeloma, primary bone tumour — the constitutional-symptom overlap with TB is
substantial and both must be actively worked up, not assumed), pyogenic discitis/vertebral
osteomyelitis, brucellosis (uncommon in SA but a recognised granulomatous-spine mimic).

**Discriminating history.** Chronic/subacute back or joint pain (weeks to months, not days), night
sweats, weight loss, low-grade fever, known TB contact or personal TB history, HIV status (TB spine
and TB joint disease occur across the HIV spectrum but the differential of a "cold abscess" and
subacute bone/joint disease is weighted heavily toward TB in a TB/HIV-endemic population), and —
the finding that should trigger urgent escalation regardless of how subacute the rest of the story
seems — any **new lower-limb weakness, sensory change, or bladder/bowel symptom**, because Pott's
disease is a recognised, relatively common (in this setting) cause of non-traumatic spinal cord
compression and can evolve into a genuine cauda-equina/cord-compression emergency (§2.11/§2.12)
even though the underlying disease process is chronic.

**Discriminating exam.** Localised spinal tenderness, a visible/palpable kyphotic deformity (a
gibbus — angular kyphosis — is a classic late sign of vertebral body collapse), a palpable
paraspinal or groin mass (a psoas abscess tracking along the psoas sheath, sometimes presenting
purely as a groin swelling or with pain on hip extension, mimicking an entirely different
diagnosis if the spine is not examined). Full neurological exam of the lower limbs (as for §2.11/
§2.12 — the same red-flag discipline applies, just triggered by a subacute rather than acute
story). For joint disease — a swollen, sometimes only mildly warm and mildly painful joint with a
disproportionately large effusion and, over time, progressive joint destruction/deformity if
untreated; regional lymphadenopathy and a low-grade, low-key systemic unwellness rather than the
high fever of pyogenic sepsis.

**Ix + interpretation nuance.** **Plain X-ray of the spine/joint** — classically shows **disc-space
narrowing with adjacent vertebral body destruction spanning two contiguous vertebrae** (TB
characteristically crosses the disc space, unlike most malignant processes which tend to spare the
disc and involve a single vertebral body or posterior elements — a genuinely useful discriminator),
progressive kyphotic collapse, and paraspinal soft-tissue shadow (abscess). **MRI is the best
modality** for early diagnosis, extent of cord/nerve root compression, and abscess mapping — again
frequently a delayed/limited-access test at district level in SA, so image liberally and escalate/
transfer promptly whenever the neurological picture demands it rather than waiting on routine MRI
turnaround. **Microbiological/histological confirmation** — CT-guided or open biopsy for
histopathology (caseating granuloma) and **Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra** on aspirate/biopsy tissue (the
SA-standard rapid TB test, also detecting rifampicin resistance), plus mycobacterial culture (slower
but more sensitive and provides full drug-susceptibility data) — grounded consistent with SA
national TB diagnostic practice used throughout medicine/surgery in this hospital system. **ESR**
often markedly elevated (a useful nonspecific supportive marker and, later, a treatment-response
trend to follow). HIV test in every case (near-universal practice in the SA TB pathway, and directly
relevant to ART-timing decisions if positive). Aspiration of a psoas/paraspinal abscess, where
performed, for the same Xpert/culture workup.

**Management anchors.** **Anti-TB chemotherapy is the primary treatment for the great majority of
cases** — standard first-line regimen per national TB guidelines (the same 2HRZE/4HR-era
fixed-dose-combination backbone used throughout SA TB practice, extended-duration courses are
often used for osteoarticular/spinal TB by specialist protocol, typically longer than pulmonary TB
courses), with pyridoxine co-administration and the same drug-resistance vigilance (Xpert
rifampicin-resistance result) as any other TB diagnosis in this system. **Bracing** for spinal
stability/pain relief and to support deformity correction during treatment in appropriate cases.
**Surgery is reserved for specific indications**: significant/progressive neurological deficit,
significant or progressive kyphotic deformity/spinal instability, failure of medical management, or
a large abscess needing drainage — **not** as first-line treatment for uncomplicated Pott's disease
without neurological compromise, which usually responds well to chemotherapy alone. **Any
neurological deficit or evolving cord compression escalates this to the same urgent-imaging,
urgent-surgical-referral pathway as §2.11/§2.12**, regardless of the fundamentally chronic nature of
the underlying disease — the deficit itself is time-critical even when the disease process is not.
For TB joint disease, similarly — anti-TB chemotherapy is primary, with surgical
debridement/synovectomy or arthrodesis reserved for advanced destruction, abscess, or diagnostic
uncertainty.

**Traps & pearls.** **TB spine is easy to under-triage because it looks "less sick" than pyogenic
disease at a comparable stage of bone/joint destruction** — a patient who is not septic, not
high-fever, and has had symptoms for months can nonetheless have significant vertebral collapse and
be one further step of progression from a cord-compression emergency; do not let the subacute tempo
translate into a subacute sense of urgency once red-flag neurology appears. **Disc-space involvement
crossing two vertebrae favours TB/pyogenic infection over malignancy**, which more often spares the
disc — a useful, genuinely discriminating radiological clue worth teaching explicitly. **A groin
mass from a tracking psoas abscess can present entirely divorced from any back complaint** — examine
the spine in any unexplained groin/iliac fossa mass, especially in a TB/HIV-endemic population.
**HIV co-infection changes the pace and severity of presentation** — a more rapidly progressive or
atypical picture in an HIV-positive patient should not be assumed to be purely pyogenic just because
it "looks too aggressive for TB"; TB in advanced HIV disease can be disseminated and fulminant.
Confirm microbiologically wherever feasible before committing to months of anti-TB therapy — the
consequences of treating a missed malignancy as TB, or vice versa, are severe.

## 3. The normals — what "normal" looks like on this ward

**Neurovascular exam — the baseline that makes deterioration recognisable** [EK]: pulses **2+**
(normal), palpable and symmetrical to the contralateral limb; capillary refill **<2 seconds**;
sensation intact to light touch and pinprick in all major nerve distributions distal to the injury;
motor power **5/5** in all major muscle groups tested (graded on the standard **MRC 0–5 scale**:
0 = no contraction, 1 = flicker/trace, 2 = movement with gravity eliminated, 3 = movement against
gravity, 4 = movement against some resistance, 5 = normal power); compartments soft and
non-tender on palpation, no pain on passive stretch of the muscles within.

**The fracture-description language — the universal grammar, used identically on every X-ray
report and every ward-round note** [EK]:
- **Site** — which bone, and which part (proximal/middle/distal third, metaphyseal/diaphyseal/
  epiphyseal, intra- vs extra-articular).
- **Pattern** — transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted (more than two fragments), segmental
  (two fracture lines isolating a segment of bone), greenstick/torus (paediatric incomplete
  patterns), avulsion.
- **Displacement** — described as the percentage of shaft width the distal fragment has shifted
  relative to the proximal fragment, and the direction (e.g. "50% dorsally displaced").
- **Angulation** — the degrees and direction the distal fragment is tilted relative to the
  proximal fragment's long axis (conventionally described by the direction the fracture apex
  points, or by the direction the distal fragment has angulated — be explicit and consistent).
- **Rotation** — assessed clinically (comparing the rotational alignment of the digits/limb to the
  uninjured side) more reliably than radiographically.
- **Shortening** — measured in cm/mm of overlap.
- **Intra-articular extension** — does the fracture line cross into a joint surface; if so, describe
  the **step-off/gap** in mm, because intra-articular incongruity above a small threshold (commonly
  cited around 2 mm, though this varies by joint) meaningfully worsens post-traumatic arthritis risk
  and often changes the management threshold toward surgical fixation.

**Post-reduction alignment — what "acceptable" looks like** [EK]: the goal after any manipulation
is anatomical or near-anatomical restoration of length, alignment, and rotation, confirmed on
orthogonal (AP + lateral, at minimum) post-reduction films, with **any rotational deformity
considered unacceptable** (rotation does not remodel with growth/healing the way angulation can,
particularly in adults) and the acceptable degree of residual angulation/displacement varying by
bone, joint proximity, and patient age (children remodel angulation substantially, especially near
a growth plate and in the plane of joint motion; adults do not).

**Compartments** [EK]: normal resting intracompartmental pressure is low (roughly 0–10 mmHg);
concerning/diagnostic thresholds are covered in §2.2/§4 — the "normal" to know here is simply that a
compartment should be soft, non-tender, and pain-free on passive muscle stretch.

**Vitals in the trauma/ortho patient** [EK]: as for the general surgical/medical normal ranges
(HR 60–100, RR 12–20, SBP >90–100, GCS 15, urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/h) — deviation in a trauma
patient should first be interpreted as **occult haemorrhage until excluded**, given how much blood
volume a closed long-bone or pelvic fracture can hide (§1, §2.8).

## 4. Cross-cutting investigation interpretation (deep)

### 4.1 Reading an orthopaedic/trauma X-ray systematically
A structured, repeatable approach, applied to every film rather than "eyeballing the fracture"
[EK]: **(1) Check the patient/date/adequacy** — correct patient, correct side marked, two
orthogonal views (AP + lateral at minimum; oblique views for complex joints), joint above and
below the injury included. **(2) Soft tissues** — swelling, joint effusion (a **posterior fat pad
sign** at the elbow, a **lipohaemarthrosis** — a fat-fluid level — at the knee on a horizontal-beam
lateral, both imply an occult intra-articular fracture even when no fracture line is visible).
**(3) Bones** — trace every cortex circumferentially, looking specifically for a step, a buckle, a
lucent line, or a subtle sclerotic impaction line (the latter easily missed — e.g. a valgus-
impacted femoral neck fracture). **(4) Joints** — congruity, alignment, joint-space width,
symmetry left-to-right where paired films are taken. **(5) Compare with the contralateral side**
in any equivocal paediatric or subtle adult injury — a genuinely useful and underused technique.
**(6) Re-examine the whole film, not just the area of clinical concern** — a second, unsuspected
fracture (calcaneal + spine; distal radius + elbow; one long bone + the ipsilateral hip/pelvis) is
a classic miss when attention narrows to the obviously painful site.

### 4.2 When CT adds value over plain film
CT is added, not as a routine reflex, but specifically when: **(a) surgical planning requires 3D
fracture-fragment detail** (intra-articular fractures — tibial plateau, pilon, calcaneus,
acetabulum, complex pelvis); **(b) plain films are equivocal but clinical suspicion remains high**
(occult hip/scaphoid/tibial-plateau fracture); **(c) associated soft-tissue/vascular injury needs
characterisation** (CT angiography for the hard/soft-sign vascular exam, §2.3); **(d) spinal
trauma** (CT is the primary modality, faster and more available than MRI, excellent for bony
injury, but blind to pure ligamentous/cord-signal injury); **(e) pelvic fracture-pattern and
active-bleed localisation** (contrast blush, §2.8).

### 4.3 When MRI adds value over CT
MRI is reserved for questions CT cannot answer: **(a) soft-tissue and ligamentous injury**
(cruciate/collateral ligaments, menisci, rotator cuff, labrum); **(b) cord/nerve-root compression
and cord signal change** (cauda equina §2.11, spinal cord injury §2.12, TB spine §2.14); **(c) early
osteomyelitis/septic arthritis** where plain film is still normal and MRI can show marrow oedema
and abscess before cortical destruction is visible; **(d) distinguishing infection from Charcot
neuroarthropathy** in the diabetic foot (§2.13); **(e) occult fracture** not seen on plain film or CT
(especially trabecular/stress patterns). **The SA reality**: MRI access is frequently the rate-
limiting, delayed investigation at district/regional level — the correct response to a genuine MRI-
requiring emergency (cauda equina, suspected cord compression, a septic hip not settled by
aspiration alone) is urgent escalation/transfer to a centre with same-day capacity, not deferral to
a routine outpatient slot.

### 4.4 Inflammatory markers in bone/joint sepsis — reading the trend, not the single value
**CRP** rises within hours of a septic insult and falls relatively quickly with successful source
control (days) — useful for **early treatment-response monitoring**. **ESR** rises and falls more
slowly (over days to weeks in each direction) — less useful for early monitoring but a reasonable
longer-term trend marker, and classically very high in TB and in some chronic/subacute bone
infections. **WCC** is the least reliable of the three in this context — it can be normal in
significant bone/joint sepsis (especially early, in the elderly, or in the immunosuppressed) and
should never be used in isolation to exclude septic arthritis or osteomyelitis. **The clinically
useful pattern**: a normal or near-normal CRP/ESR at presentation does not exclude infection early;
a **falling CRP over 48–72 hours after source control (aspiration/washout/debridement) plus
appropriate antibiotics** is the expected and reassuring trajectory, and a **CRP that plateaus or
rises despite treatment** should trigger re-imaging/re-aspiration for inadequate source control or
a resistant/undertreated organism, not simply a change of antibiotic in isolation.

### 4.5 Compartment-pressure numbers, precisely
**Absolute intracompartmental pressure >30 mmHg** is a widely used but physiologically crude
threshold. **Delta pressure (ΔP) = diastolic blood pressure − measured compartment pressure; ΔP
≤30 mmHg is the more physiologically sound indication for fasciotomy**, because it accounts for the
patient's actual perfusion pressure rather than an arbitrary fixed number [grounded — §2.2]. In
practice: measure at the level of the fracture/injury (pressure varies by location within a limb),
measure more than one compartment where clinically indicated (a leg has four compartments —
anterior, lateral, superficial posterior, deep posterior — and pressure in one does not reliably
predict another), and **treat serial clinical examination as at least as important as a single
pressure reading** — a rising trend on repeated measurement, or a worsening clinical exam despite a
single "borderline" pressure reading, still warrants fasciotomy.

## 5. Scores, charts, and structured tools

**Gustilo–Anderson open fracture classification** (finalised intraoperatively after debridement,
not at first casualty assessment): **I** wound <1 cm, minimal contamination/soft-tissue damage;
**II** wound 1–10 cm, moderate soft-tissue damage; **III** wound >10 cm OR high-energy/segmental/
farmyard-contaminated OR any fracture needing flap coverage regardless of wound size —
**IIIA** adequate soft-tissue coverage achievable, **IIIB** extensive soft-tissue loss needing flap
coverage, **IIIC** any open fracture with an arterial injury requiring repair. [grounded, §2.1]

**Ottawa Ankle Rule** (imaging decision in acute ankle injury) — X-ray indicated if: **pain in the
malleolar zone AND** any one of — bone tenderness at the posterior edge/tip of the **lateral**
malleolus (distal 6 cm), bone tenderness at the posterior edge/tip of the **medial** malleolus
(distal 6 cm), or **inability to bear weight both immediately after injury and in the ED (4 steps).**
[grounded]

**Ottawa Foot Rule** (companion rule, midfoot) — X-ray indicated if: pain in the midfoot zone AND
bone tenderness at the base of the 5th metatarsal, bone tenderness at the navicular, or inability to
weight-bear 4 steps. [EK, standard companion to the Ottawa Ankle Rule]

**Ottawa Knee Rule** (imaging decision in acute knee injury) — X-ray indicated if any of: age ≥55,
isolated tenderness of the patella (no other bony tenderness), tenderness at the head of the
fibula, inability to flex the knee to 90°, inability to bear weight both immediately and in the ED
(4 steps). [grounded]

**Kocher criteria** (paediatric septic arthritis of the hip vs transient synovitis — probability
rises steeply with each additional criterion present): **non-weight-bearing**, **fever >38.5°C**,
**WCC >12 ×10⁹/L**, **ESR ≥40 mm/h** (the original four); the **modified/Caird criteria** add
**CRP >20 mg/L** as a fifth, independently strong predictor. Approximate probability of septic
arthritis: 0 criteria <0.2%, 1 criterion ~3%, 2 criteria ~40%, 3 criteria ~93%, 4 criteria ~99.6%.
[grounded — §2.9. Use as decision support, not an override of strong clinical concern, and note
more recent literature flags reduced reliability in the Kingella kingae era of paediatric septic
arthritis, less relevant to the typical SA *S. aureus*-dominant bone/joint sepsis picture but worth
knowing exists.]

**Garden classification** (intracapsular femoral neck fracture, §2.4): **I** incomplete/valgus-
impacted (stable); **II** complete, undisplaced (stable); **III** complete, partially displaced
(unstable); **IV** complete, fully displaced (unstable, highest AVN risk). Garden I–II favour
fixation; III–IV in the elderly favour arthroplasty. [grounded]

**Weber classification** (ankle fracture, fibular level relative to syndesmosis, §2.6): **A** below
the syndesmosis (usually stable); **B** at the level of the syndesmosis (stability depends on medial
structures); **C** above the syndesmosis (syndesmosis disrupted, usually unstable, usually needs
fixation). [grounded]

**Gartland classification** (paediatric supracondylar humerus fracture, extension type, §2.7):
**I** undisplaced; **II** displaced, posterior cortex intact; **III** completely displaced, no
cortical contact — highest neurovascular risk. [grounded]

**ASIA/ISNCSCI Impairment Scale** (spinal cord injury, assessed after spinal shock resolves, §2.12):
**A** — complete: no motor or sensory function preserved in sacral segments S4–S5. **B** — sensory
incomplete: sensory but not motor function preserved below the level, including S4–S5. **C** —
motor incomplete: motor function preserved below the level, but more than half of key muscles below
the level have a grade <3. **D** — motor incomplete: motor function preserved below the level, and
at least half of key muscles below the level have a grade ≥3. **E** — normal motor and sensory
function. The **sacral-sparing exam** (perianal sensation, voluntary anal contraction, deep anal
pressure) is what distinguishes A from B/C/D — always perform and document it explicitly.
[grounded]

**Wells score (DVT)** — used post-operatively in the immobilised/fractured ortho patient exactly as
in general medicine/surgery: active cancer, paralysis/recent plaster immobilisation of the lower
limb, recently bedridden ≥3 days or major surgery within 12 weeks, localised tenderness along the
deep venous system, entire leg swollen, calf swelling >3 cm compared to the other leg, pitting
oedema confined to the symptomatic leg, collateral superficial veins, previous DVT (each scores 1);
an alternative diagnosis at least as likely scores −2. Higher scores raise pre-test probability and
lower the threshold for Doppler ultrasound; a low score with a low/negative D-dimer helps exclude
DVT, though D-dimer is of limited use in the immediate post-operative/post-trauma period when it is
non-specifically elevated by the surgery/injury itself — lean more on the score and clinical exam
and Doppler threshold in that window. [EK, standard tool, applied here to the ortho-specific
post-operative/post-fracture VTE-surveillance context]

**MRC power grading (0–5)** — the universal muscle-strength language used in every neurovascular
and cord-injury exam in this dossier (§3, §2.3, §2.12): 0 no contraction; 1 flicker/trace; 2
movement with gravity eliminated; 3 movement against gravity; 4 movement against resistance
(sometimes further split 4−/4/4+ for graded resistance); 5 normal power. [EK]

**AO/OTA fracture classification** — the structured alphanumeric system (bone – segment –
type/group/subgroup, e.g. type A extra-articular, B partial-articular, C complete-articular, with
further severity subgrouping) used for research, audit, and precise inter-specialist communication;
day-to-day clinical description more often uses the plain-language grammar in §3, with AO/OTA coding
reserved for formal documentation/registry purposes. [EK]

**Salter-Harris classification** (paediatric physeal/growth-plate injury) — **I** fracture through
the physis alone; **II** through the physis and metaphysis (commonest); **III** through the physis
and epiphysis (intra-articular); **IV** through metaphysis, physis, and epiphysis (intra-articular,
higher growth-arrest risk); **V** a crush injury to the physis (often not visible acutely,
diagnosed retrospectively when growth arrest occurs — the highest growth-disturbance risk despite
looking radiographically unimpressive at presentation). [EK]

## 6. SA-specific reality

**The injury profile is dominated by interpersonal violence and road-traffic trauma, and this is
not a subspecialty caveat, it is the default caseload** [grounded]. Series from major SA trauma
centres report **stab wounds and gunshot wounds together accounting for roughly a third to a half
of trauma-centre presentations at high-volume urban units**, with assault, stabbing, and firearm
injury dominating the mechanism profile — one Johannesburg trauma-centre audit recorded stabbing at
~33%, gunshot wounds at ~15%, and blunt assault at ~14% of the case mix, with the majority of
patients young (18–34 years) and male. South Africa's **interpersonal-violence mortality runs at
roughly seven times the global age-standardised rate**, and **road-traffic injury at roughly double
the global average**, with the majority of road-traffic-collision admissions carrying an
orthopaedic component (one district-level audit found roughly two-thirds of RTA admissions had an
orthopaedic injury) [grounded]. Practically, this means: fluency in **penetrating limb trauma with
a hard/soft-sign vascular exam** (§2.3) is core general-orthopaedic/casualty skill, not a niche
vascular-surgery topic; **multiple simultaneous long-bone and pelvic fractures** are a routine
Friday/Saturday-night presentation requiring genuine triage skill (who goes to theatre first); and
**damage-control orthopaedics** (external fixation, staged definitive fixation once the patient and
soft tissues are ready) is frequently the default pathway rather than the textbook one-stage
fixation, both because of patient physiology and because of theatre/implant access constraints.

**Theatre access, implant availability, and the fixation backlog are real clinical variables, not
administrative footnotes** [EK]. A displaced fracture that "should" go to theatre within 24–48
hours may wait days at a resource-constrained regional/district hospital because of theatre-slot
competition (trauma lists are frequently overwhelmed by the volume above), implant stock
(specific plate/screw/nail sizes are not always in stock, and external fixation is sometimes chosen
*because* it does not require a specific implant set rather than for its clinical merits alone), and
referral-pathway delay for injuries needing subspecialist fixation (complex pelvic/acetabular,
spinal instrumentation) at a tertiary centre. The clinically correct response is not to accept this
silently as background noise, but to **actively document the delay, escalate via the referral
pathway, and prioritise interim measures** (splinting, traction, provisional external fixation,
serial neurovascular monitoring) that protect the limb and the patient while awaiting definitive
theatre access.

**TB burden shapes the bone/joint and spine differential directly** (§2.14). Spinal and
osteoarticular TB are genuinely common considerations in the SA differential of subacute back pain,
a "cold" joint effusion, or an unexplained kyphotic deformity, in a way that would be a genuine
zebra in a low-TB-burden health system — one South African series described spinal TB prevalence
figures materially higher than in non-endemic settings [reported in the literature reviewed this
session; the precise magnitude of the cited figure should be independently verified before being
built into any structured decision-support content, flagged below]. The practical takeaway that does
not depend on the precise number: **every subacute back-pain-with-red-flags and every "cold",
subacute mono-arthritis in this system gets an active TB work-up (Xpert Ultra, culture, HIV test)
run in parallel with, not instead of, the malignancy/pyogenic-infection work-up.**

**HIV changes both the infection burden and the healing physiology in orthopaedics** [EK]. HIV-
positive patients carry a higher burden and more atypical presentation of septic arthritis and
osteomyelitis (§2.9, §2.13), a blunted systemic response that can under-represent the severity of an
infection clinically, and — in advanced disease — impaired fracture and wound healing and higher
surgical-site infection rates. Every trauma/ortho admission should include an HIV status check
(offer testing if unknown) as routine, both because it changes the infection differential and
threshold for source control, and because it feeds the TB-screening and ART-timing decisions that
run throughout this hospital system (see `internal-medicine.md` §2.19 for the AHD framework, which
applies identically to the orthopaedic patient with sepsis).

**VTE prophylaxis in the fractured/immobilised patient** [EK, structurally consistent with SA
practice] — mechanical prophylaxis (compression stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression, or
early mobilisation itself where feasible) is the default first step and is frequently the *only*
step available in a resource-constrained ward, given LMWH cost/availability variability across the
public system; pharmacological prophylaxis (LMWH, e.g. enoxaparin, or where genuinely unavailable a
low-dose unfractionated heparin regimen) is added for higher-risk patients — hip fracture surgery,
major lower-limb fracture surgery, pelvic fracture, prolonged immobility, spinal cord injury once
past the acute bleeding-risk window — for an extended duration (commonly quoted international
practice for hip fracture/major lower-limb arthroplasty runs several weeks post-operatively, though
local unit protocol and resource availability determine what is actually deliverable). **The
resource-constrained reality means prophylaxis decisions are actively triaged by risk, not applied
uniformly** — document the risk assessment and the chosen regimen explicitly rather than defaulting
silently to "nothing" because pharmacological options were momentarily unavailable.

**J88 documentation — a medico-legal and forensic obligation that intersects constantly with
orthopaedic trauma** [EK, cross-referenced with `clinical-foundations.md`'s ATLS/AMPLE guidance].
Every assault, gunshot, stab, and gender-based-violence-related orthopaedic injury requires
contemporaneous, precise **J88** medico-legal documentation — the exact anatomical location,
dimensions, and description of every wound (not just the fracture), because this document
frequently becomes the primary medical evidence in a subsequent criminal prosecution. This is not a
delegable afterthought: **describe wounds in the same disciplined, specific language used for
fracture description in §3** (site, size, shape, direction, any track/depth where safely assessable
without probing a wound purely for documentation purposes), photograph where the facility protocol
allows, and maintain a clear chain of evidence for any retrieved foreign material (bullet fragments,
weapon fragments) handed to forensic/police services.

**District-to-tertiary referral for fixation** is a routine, structured part of SA orthopaedic
practice rather than an exception [EK]. Complex intra-articular fractures needing specialist
fixation expertise (acetabular, complex pelvic, spinal instrumentation, complex peri-articular
patterns), major vascular injury needing combined vascular-orthopaedic input, and paediatric
injuries needing specialist paediatric-orthopaedic care are commonly stabilised (splinted, provisionally
external-fixed, resuscitated) at district level and transferred onward — the district clinician's
job in these cases is **excellent provisional stabilisation, meticulous neurovascular documentation,
and a clear, complete referral letter/handover**, not attempted definitive fixation beyond their
resource or training level.
