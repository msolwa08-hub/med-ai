## 1. The consultant's mental model

A general surgeon walking onto the ward is not primarily making a diagnosis — they are answering four questions in a fixed order, and the diagnosis is subordinate to them:

1. **Is this patient about to die in front of me?** — the resuscitation question. Shock, peritonitis, an ischaemic gut, a threatened limb, a torsed testis. Physiology before anatomy. A blood pressure is reassuring for only a few minutes in a young trauma patient (they compensate, then crash).
2. **Does this need an operation, and how soon?** — the *indication-for-surgery* question. Everything in the surgical note ultimately serves this. "Acute appendicitis" is not a note; "acute appendicitis → appendicectomy today" is. The decision is source-control (drain the pus, resect the dead gut, stop the bleeding, decompress the obstruction) versus time.
3. **Can this patient survive the operation I want to do?** — the fitness/optimisation question. The disease may be operable while the patient is not (uncorrected sepsis, K⁺ of 6.8, an INR of 4).
4. **What am I secretly ruling out?** — the misses that end careers. In every acute abdomen: **a leaking AAA, mesenteric ischaemia, an MI presenting as epigastric pain, an ectopic pregnancy (βhCG on EVERY woman of childbearing age), and a strangulated hernia in an obese abdomen you didn't fully expose.** In every "gastro": the surgical abdomen hiding behind vomiting. In every post-op patient who is tachycardic: a leak or a bleed until proven otherwise.

**The tempo.** Surgery runs on *trends*, not snapshots. A single Hb, a single lactate, a single CRP means little; the *direction* is the information. The consultant's instinct is serial: re-examine the abdomen in 2–4 hours, re-check the Hb, re-read the drain. "The abdomen that is worse on the third examination is a surgical abdomen even if the first two were equivocal." Active observation with a defined review interval is a legitimate management plan — *documented* observation, not drift.

**The three reflexes juniors lack:**
- **Tachycardia is the earliest and most honest vital sign.** A rising pulse precedes the fall in BP, precedes peritonism, precedes the obvious. A post-op patient whose heart rate climbs from 80 to 110 over a shift has a complication until proven otherwise — *look for the leak/bleed before you reach for the analgesia*.
- **Examine the hernial orifices and the external genitalia in every abdomen.** The obstructed bowel is in the groin you didn't uncover.
- **Put the finger in.** The PR is refused at the junior's peril — the rectal cancer, the melaena, the boggy prostate, the empty rectum of obstruction, the blood of ischaemia.

**Always/never anchors:** always βhCG in a woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain; always a lactate and an erect CXR in the undifferentiated acute abdomen; always mark the site/side and run the WHO checklist; never diagnose "gastroenteritis" in a patient with localised tenderness and guarding; never let an ischaemic limb or torsed testis wait for an ultrasound if the clinical picture is clear; never treat surgical sepsis with antibiotics alone — **source control is the treatment, antibiotics are the adjunct.**

## 2. Presenting syndromes — worked to depth

### 2.1 Right iliac fossa (RIF) pain

**Tier A — common:**
- Acute appendicitis (lifetime risk ~7%; the default in a young patient with migratory pain).
- Mesenteric adenitis (children/young adults, post-viral, higher fever than tenderness, shifting/diffuse tenderness).
- Constipation; UTI; right ureteric colic.
- Gastroenteritis (diffuse, precedes tenderness — but beware the surgical abdomen hiding behind vomiting).

**Tier B — dangerous / must-not-miss:**
- **Ruptured ectopic pregnancy** — any woman of reproductive age; βhCG is non-negotiable.
- Ovarian torsion (sudden severe pain, adnexal mass, intermittent) and ruptured/haemorrhagic ovarian cyst.
- Perforated appendix with abscess/mass.
- Caecal/right-colon carcinoma perforating or obstructing (older patient, weight loss, anaemia).
- Meckel's diverticulitis — mimics appendicitis exactly; **"rule of 2s"** (2% of population, 2 feet from ileocaecal valve, 2 inches long, 2% symptomatic, often 2 years old, 2 tissue types).
- Low-lying perforated duodenal ulcer tracking down the right paracolic gutter.
- Right-sided diverticulitis (commoner in patients of Asian descent).

**Tier C — the zebras & the consultant's list:**
- **Crohn's terminal ileitis** — chronic history, mouth ulcers, perianal disease, weight loss, diarrhoea; the "appendicectomy that finds a normal appendix and an inflamed ileum". **Do not resect the ileum, do not do an ileocaecal anastomosis in active Crohn's — fistula risk.**
- **TB ileitis / ileocaecal TB** — SA-critical: night sweats, weight loss, HIV, a mass, ascites; mimics both caecal cancer and appendicitis.
- **Typhlitis / neutropenic enterocolitis** — the immunocompromised/chemotherapy patient with RIF pain + neutropenia; **managed medically, operating can be fatal.**
- Yersinia enterocolitis (pseudo-appendicitis).
- Rectus sheath haematoma — anticoagulated patient, positive **Carnett's sign** (tenderness persists/worsens on tensing the abdominal wall = wall, not viscus).
- Psoas abscess — spinal TB/discitis pointing to the groin, pain on hip extension.
- Epiploic appendagitis; omental infarction.

**Discriminating history:** *migration* of pain from periumbilical to RIF (visceral → parietal peritoneum) is the classic appendicitis story and is more specific than any single sign. Anorexia ("I'm not hungry" — a hungry patient rarely has appendicitis). Last menstrual period, contraception, possibility of pregnancy, vaginal discharge, dyspareunia (gynae causes). Diarrhoea + fever *before* pain suggests adenitis/gastroenteritis; pain *before* vomiting suggests a surgical cause (**Murphy's sequence** in appendicitis: pain → nausea/vomiting → fever, in that order). Chronic diarrhoea, mouth ulcers, perianal disease (Crohn's). Night sweats, weight loss, TB contact, HIV (TB ileitis).

**Discriminating examination:** McBurney's point tenderness; **Rovsing's sign** (LIF palpation → RIF pain); **psoas sign** (pain on right hip extension — retrocaecal appendix); **obturator sign** (pain on internal rotation of flexed right hip — pelvic appendix); **Blumberg's** (rebound). Localised guarding that becomes generalised = perforation. A palpable mass = appendix mass/abscess or a caecal carcinoma. **Cervical excitation and adnexal tenderness** on bimanual (PID/ectopic/ovarian). **Carnett's sign** positive → abdominal wall, not intra-abdominal. Pyrexia disproportionate to abdominal signs → adenitis.

**Investigations & interpretation:** WCC/CRP (a *normal* WCC does not exclude appendicitis, especially early; a normal CRP + normal WCC together lower probability substantially). **βhCG on every female** — a positive βhCG with pain and no intrauterine pregnancy on scan = ectopic until proven otherwise. Urine dip (blood/leucocytes → but sterile pyuria can occur with an inflamed appendix lying on the ureter — do not be falsely reassured). **Ultrasound** first-line in children, young women, and thin patients (non-compressible blind-ending tubular structure >6 mm; also finds ovarian/tubal pathology and free fluid). **CT** for the older patient, the atypical, the query-malignancy — but ionising dose matters in the young. Alvarado score to stratify (see §5). In SA reality, appendicitis is frequently a *clinical* diagnosis taken to theatre without CT.

**Management anchors:** appendicectomy (laparoscopic where available, open Lanz/gridiron otherwise). Resuscitate, analgesia (does NOT mask the diagnosis — the old dogma is wrong), IV antibiotics per SA STG surgical prophylaxis/therapy (a cephalosporin + metronidazole, e.g. cefazolin + metronidazole, escalated for perforation). **Appendix mass** (a walled-off phlegmon, several days into the illness): the consultant often chooses *conservative* management — IV antibiotics, bowel rest, interval imaging — because operating into an inflamed mass risks injuring caecum/ileum; interval appendicectomy or colonoscopy to exclude a caecal cancer follows. **Appendix abscess:** image-guided/surgical drainage + antibiotics.

**Traps & pearls:** the pregnant appendix sits *higher* (RUQ by third trimester) — do not exclude appendicitis on the site of pain in pregnancy, and it carries high fetal loss. The retrocaecal appendix gives few peritoneal signs (psoas sign instead). In the elderly and the immunosuppressed, signs are muted and perforation is the first presentation. A "normal appendix" at operation obliges you to **look for Meckel's, examine the ileum for Crohn's/TB, and check the ovaries** — do not close on a negative laparoscopy without a systematic search. **Never resect an inflamed terminal ileum you find unexpectedly** — it may be Crohn's or TB, biopsy and treat medically.

### 2.2 Right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain — the biliary spectrum

**Tier A:**
- Biliary colic (post-prandial, fatty food, RUQ/epigastric, radiates to right scapular tip, self-limiting < a few hours, no fever).
- Acute cholecystitis (constant RUQ pain >6h, fever, Murphy's positive).
- Non-ulcer dyspepsia; hepatic congestion (right heart failure — tender smooth hepatomegaly).

**Tier B — must-not-miss:**
- **Ascending cholangitis** — **Charcot's triad** (RUQ pain + jaundice + fever/rigors); **Reynolds' pentad** adds hypotension + confusion = suppurative cholangitis, a surgical/endoscopic emergency — **decompress the biliary tree.**
- Gallstone pancreatitis.
- Empyema / gangrene / perforation of the gallbladder.
- Choledocholithiasis with obstruction.
- **Right lower lobe pneumonia and inferior MI masquerading as RUQ pain** (ECG + CXR belong here).
- Liver abscess — pyogenic or amoebic (SA-relevant).

**Tier C — zebras:**
- **Acalculous cholecystitis** — the critically ill/ICU/fasted/burns/post-op patient; gallbladder ischaemia, no stones, high mortality, easily missed.
- **Mirizzi syndrome** — stone in Hartmann's pouch compressing the CBD → obstructive jaundice with cholecystitis.
- Gallbladder carcinoma (porcelain gallbladder, mass, weight loss, elderly).
- **Fitz-Hugh–Curtis syndrome** — perihepatitis from PID/chlamydia/gonorrhoea; young woman, RUQ pain + pelvic infection.
- Hepatocellular carcinoma with capsular pain/rupture (HBV/HCV, cirrhosis — SA-relevant).
- Amoebic liver abscess (fever, tender hepatomegaly, recent dysentery, "anchovy sauce" aspirate).

**Discriminating history:** relation to fatty meals; duration (colic self-limits, cholecystitis persists); rigors + dark urine + pale stool + jaundice (obstruction/cholangitis); previous ERCP/known stones; alcohol; travel/dysentery (amoebic abscess); weight loss (malignancy).

**Discriminating examination:** **Murphy's sign** (arrest of inspiration on RUQ palpation — sensitive for cholecystitis, less specific in the elderly); palpable tender gallbladder; jaundice (obstruction); **Courvoisier's law** — a palpable, non-tender gallbladder with jaundice is *unlikely* to be stones (stones scar and shrink the gallbladder), think pancreatic/biliary malignancy. Tender hepatomegaly (abscess/congestion).

**Investigations & interpretation:** **LFTs pattern is the discriminator** — an *obstructive/cholestatic* pattern (ALP and GGT ↑↑ out of proportion to ALT/AST, bilirubin ↑) points to CBD obstruction/cholangitis; a *hepatocellular* pattern (ALT/AST ↑↑) points to hepatitis/ischaemia; isolated bilirubin with normal enzymes → haemolysis/Gilbert's. **Amylase/lipase** to catch gallstone pancreatitis. **US** is first-line: stones, gallbladder wall >3 mm, pericholecystic fluid, sonographic Murphy's, and the key measurement — **CBD diameter (normal ≤6 mm, +1 mm/decade over 60; a dilated duct = obstruction, look for the stone)**. MRCP/ERCP for ductal stones (ERCP is therapeutic — sphincterotomy + stone extraction + stent).

**Management anchors:** cholecystitis — resuscitate, nil per mouth, IV antibiotics (SA STG: e.g. amoxicillin-clavulanate, or cephalosporin + metronidazole), analgesia; **early laparoscopic cholecystectomy within the index admission / <72h of onset is preferred** where the service allows (fewer complications than delayed). Cholangitis — **the priority is biliary drainage** (ERCP, or PTC, or surgery) + antibiotics + resuscitation; antibiotics alone in an obstructed, infected biliary tree will fail. Gallstone pancreatitis — treat the pancreatitis, then cholecystectomy in the same admission once recovered (prevents recurrence). Amoebic liver abscess — **metronidazole** (drainage only if large/left-lobe/impending rupture/no response); pyogenic abscess — drain + antibiotics.

**Traps & pearls:** normal LFTs do not exclude a CBD stone. Acalculous cholecystitis is missed because "there are no stones" — suspect it in any septic ICU patient with RUQ signs and an inability to explain the sepsis. Do not send a jaundiced cholangitis patient to a routine list — they need drainage now. A right-lower-lobe pneumonia and an inferior MI both refer to the RUQ — **ECG and CXR belong in the RUQ workup.**

### 2.3 Epigastric pain

**Tier A:** peptic ulcer disease / gastritis, GORD, biliary/pancreatic (see above), non-ulcer dyspepsia.

**Tier B — must-not-miss (the "epigastric five"):**
- **Perforated peptic ulcer** — sudden severe pain, "board-like" rigidity, generalised peritonitis, loss of liver dullness, free air under the diaphragm on erect CXR. Chemical peritonitis first (acid), bacterial later.
- **Acute pancreatitis** — severe constant epigastric pain radiating to the back, relieved by sitting forward, vomiting; look for gallstones/alcohol.
- **Leaking/ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)** — the great mimic: back/flank/epigastric pain + collapse + a pulsatile expansile mass; may present as "renal colic" in an older man. **Any man >60 with new "renal colic" has a AAA until excluded.**
- **Myocardial infarction (inferior/posterior)** — epigastric pain, nausea, sweating, "indigestion that won't settle." ECG + troponin belong here.
- **Mesenteric ischaemia** (see §2.6) — pain out of proportion.

**Tier C — zebras:** ruptured/dissecting thoracic aorta extending, oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave's — vomiting → severe chest/epigastric pain → surgical emphysema, mediastinitis; Mackler's triad), diabetic ketoacidosis presenting with abdominal pain, hypercalcaemia, Addisonian crisis, lead poisoning, gastric volvulus (Borchardt's triad: severe pain, retching without vomiting, inability to pass an NG tube).

**Discriminating history:** sudden "worst ever" instant onset (perforation, ruptured AAA, vascular) vs crescendo (pancreatitis, cholecystitis); NSAID/aspirin/steroid use and prior ulcer/H. pylori (perforation); alcohol binge, gallstones (pancreatitis); cardiac risk factors, exertional component, diaphoresis (MI); back pain + syncope + smoker/hypertensive male (AAA).

**Discriminating examination:** board-like rigidity + absent bowel sounds (perforation/generalised peritonitis); epigastric bruising — **Cullen's** (periumbilical) and **Grey Turner's** (flank) signs of retroperitoneal haemorrhage in severe necrotising pancreatitis (late, ominous); pulsatile expansile mass + unequal femoral pulses + hypotension (AAA — **palpate gently, do not provoke rupture**); the deceptively soft abdomen with a shocked patient in mesenteric ischaemia and AAA.

**Investigations & interpretation:** **erect CXR** (free subdiaphragmatic air in ~70–80% of perforations — its *absence does not exclude* perforation; a lateral decubitus or CT is more sensitive). **Serum amylase/lipase** — lipase is more specific and stays elevated longer; **amylase >3× upper limit of normal supports pancreatitis but the height does not correlate with severity, and amylase can be normal in necrotising pancreatitis or falsely raised in perforation/ischaemia/mesenteric infarction/ectopic/DKA** — interpret with the picture. **ECG + troponin** for the MI mimic. **Bedside US / CT aorta** for AAA (do not delay a shocked patient with a known/obvious AAA for imaging — theatre). **ABG/lactate** (ischaemia, severity). Glasgow-Imrie / modified score at 48h for pancreatitis severity (§5).

**Management anchors:** perforated ulcer — resuscitate, NG decompression, IV PPI, broad-spectrum antibiotics + antifungal cover for upper GI perforation, **surgery: omental (Graham) patch + washout**; H. pylori eradication and PPI after. **Boey score** (§5) predicts mortality and guides urgency. Pancreatitis — this is *medical* resuscitation, not an operation: aggressive fluids (goal-directed, avoid over-resuscitation), analgesia, early enteral nutrition when tolerated, treat the cause (ERCP for obstructing stone + cholangitis); antibiotics only for proven infected necrosis, not prophylactically. Ruptured AAA — **theatre/vascular immediately**, permissive hypotension, massive transfusion protocol, do not over-resuscitate before clamp.

**Traps & pearls:** "indigestion" in a diaphoretic older patient is an MI until the ECG says otherwise. Amylase is neither perfectly sensitive nor specific — a normal amylase does not exclude pancreatitis and a raised amylase is not always pancreatitis (perforation and mesenteric ischaemia raise it too). Do not repeatedly examine a suspected leaking AAA — palpation can convert a contained leak into free rupture. Severity in pancreatitis is a 48-hour and physiological judgement (organ failure, CRP >150 at 48h), not the amylase number.

### 2.4 Left iliac fossa (LIF) / left-sided pain

**Tier A:**
- Acute diverticulitis (older patient, LIF pain, low-grade fever, altered bowel habit).
- Constipation; gastroenteritis/colitis; ureteric colic.
- Gynae (as for RIF — ectopic/ovarian/PID on the left).

**Tier B — must-not-miss:**
- **Perforated diverticulitis** with faeculent/purulent peritonitis (Hinchey III–IV — septic, generalised peritonitis).
- Obstructing/perforating **sigmoid colorectal carcinoma** (weight loss, change in bowel habit, PR bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia). **Diverticulitis and cancer coexist and mimic — always exclude cancer after an attack with interval colonoscopy.**
- **Ischaemic colitis** — watershed splenic flexure/left colon; "pain then bloody diarrhoea" in an arteriopath / post-AAA-repair / hypotensive patient.
- Sigmoid volvulus (see §2.5).

**Tier C — zebras:**
- IBD flare (UC/Crohn's — bloody diarrhoea, systemic features); **toxic megacolon** — colon >6 cm + systemic toxicity, a surgical emergency.
- Infective / pseudomembranous (C. difficile) colitis.
- Epiploic appendagitis of the sigmoid; a left-sided appendix in situs inversus/malrotation.

**Discriminating history:** change in bowel habit, PR bleeding, weight loss, tenesmus, iron-deficiency (cancer); previous diverticulitis; bloody diarrhoea + urgency (colitis); vascular disease + post-prandial pain (ischaemic colitis); the constipated elderly nursing-home patient with gross distension (volvulus).

**Investigations & interpretation:** WCC/CRP; **CT abdomen with contrast is the investigation of choice for LIF pain** (grades diverticulitis by Hinchey, finds the abscess/free air, distinguishes cancer, shows ischaemic colon and volvulus). **AXR** for obstruction/volvulus. **Do NOT do a colonoscopy in acute diverticulitis** (perforation risk) — defer it 6 weeks to exclude cancer. Flexible sigmoidoscopy/colonoscopy for colitis when settled.

**Management anchors:** uncomplicated diverticulitis (Hinchey 0–I) — increasingly managed conservatively, even as outpatient in the well: antibiotics (SA STG: amoxicillin-clavulanate, or ciprofloxacin + metronidazole), analgesia, clear fluids. Abscess (Hinchey II) — image-guided drainage + antibiotics. Perforated with peritonitis (Hinchey III–IV) — **resuscitate + Hartmann's procedure (sigmoid resection + end colostomy)** classically; primary anastomosis in selected stable patients. Ischaemic colitis — usually supportive (fluids, bowel rest, antibiotics), most resolve; operate for gangrene/peritonitis/stricture. Toxic megacolon — aggressive medical therapy + early surgery (colectomy) if no rapid response.

**Traps & pearls:** never attribute LIF symptoms with weight loss and anaemia to diverticulitis without excluding cancer. Ischaemic colitis is under-diagnosed — think of it in the older vasculopath with sudden LIF pain and bloody diarrhoea; the classic setting is *after AAA repair* (IMA ligated). Free air is a surgical abdomen regardless of the label.

### 2.5 The obstructed abdomen

**The four cardinal questions:** (1) small bowel or large bowel? (2) is it obstruction (mechanical) or ileus (functional)? (3) is it simple or **strangulated/closed-loop** (the surgical emergency)? (4) what is the level and cause?

**Small bowel obstruction (SBO) — causes, in order:**
- **Adhesions** (60–75%) — previous surgery; "the abdomen with a scar and obstruction is adhesional until proven otherwise — but never *assume*."
- **Hernias** — the commonest cause with *no* prior surgery (external: inguinal/femoral/umbilical/incisional; internal).
- Malignancy (primary, or metastatic/peritoneal carcinomatosis).
- Crohn's stricture; TB stricture (SA); radiation enteritis.
- Intussusception (adult — usually a lead-point tumour); bezoar.
- Gallstone ileus — elderly woman; **Rigler's triad** (pneumobilia + SBO + ectopic gallstone on imaging).

**Large bowel obstruction (LBO) — causes:**
- **Colorectal carcinoma** — the commonest (~60%).
- **Volvulus** — sigmoid » caecal; sigmoid in the elderly/constipated/institutionalised ("coffee-bean"/inverted-U on AXR pointing to RUQ); caecal in younger patients with malrotation.
- Diverticular stricture; faecal impaction; hernia; pseudo-obstruction (see below).

**Pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie's syndrome) — the mechanical-obstruction mimic to know:** acute colonic dilatation *without* a mechanical cause, from an imbalance of autonomic innervation — the classic setting is the elderly, bed-bound, or recently operated patient (orthopaedic/spinal/pelvic surgery), the septic, the electrolyte-deranged (low K⁺/Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺), the patient on opioids/anticholinergics, or with Parkinson's/hypothyroidism. Massive colonic (especially caecal) distension mimics LBO but **CT/contrast enema shows no transition point/no obstructing lesion.** Management: treat the cause, correct electrolytes, stop the offending drugs, NG + rectal tube decompression, and **IV neostigmine** (with cardiac monitoring — bradycardia/asystole risk) for refractory cases; **colonoscopic decompression** if that fails. **The danger is the same as true LBO — caecal diameter >12 cm risks ischaemic perforation.**

**SBO vs LBO discriminators:**
| | SBO | LBO |
|---|---|---|
| Vomiting | early, bilious then faeculent | late |
| Distension | central, less | peripheral, marked |
| Pain | colicky, higher frequency | colicky, lower frequency |
| Constipation | later | early, absolute |
| AXR | central, valvulae conniventes cross the full lumen, <3 cm | peripheral, haustra (partial), >6 cm (caecum >9 cm) |

**Closed-loop & strangulation — the emergency to detect:** a **closed-loop** obstruction (bowel obstructed at two points — e.g. volvulus, an incarcerated hernia, obstructing cancer with a competent ileocaecal valve) cannot decompress and progresses to ischaemia and perforation rapidly. **Strangulation** = the blood supply is cut off. Warning signs: **continuous (rather than colicky) pain, localised tenderness/guarding, tachycardia, fever, rising WCC and lactate, and a metabolic acidosis** — these override the "obstruction can wait" instinct and mandate urgent surgery. **The competent ileocaecal valve in LBO is dangerous** — it turns the colon into a closed loop; the caecum (Laplace: largest diameter, highest wall tension) perforates first; **caecal diameter >12 cm is impending perforation.**

**Discriminating history:** previous surgery (adhesions), previous obstruction, hernia, change in bowel habit/PR bleeding/weight loss (cancer), constipation and institutionalisation (sigmoid volvulus), the character of vomit (faeculent = established/distal), whether flatus/stool still passing (partial vs complete).

**Discriminating examination:** distension, visible peristalsis, high-pitched "tinkling" then absent bowel sounds; **every hernial orifice and the genitalia examined and documented**; a mass; **PR — empty rectum (obstruction above), a rectal cancer, or blood (ischaemia/intussusception)**; scars. Signs of strangulation: focal tenderness, guarding, systemic toxicity.

**Investigations & interpretation:** **AXR** (dilated loops, air-fluid levels on erect, the valvulae/haustra pattern, "coffee bean" of sigmoid volvulus, pneumobilia of gallstone ileus, absence of rectal gas). **Erect CXR** for free air (perforation). **CT with contrast** is now the key study — confirms level, cause (transition point), closed loop, and — critically — **signs of ischaemia: bowel-wall thickening, reduced/absent mural enhancement, pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, mesenteric fluid/oedema, a whirl sign (volvulus).** **UEC** (hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis with proximal vomiting; dehydration/AKI), **lactate** (rising = ischaemia), FBC.

**Management anchors:** **"drip and suck"** — NG tube decompression + IV fluid resuscitation + electrolyte correction + urinary catheter + analgesia — is the initial management of *simple* adhesional SBO, and ~70–90% settle without surgery over 24–48h (a water-soluble contrast study, e.g. Gastrografin, is both prognostic and therapeutic — contrast reaching the colon within 24h predicts resolution). **Operate for:** strangulation/closed loop, an obstructing hernia (incarcerated/strangulated — do NOT reduce a strangulated hernia; reduce-en-masse is dangerous), a virgin abdomen (no adhesions to blame), failure to settle, or peritonitis. **Sigmoid volvulus** — endoscopic decompression (rigid/flexible sigmoidoscopy + flatus tube) is first-line and dramatically effective; **but if there is any sign of ischaemia/peritonitis go to theatre**; high recurrence means elective sigmoidectomy is offered after. **LBO from cancer** — resuscitate, then resect (Hartmann's/right hemicolectomy) or defunctioning stoma/stent depending on site and fitness.

**Traps & pearls:** **the hernia in the fat groin you didn't expose is the missed cause of "adhesional" SBO** — expose fully. **Never label obstruction "adhesions" in a patient who has never had surgery.** Colicky pain becoming constant + tachycardia + rising lactate = strangulation — the window is hours. A normal lactate does not exclude early strangulation. Reducing an incarcerated hernia that contains dead bowel returns necrotic gut and its toxins into the abdomen — if in doubt, explore. In LBO, a competent ileocaecal valve is a *bad* sign (closed loop), an incompetent one vents into the small bowel and buys time.

### 2.6 Mesenteric ischaemia

**The consultant's fear diagnosis** — high mortality (60–80%), diagnosed late because *the examination is normal early*.

**Four mechanisms [EK]:** (1) **arterial embolism** (~50%, usually SMA — AF, recent MI, valve disease; sudden catastrophic pain, often with an embolic source; classically spares the first few cm of jejunum), (2) **arterial thrombosis** (~25%, on pre-existing atherosclerosis — history of *chronic mesenteric ischaemia*: post-prandial pain / "intestinal angina", food fear, weight loss), (3) **non-occlusive mesenteric ischaemia (NOMI)** (low-flow state — shock, sepsis, cardiac failure, vasopressors, dialysis; the ICU patient), (4) **mesenteric venous thrombosis** (younger, hypercoagulable/thrombophilia, portal hypertension, malignancy, prior DVT; more subacute).

**The classic triad of clinical suspicion:** **severe pain out of proportion to physical findings** + an **embolic source (AF/vascular disease)** + **gut emptying** (early vomiting/diarrhoea). Sudden onset and a need for morphine in an at-risk patient should trigger CT angiography.

**Investigations & interpretation:** **lactate** rises but is a *late* marker (transmural infarction) — a normal lactate does not exclude early ischaemia; a rising lactate with a metabolic acidosis and a soft abdomen is ominous. WCC often very high. **CT angiography (arterial + portal venous phases) is the investigation of choice** — shows the occlusion, bowel-wall changes, pneumatosis, portal venous gas. Amylase may be mildly raised (mimic trap). Do not wait for peritonitis — by then the gut is dead.

**Management anchors:** aggressive resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, correct the low-flow state (NOMI — treat the cause, consider intra-arterial papaverine), anticoagulation (venous thrombosis, and generally), **and revascularisation ± resection of dead bowel** with a planned **second-look laparotomy** at 24–48h. Early angioembolectomy/thrombectomy or stenting where vascular services exist.

**Traps & pearls:** the soft abdomen with a screaming patient is the diagnosis, not reassurance. The elderly patient in AF with sudden severe abdominal pain and a normal abdomen has mesenteric ischaemia until CTA says otherwise. Do not be reassured by a normal lactate early. In NOMI the whole gut can be marginal — resect the frankly dead and re-look.

### 2.7 Generalised peritonitis

**Definition/picture:** the whole peritoneum inflamed — a rigid, silent, exquisitely tender abdomen; the patient lies still (movement/coughing hurts), shallow breathing, tachycardic, may be septic/shocked.

**Causes (by source) [EK]:**
- **Perforated viscus** — peptic ulcer, appendix, diverticulum, colon cancer, small bowel, gallbladder.
- **Anastomotic leak** (post-op); ischaemic/gangrenous bowel.
- Pancreatitis (chemical peritonitis); pelvic sepsis (PID, ruptured ectopic/ovarian, tubo-ovarian abscess).
- **The medical mimics a laparotomy would harm — exclude these:**
  - **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)** — cirrhotic with ascites; ascitic tap **PMN >250/mm³**; treat with a 3rd-gen cephalosporin. **Do NOT rush a cirrhotic with SBP to a lethal laparotomy.**
  - **TB peritonitis** — SA: subacute, ascites, doughy abdomen, weight loss, HIV; ascitic ADA, tissue for GeneXpert.
  - **CAPD peritonitis** — dialysis patient, cloudy bag; intraperitoneal antibiotics.
  - DKA, hypercalcaemia, Addisonian crisis, familial Mediterranean fever — medical causes of a rigid abdomen.

**Approach:** resuscitate (fluids, oxygen, analgesia, antibiotics, catheter, NG), erect CXR + bloods (WCC, CRP, lactate, amylase, βhCG, UEC, group-and-save), CT if stable enough to localise the source. **Source control is the operation** — laparotomy, wash out, treat the cause (patch/resect/drain/stoma). "There is no antibiotic for a hole in the gut."

**Traps:** exclude the medical mimics that a laparotomy would harm — SBP, TB peritonitis, DKA, and the pancreatitis that needs ICU not theatre. But when in doubt with a rigid abdomen and free air, the answer is the operation.

### 2.8 The silent abdomen — immunosuppressed / HIV / diabetic / elderly / on steroids

**The principle:** these patients **mute the physical signs** — corticosteroids and immunosuppression suppress the inflammatory response and fever; diabetic autonomic neuropathy and the elderly blunt pain and guarding; a perforation can present with minimal signs and a normal WCC. **Have a lower threshold to image and to operate; trust physiology (tachycardia, lactate, acidosis) over the reassuringly soft abdomen.**

**HIV-specific abdomen (SA-critical):** abdominal TB (ileocaecal TB, TB peritonitis, TB lymphadenopathy/mesenteric nodes, TB psoas abscess), CMV colitis/enteritis (can perforate), Kaposi sarcoma of the gut, lymphoma, opportunistic infections, drug-related pancreatitis (ddI historically), and immune-reconstitution phenomena. The HIV patient with an acute abdomen still gets appendicitis and cholecystitis — do not attribute everything to opportunistic disease. Low CD4 changes the differential toward CMV, TB, lymphoma.

**Diabetic abdomen:** DKA can *cause* abdominal pain (and a raised amylase) — but abdominal pathology can *precipitate* DKA; do not blame all abdominal pain on the DKA and miss the surgical cause. **Emphysematous cholecystitis/pyelonephritis** (gas-forming, diabetic, gravely ill) and **necrotising soft-tissue infection** are diabetic-specific.

**Pearls:** in the immunosuppressed, "the CRP and the lactate and the CT tell the truth the abdomen won't." Neutropenic patient + RIF pain = typhlitis — manage medically. Steroids mask perforation — free air on imaging with a soft abdomen is still a perforation.

### 2.9 Upper GI bleed (surgical lens)

**Causes [EK]:**
- **Peptic ulcer** — posterior duodenal ulcer eroding the gastroduodenal artery is the classic exsanguinating bleed; gastric ulcer on the lesser curve (left gastric).
- **Oesophageal/gastric varices** — portal hypertension (cirrhosis; **schistosomiasis is a major SA cause of pre-sinusoidal portal hypertension**).
- Gastritis / erosions (NSAIDs, alcohol, stress).
- Mallory-Weiss tear (retching → then haematemesis); oesophagitis; malignancy.
- Dieulafoy lesion (a large tortuous submucosal artery; recurrent brisk bleeds, hard to find).
- **Aorto-enteric fistula** — previous aortic graft; melaena/haematemesis + a graft = fatal until excluded.

**Approach:** ABC and resuscitation *first* (two large-bore lines, crossmatch, restrictive transfusion target Hb ~7 g/dL unless active massive bleed/cardiac), **risk-stratify with Glasgow-Blatchford (pre-endoscopy — a score of 0–1 may allow outpatient management) and Rockall (post-endoscopy mortality)** (§5). **NG/PPI infusion**, **endoscopy within 24h** (diagnostic + therapeutic: adrenaline injection + a second modality — clip/thermal). Variceal bleed: **terlipressin/octreotide + prophylactic antibiotics (ceftriaxone) + urgent endoscopic band ligation**; balloon tamponade (Sengstaken-Blakemore) as a bridge; TIPSS for refractory. Surgery/interventional radiology (embolisation) when endoscopy fails.

**Surgical trigger:** ongoing haemodynamic instability despite resuscitation and failed endoscopic control, or rebleeding — **oversew the ulcer / ligate the GDA.** The surgeon's job is the exsanguinating bleed endoscopy can't hold.

**Traps:** the "coffee-ground" that is actually brisk. A bleeding varix in a cirrhotic needs antibiotics (reduces mortality) — don't forget them. Melaena + a prior aortic graft = aorto-enteric fistula.

### 2.10 Lower GI bleed

**Causes [EK]:**
- **Diverticular bleed** — commonest; painless, brisk, usually self-limits.
- Angiodysplasia (elderly, recurrent); haemorrhoids/anorectal (bright, coating stool).
- Colorectal cancer/polyps; post-polypectomy bleed.
- Ischaemic colitis; IBD; infective colitis.
- **A brisk UPPER GI bleed presenting as maroon/red PR bleeding** — 10–15% of apparent lower bleeds are upper; **a shocked "lower" bleed needs an OGD.**

**Approach:** resuscitate; **exclude an upper source (NG aspirate/OGD) in the unstable patient**; PR + proctoscopy for anorectal cause; **CT angiography** localises active bleeding (needs a rate ~0.3–0.5 mL/min); colonoscopy after bowel prep once stable (diagnostic + therapeutic); mesenteric angiography + embolisation or surgery for uncontrolled bleeding. Most (~80%) settle.

**Traps:** don't scope an unstable patient — resuscitate/CTA first. Don't miss cancer — every significant lower GI bleed needs colonic evaluation once settled.

### 2.11 Anorectal presentations

**Perianal/anorectal abscess:** severe constant perianal pain, swelling, fever; **the treatment is incision and drainage — antibiotics alone do not cure an abscess** (source control). Look for an underlying fistula (do not aggressively probe acutely). Consider Crohn's, diabetes, immunosuppression, and — if the pain is severe with minimal external signs plus systemic sepsis in a diabetic/immunosuppressed — **necrotising perineal infection (Fournier's gangrene)**, a surgical emergency needing aggressive debridement.

**Fistula-in-ano:** chronic discharge, recurrent abscess; **Goodsall's rule** predicts the internal opening's track; classify (inter-/trans-/supra-/extra-sphincteric); treat by fistulotomy (low) or seton (high, to preserve continence). Exclude Crohn's and TB.

**Thrombosed external haemorrhoid ("perianal haematoma"):** acute painful tender blue perianal lump; if <48–72h and severe, excise/evacuate under local; later, conservative (analgesia, stool softeners, sitz baths) as it resolves.

**Prolapsed/strangulated internal haemorrhoids, fissure-in-ano** (posterior midline tear, painful defecation + PR bleeding + sentinel tag; GTN/diltiazem ointment, botulinum, lateral sphincterotomy for chronic).

**The missed rectal cancer — the consultant's anger:** attributing PR bleeding, tenesmus, or change in bowel habit to "haemorrhoids" without a PR and proctosigmoidoscopy. **Every PR bleed gets a finger and a scope.** Red flags: age >45–50, weight loss, iron-deficiency anaemia, altered bowel habit, tenesmus, a mass on PR, dark blood mixed with stool (vs bright coating = anal). **"Haemorrhoids do not cause anaemia and weight loss — cancer does."**

### 2.12 The acute scrotum — the time-critical miss

**The rule: torsion until proven otherwise, especially <25 years — and the testis dies in hours.**

**Differential [EK]:**
- **Testicular torsion** — sudden severe pain (often nocturnal/on waking), nausea/vomiting, **high-riding testis with horizontal/transverse lie, absent cremasteric reflex, negative Prehn's** (elevation does not relieve).
- **Epididymo-orchitis** — more gradual, dysuria/urethral discharge/UTI, fever, positive Prehn's (elevation relieves), cremasteric present; young sexually active = chlamydia/gonorrhoea, older = coliforms/UTI.
- **Torsion of a testicular appendage** — "blue dot sign", younger boys, localised upper-pole tenderness.
- Incarcerated inguinal hernia; epididymal cyst; hydrocele.
- **Testicular tumour** — usually painless, but can present acutely (bleed into tumour).
- Henoch-Schönlein / idiopathic scrotal oedema (children); **Fournier's gangrene**; referred pain (ureteric stone).

**Discriminators:** onset (abrupt = torsion), age (<25 favours torsion), cremasteric reflex (**absent in torsion**), Prehn's sign (relief on elevation favours epididymitis — unreliable, do not rely on it alone), fever/dysuria/discharge (epididymitis), the horizontal high-riding testis (torsion). **TWIST score** (§5) stratifies: high-risk → straight to theatre without imaging.

**Investigations:** **do NOT delay exploration for a Doppler ultrasound if the clinical picture is torsion** — imaging is for the equivocal case; US shows reduced/absent testicular flow in torsion (operator- and time-dependent — can be falsely reassuring). Urine dip (epididymitis).

**Management:** **surgical exploration within 6 hours of onset** — salvage ~90% <6h, ~50% at 6–12h, ~10% at 12–24h, near-zero beyond 24h. Detorse, assess viability, **fix BOTH testes (bilateral orchidopexy — the bell-clapper anomaly is bilateral)**; orchidectomy if non-viable. Epididymo-orchitis — antibiotics per SA STG (STI cover: ceftriaxone + doxycycline for the young; ciprofloxacin/co-amoxiclav for coliform in older), analgesia, scrotal support.

**Traps:** "it's probably epididymitis" in a teenager with 5 hours of pain is how a testis is lost — **when in doubt, explore.** A positive urine dip does not exclude torsion. Manual detorsion (open the book — medial to lateral) can be a temporising bridge but never replaces theatre.

### 2.13 The acutely ischaemic limb — the 6 Ps

**The 6 Ps [EK]:** **Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Perishingly cold (Poikilothermia), Paraesthesia, Paralysis.** The last two (sensorimotor deficit) signal a *threatened, time-critical* limb (Rutherford IIb) — **fixed mottling, paralysis and a woody/tense muscle mean irreversible (Rutherford III) → primary amputation, revascularising dead muscle causes lethal reperfusion.**

**Embolic vs thrombotic [EK]:**
- **Embolus** — sudden onset, no prior claudication, an obvious source (AF, recent MI, mural thrombus, aneurysm), a *normal contralateral* pulse examination → embolectomy (often ward-level under LA via a Fogarty catheter).
- **Thrombosis in situ** — on a background of chronic PVD (history of claudication, absent contralateral pulses, tissue changes; **less dramatic because collaterals exist**) → usually needs imaging + bypass/angioplasty.
- Other: aortic dissection extending, trauma, popliteal aneurysm thrombosis, thrombosed graft, phlegmasia cerulea dolens (massive iliofemoral venous thrombosis).

**Assessment:** palpate all pulses both limbs, capillary return, sensation/power, **hand-held Doppler signals (arterial and venous)**, ankle-brachial pressure index; the "6 Ps" and Rutherford class dictate tempo.

**Management:** **immediate IV heparin (anticoagulation) to prevent propagation** the moment the diagnosis is entertained; analgesia; urgent vascular referral. Viable (I) / marginally threatened (IIa) → imaging (CT angiography) + revascularisation (embolectomy, thrombolysis, bypass, angioplasty). Immediately threatened (IIb) → **emergency revascularisation (no time for thrombolysis).** Irreversible (III) → amputation. **Beware reperfusion — compartment syndrome (fasciotomy) and hyperkalaemia/rhabdomyolysis/AKI after restoring flow.**

**Traps:** the cold pulseless leg after 6 hours is losing muscle every hour — heparinise and refer *now*, don't wait for the morning list. Don't revascularise a dead (rigid, anaesthetic, fixed-mottling) limb.

### 2.14 The surgical neck / breast lump

**Groin lump [EK]** — the "lumps in the groin" differential every surgeon carries: inguinal hernia (above and medial to the pubic tubercle) vs **femoral hernia** (below and lateral — narrow neck, strangulates, commoner in women, easily missed), saphena varix (bluish, disappears lying down, cough impulse, thrill — a "blue emptying lump"), femoral artery aneurysm/pseudoaneurysm (pulsatile, expansile), enlarged inguinal lymph node (infection, STI, lymphoma, metastasis), lipoma of the cord, hydrocele/varicocele of the cord, psoas abscess pointing (TB), an undescended/ectopic testis. **Discriminators: relation to the pubic tubercle (inguinal vs femoral), cough impulse and reducibility (hernia), pulsatility (aneurysm), position on standing/lying (saphena varix), and whether both testes are in the scrotum.**

**Neck lump [EK]:** the discriminator is *site* + *movement* + *age*. Midline moving with swallowing = thyroid/thyroglossal (thyroglossal also moves with tongue protrusion); lateral pulsatile = carotid body tumour/aneurysm; anterior triangle = lymph node/branchial cyst; posterior triangle = lymph node (**TB lymphadenitis / "cold abscess" is common in SA — matted nodes, may point and discharge; also HIV lymphadenopathy, lymphoma, metastatic SCC**). Any hard fixed node in an adult over 40, or a persistent lump >3 weeks, needs triple assessment / FNA and ENT review to exclude malignancy. Thyroid: assess function, retrosternal extension, hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve → cancer).

**Breast lump [EK]:** **triple assessment** — clinical exam + imaging (US <35–40y, mammography ≥40y) + tissue (core biopsy). Common: fibroadenoma (young, mobile "breast mouse"), cyst, fibrocystic change. Must-not-miss: **carcinoma** (hard, irregular, fixed, skin tethering/peau d'orange, nipple retraction/discharge, axillary nodes), **inflammatory breast cancer** (masquerades as mastitis that doesn't settle — do not treat "mastitis" with repeated antibiotics in a non-lactating woman; biopsy). Abscess (lactational — drain + antibiotics + continue feeding/express). SA: present late/large; HIV alters the picture; consider TB of the breast (rare). Male breast lump — gynaecomastia vs carcinoma.

### 2.15 The diabetic foot / soft-tissue sepsis

**Diabetic foot sepsis:** neuropathy (loss of protective sensation, Charcot), ischaemia (PVD), and immunopathy combine — a trivial portal → deep spreading infection, often **painless** and under-appreciated. Assess: the wound (probe-to-bone = osteomyelitis likely), spreading cellulitis, crepitus/gas (necrotising/gas gangrene — emergency), pulses/ABPI, glucose control, systemic sepsis. **X-ray** (gas, osteomyelitis, foreign body), inflammatory markers, blood cultures, deep tissue culture. **Management: aggressive source control — incision, drainage, debridement of all dead tissue (do not "trial antibiotics" a collection), broad-spectrum antibiotics, glycaemic control, revascularisation assessment; amputation to control life-threatening sepsis.** "Control the sepsis first, worry about the level of amputation second."

**Necrotising fasciitis [EK]:** the great surgical emergency. **Pain out of proportion to visible signs**, rapid spread, systemic toxicity, then late skin changes (dusky, bullae, crepitus, anaesthesia over the area from nerve destruction, a "dishwater" grey exudate). Type I (polymicrobial — diabetics, perineum = Fournier's), Type II (Group A Strep ± Staph). **LRINEC score** aids suspicion (§5) but a normal score does not exclude it — **it is a clinical/surgical diagnosis.** **Treatment: immediate aggressive surgical debridement (repeated) + broad-spectrum antibiotics including clindamycin (anti-toxin) + resuscitation.** Delay kills — hours matter. Do not wait for imaging if the clinical picture is convincing.

### 2.16 Surgical (obstructive) jaundice

**The framework:** classify jaundice as **pre-hepatic** (haemolysis — unconjugated bilirubin ↑, normal LFTs, normal urine/stool), **hepatic** (hepatitis/cirrhosis — mixed, ALT/AST ↑), or **post-hepatic/obstructive** ("surgical" — conjugated bilirubin ↑, **ALP/GGT ↑↑, dark urine, pale stools, pruritus**).

**Obstructive causes [EK]:** stones (CBD — often painful, fluctuating, ± cholangitis), **malignancy — pancreatic head carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, ampullary carcinoma** (painless progressive jaundice + weight loss + **Courvoisier-positive** palpable non-tender gallbladder), Mirizzi, benign strictures, pancreatitis, porta hepatis nodes (including TB/lymphoma), parasites (rare).

**Workup:** LFTs (confirm cholestatic pattern), **coagulation/INR (obstructive jaundice → fat-soluble vitamin K malabsorption → coagulopathy — correct before any intervention), and renal function (hepatorenal risk).** **Ultrasound first** (dilated ducts, level of obstruction, stones, mass, CBD diameter). Then **CT / MRCP** to characterise a mass and stage; **ERCP** (therapeutic — stone extraction, stent, brushings) or **PTC**. Tumour markers (CA 19-9, CEA) as adjuncts.

**Management anchors:** stones → ERCP + cholecystectomy. Malignancy → staging then resection (Whipple's for resectable pancreatic head/ampullary) or palliative biliary stenting. **Always correct coagulopathy (vitamin K/FFP) and hydrate (prevent hepatorenal AKI — "renal protection" with fluids) before instrumentation.** Relieve pruritus.

**Traps:** the painless jaundiced patient with a palpable gallbladder is cancer, not stones (Courvoisier). Never puncture/operate a coagulopathic obstructed patient without correcting clotting. Dilated ducts on US localise the problem — no dilatation early does not fully exclude obstruction.

### 2.17 Trauma — blunt and penetrating (the SA bread-and-butter)

**Framework: ATLS.** Primary survey **<C>ABCDE** — **Catastrophic haemorrhage** control (tourniquet/pressure — SA penetrating trauma), **Airway with C-spine control, Breathing, Circulation with haemorrhage control, Disability (GCS/pupils), Exposure/Environment.** Identify and treat the immediately life-threatening: **ATOM-FC** — Airway obstruction, **Tension pneumothorax, Open pneumothorax, Massive haemothorax, Flail chest, Cardiac tamponade** — clinically, before imaging.

**Shock in trauma [EK]:** assume haemorrhagic until proven otherwise; the sources of major blood loss — **"blood on the floor and four more": chest, abdomen, pelvis, long bones (thighs), and external.** ATLS haemorrhagic shock classes I–IV by physiology (see §5). Young patients compensate then crash — a narrowing pulse pressure and tachycardia precede hypotension.

**The immediately life-threatening thoracic injuries and their bedside fix [EK]:**
- **Tension pneumothorax** — clinical diagnosis (respiratory distress, absent breath sounds + hyper-resonance, tracheal deviation away, distended neck veins, hypotension); **immediate needle decompression (2nd ICS mid-clavicular or 4th/5th ICS anterior axillary) then chest drain — do NOT wait for a CXR.**
- **Open pneumothorax ("sucking chest wound")** — three-sided occlusive dressing (flutter valve) + chest drain.
- **Massive haemothorax** — >1500 mL initial drain output or >200 mL/h ongoing → theatre (thoracotomy).
- **Cardiac tamponade** — Beck's triad (hypotension, muffled heart sounds, distended neck veins); FAST/echo shows pericardial fluid → pericardiocentesis/thoracotomy. Common in penetrating precordial wounds.
- **Flail chest** — paradoxical segment + underlying pulmonary contusion; analgesia, oxygen, ventilatory support.

**Blunt abdominal trauma:** spleen and liver commonest; assess by **FAST** (Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma — free fluid in Morrison's pouch, splenorenal recess, pelvis, pericardium — **positive FAST + unstable = laparotomy; a negative FAST does NOT exclude injury, especially hollow viscus/retroperitoneal/diaphragm**), and **CT in the stable patient** (grades solid-organ injury, guides non-operative management). **Non-operative management of splenic/liver injury (AAST grade) in the *haemodynamically stable* patient is now standard** — with a monitored bed, serial obs/Hb, and a surgeon; angioembolisation salvages selected higher grades. Post-splenectomy → **OPSI prophylaxis (vaccination against encapsulated organisms — pneumococcus, meningococcus, Hib — and consider penicillin prophylaxis).**

**Pelvic fracture haemorrhage:** an unstable "open-book" pelvis bleeds massively into the retroperitoneum — **apply a pelvic binder at the level of the greater trochanters early** (do not spring the pelvis repeatedly), resuscitate with blood, and escalate to angioembolisation / pre-peritoneal packing / external fixation. Associated urethral injury (blood at meatus, high-riding prostate — do not catheter blindly).

**Penetrating trauma (SA-dominant):** **gunshot** → most abdominal GSWs need laparotomy (high visceral injury rate); trajectory matters. **Stab** → selective non-operative management in the stable patient without evisceration/peritonitis/haemodynamic instability (serial examination); **anterior abdominal stab with evisceration, peritonitis, or instability → laparotomy.** Thoraco-abdominal wounds threaten the diaphragm. **Neck zones (I–III)** guide management of penetrating neck injury; **"hard signs"** (expanding haematoma, active bleeding, bruit/thrill, airway compromise, neuro deficit) → theatre.

**Damage-control surgery [EK]:** in the exsanguinating/coagulopathic patient — the **"lethal triad": hypothermia + acidosis + coagulopathy.** Abbreviated laparotomy: **control haemorrhage (pack) and contamination (staple/clamp bowel), leave the abdomen open (temporary closure), resuscitate and correct physiology in ICU, then return to theatre in 24–48h for definitive repair.** **Damage-control resuscitation:** permissive hypotension (until surgical control), **balanced blood-product transfusion (~1:1:1 RBC:FFP:platelets), tranexamic acid (within 3h — CRASH-2), minimise crystalloid, keep warm, correct calcium.** Massive transfusion protocol activation.

**Documentation/medico-legal:** the **J88** form for all assault/GBV/GSW/stab — accurate, objective injury documentation is a legal obligation and evidence.

### 2.18 Burns

**Assessment:** **depth** (superficial/epidermal, superficial & deep partial-thickness, full-thickness — full-thickness is painless, leathery, non-blanching), and **%TBSA** by the **Rule of Nines** (adult: head 9, each arm 9, each leg 18, anterior trunk 18, posterior trunk 18, perineum 1) or the patient's **palm = ~1%**; **do not count simple erythema in TBSA.** Assess for **inhalation/airway injury** (facial/neck burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in sputum, hoarseness, stridor, enclosed-space fire — **intubate early before oedema closes the airway**) and **carbon monoxide/cyanide** (measure carboxyhaemoglobin; SpO₂ reads falsely normal in CO poisoning).

**Fluid resuscitation — Parkland:** **4 mL × weight(kg) × %TBSA of Ringer's lactate over 24h from the time of the burn — half in the first 8 hours, half in the next 16** (paediatric formulas add maintenance ± dextrose). **The formula is a starting estimate — titrate to urine output 0.5 mL/kg/h in adults (~1 mL/kg/h in children).** Resuscitation is indicated for **>15–20% TBSA in adults (>10% children).** Avoid over-resuscitation ("fluid creep" → compartment syndrome/ARDS).

**Referral to a burns unit (major burns) [EK ± SA burns guidelines]:** partial-thickness **>10–15% TBSA**, any **full-thickness** burn, burns of **face, hands, feet, perineum/genitalia, major joints**, **circumferential** burns (escharotomy risk), **inhalation** injury, **electrical (incl. lightning) and chemical** burns, burns with trauma or significant comorbidity, extremes of age, and **non-accidental injury** suspicion (children — a notifiable child-protection concern).

**Other:** analgesia (opioids), tetanus prophylaxis, escharotomy for circumferential full-thickness limb/chest burns (compartment/ventilation compromise), early nutrition (hypermetabolic), infection surveillance (avoid prophylactic systemic antibiotics), cling-film/clean dressing for transfer.

### 2.19 The incarcerated / strangulated hernia

**Definitions [EK]:** **reducible** (contents return), **irreducible/incarcerated** (stuck, but blood supply intact), **obstructed** (incarcerated + causing bowel obstruction), **strangulated** (blood supply compromised → ischaemia → gangrene → perforation — the emergency). **Femoral hernias** (below and lateral to the pubic tubercle; commoner in women; narrow neck) strangulate far more often than inguinal and are more often missed.

**Red flags for strangulation:** a **tender, tense, irreducible, non-transilluminating** hernia with overlying erythema, **pain over the lump**, and signs of bowel obstruction ± systemic toxicity (tachycardia, fever, raised lactate/WCC).

**Management:** a **non-tender, recently irreducible hernia** without obstruction/ischaemia may have a **gentle single attempt at reduction** (taxis). **A tender/strangulated hernia is NOT reduced** — reduction-en-masse can return dead bowel into the abdomen; take it to theatre to assess viability, resect if needed, and repair. Resuscitate, NG if obstructed, IV fluids, antibiotics, urgent surgery.

**Special: Richter's hernia** — only part of the bowel circumference is trapped → can strangulate and perforate **without** full obstruction (deceptively few obstructive symptoms). **Maydl's hernia** — a "W" loop with the strangulated segment inside the abdomen. **Littre's hernia** — a Meckel's in the sac.

**Traps:** examine hernial orifices in every SBO. Never forcibly reduce a tender hernia. The obese groin hides the femoral hernia — palpate carefully below the inguinal ligament.

### 2.20 Indication-for-surgery reasoning & pre-operative workup

**The indication logic:** every operation is justified by *diagnosis → indication → risk-benefit → consent*. The four broad surgical indications: **relieve obstruction, control haemorrhage, achieve source control of sepsis (drain/resect), or resect/repair (tumour, ischaemia, perforation).** Elective operations add: symptom relief, cancer cure, function. **Ask "what happens if I don't operate?"** — the answer defines urgency (immediate/urgent/expedited/elective).

**Fitness assessment:**
- **Functional capacity in METs** — **can they climb two flights of stairs (~4 METs)?** <4 METs (unable) predicts higher peri-operative cardiac risk and triggers further cardiac assessment.
- **Cardiac risk** — **Revised Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI, Lee):** one point each for high-risk surgery (intraperitoneal/intrathoracic/suprainguinal vascular), ischaemic heart disease, congestive cardiac failure, cerebrovascular disease, insulin-treated diabetes, and creatinine >177 µmol/L; more factors → higher peri-operative major cardiac event rate. **Active cardiac conditions** (unstable angina, recent MI, decompensated failure, significant arrhythmia, severe symptomatic valve disease — especially severe aortic stenosis) demand optimisation/deferral of elective surgery and cardiology input. Defer elective non-cardiac surgery after MI (traditionally ≥4–6 weeks / until stable).
- **Respiratory** — COPD/asthma control, smoking (stop, ideally weeks before), OSA; risk of post-op atelectasis/pneumonia.
- **Comorbidity optimisation** — **diabetes** (glycaemic control, HbA1c, peri-op insulin/sliding scale, first on the list), **renal** (electrolytes, timing around dialysis), **anaemia** (correct before elective surgery), **hypertension**, **liver disease** (Child-Pugh — coagulopathy, ascites), **thyroid**.
- **Anaesthetic history** — previous anaesthetics/complications, PONV, difficult airway/Mallampati, **family history of malignant hyperthermia or suxamethonium apnoea**, allergies (see Anaesthetics dossier).

**Peri-operative essentials:**
- **Consent** — informed, capacity, the specific risks and alternatives, the operation named, site/side marked.
- **NPO/fasting** — **6 hours solids, 2 hours clear fluids** (elective); assume full stomach in emergencies/trauma/obstruction (rapid sequence induction, aspiration risk).
- **Group-and-save / crossmatch** — crossmatch for anticipated blood loss; group-and-save with a valid sample; check availability (SA — variable blood stock).
- **Anticoagulation/antiplatelet management** — stop/bridge by indication and bleeding risk (mechanical valve/recent VTE = bridge; AF may just pause; DOACs timed to renal function); balance against thrombotic risk.
- **VTE prophylaxis** — risk-assess; mechanical (TEDS/intermittent compression) + pharmacological (LMWH) unless contraindicated; the immobile surgical patient is high-risk.
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis** — the right agent at the right time (within 60 min of incision) per SA STG for the operation class (clean/clean-contaminated/contaminated/dirty).
- **Bowel prep** (selected colorectal), catheter, NG, glucose plan, DVT stockings, and the **WHO Surgical Safety Checklist**.

### 2.21 The post-operative day framework (day-specific complications)

**The signature pearl: TACHYCARDIA BEFORE PERITONISM.** In the post-op abdomen, a rising heart rate and rising early-warning score precede the classic signs of a leak. **A persistently tachycardic post-op patient has a complication (bleed, leak, sepsis, PE) until proven otherwise — investigate, don't just give analgesia and fluids.** "The pulse is the surgeon's early warning."

**The "5 Ws" of post-op fever, by timing [EK]:**
- **Day 0–2 — Wind (atelectasis, then early pneumonia):** basal atelectasis is the classic early low-grade fever + reduced air entry; encourage deep breathing/physio/incentive spirometry, mobilise, analgesia to allow coughing.
- **Day 3–5 — Water (UTI, especially catheter-related):** dysuria, catheter, growing organisms; and **Wind → pneumonia.**
- **Day 3–5 — Walking (DVT) / later Wind (PE):** calf swelling; PE = tachycardia, hypoxia, pleuritic pain, collapse — **the tachycardic, hypoxic post-op patient on day 3–7.** Wells score, D-dimer of limited use post-op, CTPA.
- **Day 5–7 — Wound (surgical site infection):** erythema, discharge, dehiscence; also the deeper collection.
- **Day 5–7 and beyond — the deep abscess / collection and the ANASTOMOTIC LEAK:** the most feared. **Anastomotic leak classically presents around day 5–7** with fever, tachycardia, ileus that won't resolve, rising CRP after day 4, and **a change in drain output to bilious/faeculent/turbid**; can be insidious (just "not right", tachycardic, confused elderly) or catastrophic (peritonitis/sepsis). **CT with contrast** confirms; management = resuscitate, antibiotics, drainage (radiological) or re-look laparotomy ± defunctioning stoma. **Wonder drugs (the sixth W) — drug fever; and "What did we do to them" — lines/transfusion.**

**Other day-specific issues [EK]:**
- **Post-op ileus** (physiological up to ~3–5 days for colonic surgery) vs **mechanical obstruction / leak** — prolonged ileus beyond day 5, with pain/tachycardia/rising CRP, is a leak until excluded; don't just "wait for it to settle."
- **Post-op bleeding** — **primary** (in theatre/immediately), **reactionary** (first 24h — slipped tie, rising BP dislodges clot), **secondary** (day 7–14 — infection erodes a vessel). Falling Hb + tachycardia + hypotension → resuscitate + return to theatre conversation.
- **Post-op AKI/oliguria** (pre-renal from under-resuscitation/third-spacing most common — assess fluid status before reaching for diuretics), **electrolyte disturbance, delirium** (elderly — hypoxia, sepsis, pain, drugs, retention), **urinary retention.**

**The tachycardia-Hb-drain triad the consultant lives by:** *HR up + Hb down = bleed → theatre; HR up + CRP up after day 4 + drain turned bilious = leak → CT/theatre.* Serial numbers, not single ones.

## 3. The normals

**Vital-sign / physiology anchors [EK]:**
- HR 60–100 (a post-op HR persistently >100 is a red flag). BP: MAP >65 for organ perfusion. Urine output target **≥0.5 mL/kg/h** (adult) — the simplest resuscitation endpoint; **1 mL/kg/h in children and burns**.
- Respiratory rate 12–20 (tachypnoea is an early, often-missed sign of sepsis/acidosis/PE).
- Base deficit: normal ±2; a base deficit worse than −6 in trauma signals significant shock/lactic acidosis.

**Bloods:**
- Hb (male 13–17, female 12–15 g/dL); a post-op drop of ~1 g/dL can be dilutional — the *trend + haemodynamics* decide.
- WCC 4–11 ×10⁹/L; CRP <5 mg/L (rises 6–12h after an insult, **peaks post-op day 2–3, then falls — a rising CRP after day 4 signals a complication**).
- Lactate <2 mmol/L (2–4 = watch, >4 = significant hypoperfusion/possible ischaemia).
- Amylase — up to ~3× ULN non-specific; **>3× ULN supports pancreatitis** (lipase more specific, longer window).
- INR ~1.0 (obstructive jaundice/liver disease/warfarin prolong it — correct before surgery).
- Urea/creatinine — surgical relevance: dehydration/third-spacing (urea rises disproportionately), upper GI bleed (urea rises from digested blood protein load).

**Anatomy/measurement normals [EK]:**
- **CBD diameter ≤6 mm** on US (add ~1 mm/decade over 60; up to ~10 mm post-cholecystectomy) — above this = obstruction.
- Bowel calibre on imaging: **small bowel <3 cm, colon <6 cm, caecum <9 cm** — beyond these = obstruction; **caecum >12 cm = impending perforation.**
- Appendix diameter >6 mm (non-compressible) on US = abnormal.
- Aorta: infrarenal >3 cm = aneurysmal; >5.5 cm (or rapid growth/symptoms) = repair threshold.
- Free peritoneal air is never normal (except recent laparotomy — resolves over ~1 week).

## 4. Cross-cutting investigation interpretation (deep)

### 4.1 The acute-abdomen investigation ladder
1. **Bedside/physiology first:** vitals + **early-warning score (TEWS/NEWS)**, glucose, **βhCG (every woman of reproductive age — mandatory)**, urine dip, ECG (the MI mimic), venous/arterial gas + **lactate**.
2. **Bloods:** FBC (WCC, Hb baseline), CRP, **UEC** (dehydration/AKI/electrolytes for correction), **amylase/lipase**, LFTs (biliary), **coagulation + group-and-save/crossmatch**, ± troponin.
3. **Erect CXR** — **free air under the diaphragm** (perforation; present in ~70–80% only — absence does not exclude), and it catches the pneumonia/effusion mimic.
4. **AXR** (selective) — obstruction pattern, volvulus, foreign body, radio-opaque stones (only ~10% of gallstones, ~90% of renal stones are visible).
5. **Ultrasound** — biliary (stones/CBD/gallbladder), free fluid/FAST, gynae (ectopic/ovarian/tubo-ovarian), appendix (thin/young), AAA, hydronephrosis.
6. **CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast** — the workhorse for the undifferentiated/atypical/older acute abdomen: level and cause of obstruction, ischaemia signs, abscess/collection, free air, appendix, diverticulitis grading, vascular (CTA for mesenteric ischaemia/AAA).

**Interpretation nuances:** amylase can be raised by perforation and mesenteric ischaemia (not just pancreatitis); a normal WCC/CRP does not exclude early appendicitis; the absence of free air does not exclude perforation; lactate is a *late* marker in ischaemia; sterile pyuria can accompany appendicitis; a "renal colic" over 60 needs an aorta.

### 4.2 The septic post-op screen (fever/deterioration workup)
- **Re-examine, in person** — wound, drains, lines, calves, chest, abdomen, catheter.
- **The 5 Ws mapped to day (see §2.21).**
- **Cultures: blood ×2, urine, sputum, wound swab, drain fluid, line tips**; **CRP + WCC trend** (rising after day 4 = complication); lactate; procalcitonin (where available).
- **Imaging: CXR** (pneumonia/collection), **CT abdomen with contrast** for a suspected collection/leak (the key study for the deteriorating post-op abdomen), Doppler for DVT, CTPA for PE.
- **Source control is the priority** — drain the collection, not just escalate antibiotics.

### 4.3 Drain-output interpretation
- **Volume trend + character trend** both matter. Serous (straw) → normal. **Sanguineous (frank blood) + rising volume + tachycardia = post-op bleed.** **Bilious/green = biliary or upper-GI anastomotic leak.** **Faeculent/turbid brown = bowel/lower anastomotic leak.** Cloudy/pus = infected collection. **Milky = chyle leak** (thoracic duct/lymphatic). **High clear output after urological/renal surgery = urine leak** (check drain-fluid creatinine — markedly higher than serum confirms urine).
- **A sudden change in drain character is an alarm** — investigate the leak.

### 4.4 The post-op Hb drift
- Serial Hb + haemodynamics. A gentle predictable fall (dilution/expected loss) that stabilises is benign. **A falling Hb + rising HR + falling BP + rising lactate = ongoing haemorrhage → resuscitate (crossmatched blood) and the return-to-theatre conversation.** Do not chase the Hb with transfusion while ignoring an unstable patient who needs the bleeding stopped. Correlate with drain output and abdominal distension.

### 4.5 Fluid & electrolyte management in the surgical patient
- **Assess the deficit + ongoing losses + maintenance.** Surgical patients lose to the "third space" (bowel wall/lumen, peritoneum), the NG, the drains, the stoma, the fistula, and the fever — these are **isotonic** losses; replace with a **balanced crystalloid (Ringer's lactate / Plasmalyte), not 5% dextrose.** Maintenance (roughly 25–30 mL/kg/day water, ~1 mmol/kg/day Na⁺ and K⁺) is separate from resuscitation.
- **The classic surgical electrolyte picture: hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis** — from persistent vomiting or high NG loss (loss of H⁺ and Cl⁻ gastric juice), the paradigm being **gastric outlet obstruction (pyloric stenosis / a chronic scarring duodenal ulcer)**; the kidney worsens the K⁺ loss (exchanging K⁺/H⁺ for Na⁺ under aldosterone), giving **paradoxical aciduria**. **Correct with 0.9% saline + potassium** (chloride-rich fluid replaces the deficit and allows the kidney to correct the alkalosis).
- **High-output stoma/fistula** → sodium and volume depletion, hypomagnesaemia, hypokalaemia, AKI; measure and replace losses, treat magnesium (needed to correct refractory hypokalaemia).
- **Post-op oliguria** is **pre-renal (under-resuscitation/third-spacing) until proven otherwise** — give a fluid challenge and reassess, do not default to a diuretic. Beware **over-resuscitation** (bowel-wall oedema delays ileus resolution, worsens anastomotic healing, causes pulmonary oedema). **Urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/h is the bedside endpoint.**
- **Refeeding risk** in the malnourished/chronic-alcohol/prolonged-NPO patient — watch phosphate, potassium, magnesium on re-introduction of feeding.

## 5. Scores, charts, and structured tools

**Alvarado score (appendicitis) — MANTRELS, /10:** Migration of pain (1), Anorexia (1), Nausea/vomiting (1), Tenderness RIF (2), Rebound (1), Elevated temp ≥37.3 (1), Leucocytosis >10 (2), Shift to left / neutrophilia (1). **Interpretation: 1–4 low, 5–6 intermediate (observe/image), 7–10 high (surgical).** Good at ruling out at <5; specificity poor at ruling in (over-operates women). *[grounded]*

**Glasgow-Imrie score (acute pancreatitis severity, at 48h) — PANCREAS, /8; ≥3 = severe:** **P**aO₂ <60 mmHg (<8 kPa), **A**ge >55, **N**eutrophils/WCC >15×10⁹/L, **C**alcium <2.0 mmol/L, **R**enal urea >16 mmol/L, **E**nzymes LDH >600 IU/L (or AST >200), **A**lbumin <32 g/L, **S**ugar/glucose >10 mmol/L. **Re-score at 48h.** *[grounded]* **Ranson (US alternative):** 5 criteria on admission (age >55, WCC >16, glucose >11, LDH >350, AST >250) + 6 at 48h (Hct fall >10%, urea rise >1.8 mmol/L, Ca <2.0, PaO₂ <60, base deficit >4, fluid sequestration >6 L); ≥3 = severe. **Modern severity also uses CRP >150 at 48h, persistent organ failure (modified Marshall), and BISAP.** *[EK]*

**Boey score (perforated peptic ulcer mortality), /3:** one point each for **pre-op shock (SBP <100), major medical comorbidity, duration of perforation >24h.** Rising score → rising mortality. *[grounded]*

**ASA physical status (I–VI):** I healthy; II mild systemic disease; III severe systemic disease (functional limitation); IV severe disease that is a constant threat to life; V moribund, not expected to survive without operation; VI brain-dead organ donor; **"E" suffix = emergency** (higher risk). *[EK]*

**POSSUM (concept):** Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the enUmeration of Mortality and morbidity — combines a **physiological score** (age, cardiac/respiratory signs, BP, pulse, GCS, Hb, WCC, urea, Na, K, ECG) with an **operative severity score** (procedure magnitude, blood loss, peritoneal soiling, malignancy, urgency) to predict morbidity/mortality; used for audit/risk-adjusted comparison (tends to over-predict in low-risk). *[EK]*

**Rockall (UGIB mortality) — pre-endoscopy + full:** age, shock (HR/BP), comorbidity + (endoscopic) diagnosis and stigmata of recent haemorrhage; higher = higher rebleed/mortality. **Glasgow-Blatchford (GBS):** urea, Hb, SBP, HR, melaena, syncope, hepatic/cardiac disease — **pre-endoscopy; score 0–1 = low risk, may manage as outpatient.** *[EK]*

**Wells score (DVT/PE):** clinical probability for VTE (e.g. PE: signs of DVT, PE most likely diagnosis, HR >100, immobilisation/surgery in prior 4 weeks, previous VTE, haemoptysis, malignancy) → stratifies to guide D-dimer vs imaging (D-dimer near useless in the early post-op patient — go to CTPA/Doppler). *[EK]*

**TWIST (testicular torsion), /7:** testicular swelling (2), hard testis (2), absent cremasteric reflex (1), nausea/vomiting (1), high-riding testis (1). **0–2 low (torsion unlikely), 3–4 intermediate (US), 5–7 high → straight to exploration.** *[grounded]*

**Parkland formula (burns):** 4 mL × kg × %TBSA Ringer's lactate/24h, half in first 8h; titrate to urine output 0.5 mL/kg/h (adult). *[grounded]*

**Rule of Nines (TBSA):** head 9, each arm 9, each leg 18, anterior trunk 18, posterior trunk 18, perineum 1; palm ≈ 1%. *[EK]*

**Gustilo-Anderson (open fracture — cross-ref Orthopaedics):** I <1 cm clean; II 1–10 cm moderate; **IIIA** adequate soft-tissue cover; **IIIB** extensive loss needing flap; **IIIC** arterial injury needing repair — antibiotics + urgent debridement, IIIC threatens the limb. *[EK]*

**Mangled Extremity Severity Score (MESS), /12 (limb salvage vs amputation):** skeletal/soft-tissue injury energy (1–4), limb ischaemia (1–3, ×2 if >6h), shock (0–2), age (0–2). **≥7 traditionally suggests amputation** (a guide, not a mandate — decision is clinical/multidisciplinary). *[EK]*

**LRINEC (necrotising fasciitis suspicion):** CRP, WCC, Hb, Na, creatinine, glucose; ≥6 suspicious, ≥8 high — **an adjunct only; a low score never excludes it (clinical/surgical diagnosis).** *[EK]*

**ATLS haemorrhagic shock classes (approx. blood loss):** I <15% (normal vitals, mild anxiety); II 15–30% (tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure, anxious); III 30–40% (hypotension, tachycardia, confusion, oliguria); IV >40% (profound hypotension, obtunded, anuria — immediately life-threatening). *[EK]*

**Charts to build structurally:** WHO Surgical Safety Checklist (Sign-in / Time-out / Sign-out — *grounded*), fluid-balance chart (ins/outs/drains/NG/stoma/urine), post-op observation/early-warning (TEWS/NEWS), wound/pressure-injury (Braden), VTE risk assessment, Hinchey grade (diverticulitis I–IV), Rutherford acute-limb-ischaemia class (I/IIa/IIb/III).

## 6. SA-specific reality

**Trauma burden.** SA has one of the world's highest **interpersonal-violence and RTC** burdens — penetrating trauma (stab, gunshot) dominates the acute surgical intake, especially weekends/month-end ("payday"). Every unit runs damage-control and massive-transfusion pathways; blood-product supply is finite (SANBS/WCBS) — **group-and-save early, activate MTP judiciously, use tranexamic acid within 3h.** **The J88 medico-legal form** is a legal obligation for all assault/GBV/GSW/stab — objective, contemporaneous injury documentation is evidence and is frequently subpoenaed. Chest drains for stab chests are a ward-level skill.

**HIV/TB abdomen.** Consider **abdominal TB** in any unexplained ascites, mesenteric/omental mass, matted lymphadenopathy, ileocaecal mass (mimics caecal cancer and appendicitis), or subacute obstruction from a **TB stricture** — ascitic ADA, tissue for GeneXpert/TB culture, and a low threshold for it. **TB lymphadenitis** ("cold abscess") is the commonest surgical neck-node cause in the young. **HIV** blunts inflammatory signs (silent abdomen), impairs wound healing (dehiscence), changes the differential at low CD4 (**CMV colitis/enteritis — can perforate**, lymphoma, KS), and coexists with ordinary surgical disease — do not attribute everything to HIV. Amoebic liver abscess and schistosomal portal hypertension (variceal bleeds) are endemic.

**Resource-limited imaging & services.** CT is often **not available after hours or at district level** — the acute abdomen, appendicitis, and trauma laparotomy are frequently **clinical decisions taken to theatre without cross-sectional imaging**; ultrasound (FAST, biliary) and the erect CXR carry more weight. ERCP, interventional radiology (image-guided drainage, angioembolisation), and vascular surgery are **regional/tertiary** — the ischaemic limb, ruptured AAA, cholangitis needing drainage, and the bleed needing embolisation often require **referral and transfer**, which costs time the pathology doesn't have. Know your referral pathways and phone early.

**Referral realities & the district lens.** The district hospital resuscitates, decompresses (NG, catheter), gives antibiotics, drains superficial sepsis, does the emergency laparotomy for the unstable penetrating abdomen and the obvious perforation, and **stabilises-and-transfers** for the specialist need (vascular, HPB, complex trauma, paediatric surgery, ERCP). Delayed presentation is the norm — appendicitis presents perforated, cancers present obstructed, hernias present strangulated — so the "textbook early" picture is the exception.

**Formulary/notifiable.** Antibiotic choices follow the **Adult Hospital Level STG & EML** (surgical prophylaxis within 60 min of incision; therapeutic escalation for perforation/faecal contamination; STI cover for epididymitis). **Tetanus-prone wounds** → tetanus prophylaxis per EPI status (± immunoglobulin). **Notifiable/child-protection:** suspected NAI (burns/injuries in children), and public-health notifiable conditions where relevant. **Consent, the WHO checklist, site-marking, and the J88** are the medico-legal touchpoints that recur in every complaint and inquest.
