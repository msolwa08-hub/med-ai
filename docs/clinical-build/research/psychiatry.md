# Psychiatry — Consultant Depth Dossier

*Target reader: the intern/MO covering psychiatry — often solo, often after hours, often at a
district hospital with no psychiatrist on site and a 72-hour assessment clock already running —
and the MedAI engine standing behind them. This is the depth reference, not a summary. It goes
deeper than `docs/clinical-foundations.md` (Psychiatry) — that page is the breadth scaffold; this
is the reasoning underneath it.*

*Primary SA sources: **Mental Health Care Act 17 of 2002** and its Regulations (the prescribed
MHCA forms, the 72-hour assessment, the three admission categories, the Mental Health Review
Board); **Adult Hospital Level STGs & EML** (NDoH) Mental Health/Psychiatry chapter; **SASOP**
(South African Society of Psychiatrists) practice guidance; **Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977**
ss 77–79 (fitness to stand trial, criminal capacity, state patients); **SACENDU** (South African
Community Epidemiology Network on Drug Use) reports; **2023 ART Clinical Guidelines (NDoH)** for
the HIV-neuropsychiatric overlap. Global: **DSM-5-TR** diagnostic framework, **Maudsley
Prescribing Guidelines in Psychiatry**, **Oxford Handbook of Psychiatry**, standard toxicology/
psychiatric-emergency references for NMS/serotonin syndrome/lithium toxicity/dystonia. Items
resting on established knowledge are marked [EK]. Full source ledger at the end.*

## 1. The psychiatrist's mental model

**The organic exclusion FIRST reflex.** The single sentence that separates a consultant from a
junior in this discipline: *it is not psychiatric until medical, neurological, and toxic causes
have been excluded* — and this reflex re-fires at every new presentation, not just the first one
a patient ever has. A known schizophrenic can develop delirium from a UTI; a known bipolar patient
can present with lithium toxicity, not relapse; a "chronic" patient's new confusion is a new
problem, not a continuation of the old one. This matters practically, not just academically: an
antipsychotic sedates a patient with an undiagnosed subdural or hypoglycaemia and can mask
deterioration or precipitate harm (NMS, respiratory depression) in a brain that was never
primarily psychiatric to begin with; a psychiatric ward or 72-hour-assessment bed typically has
far less physiological monitoring than a medical ward, so getting this wrong before admission has
real consequences. Red flags that shift the probability sharply toward an organic/secondary cause
rather than a primary psychiatric disorder [EK]: first psychotic episode or first manic episode
after age 40–45; abrupt onset over hours-to-days without a clear psychosocial precipitant;
fluctuating consciousness or impaired attention (delirium, not psychiatric illness — see §2.3);
abnormal vital signs; focal neurological signs; prominent visual or tactile hallucinations (more
typical of organic/toxic/delirium than of primary psychosis, though not absolute); catatonia;
incontinence; new headache or seizures; a known systemic illness (HIV, autoimmune disease,
endocrine disease); no prior psychiatric history; rapid cognitive decline; abnormal movements.

**Risk assessment as continuous cognition, not a form filled in once.** Risk to self (suicide,
self-harm), risk to others (violence, homicide), risk of self-neglect (grave disability — the
inability to provide for one's own food, shelter, safety, or medical care), and risk to
dependents (children or others in the patient's care) are re-assessed at *every* contact — each
ward round, each medication change, each piece of new collateral, each MSE — because risk is
dynamic. It rises around discharge, around anniversaries and dates of significance, after bad
news, after intoxication, in the window immediately after a patient discloses suicidal ideation,
and — counter-intuitively — as energy returns during recovery from severe depression, before mood
fully lifts (a previously too-depressed-to-act patient becomes able to act). A formulation must
state the *current* risk level and how the plan answers it, not merely record the history of risk.

**The MSE is the examination.** The mental state examination is to psychiatry what the
cardiovascular, respiratory, and abdominal examinations are to medicine: the objective,
structured, reproducible act of data-gathering performed at every single encounter, distinct from
the history (subjective symptoms) and from collateral (someone else's account). Juniors under-
weight it as a formality to complete after the "real" conversation; consultants read it like an
ECG — a structured signal that is often more reliable than what the patient says, especially when
insight is impaired. The full structure and what normal looks like in each domain is in §3.

**Capacity and the Mental Health Care Act as core clinical reasoning, not paperwork.** Capacity —
the ability to understand, retain, weigh/use, and communicate a decision (§3) — is decision-
specific and time-specific, and it is the hinge on which every disposition decision turns:
voluntary care, assisted care (unable to consent, does not refuse), or involuntary care (refuses,
or unable to consent, and poses a risk or needs treatment otherwise unobtainable) under the MHCA
(full detail in §6). This is not a legal technicality bolted onto clinical care — the capacity
assessment *is* clinical reasoning: a diagnosis of schizophrenia does not equal incapacity; an
involuntary patient can retain capacity for other decisions (what to eat, whether to consent to
an unrelated operation, §2.11); capacity must be actively assessed and documented for the
specific decision in front of you, never assumed from a diagnostic label.

**The biopsychosocial formulation.** The differential diagnosis is only half the job. The other
half is the formulation across **predisposing** factors (genetic loading, early adversity,
personality, chronic illness), **precipitating** factors (the proximate trigger — substance use,
loss, non-adherence, postpartum state, infection, a new diagnosis), **perpetuating** factors
(ongoing stressors, continued substance use, non-adherence, unresolved trauma, social
circumstances), and **protective** factors (support network, insight, engagement, coping skills,
reasons for living). Two patients with an identical diagnostic label can need very different
management once the formulation is added — a major depressive episode precipitated by
bereavement with strong family support is a different management problem from the same episode on
a background of ongoing intimate-partner violence with no support, even though the DSM label is
identical.

**Tempo maps to risk and reversibility, not to organ threat.** *Immediate* (minutes): the patient
about to harm self or others right now — the de-escalation/rapid-tranquillisation clock (§2.2).
*Acute* (hours): a first presentation needing organic exclusion before any psychiatric label
sticks, and the 72-hour MHCA assessment clock once triggered. *Subacute* (days–weeks): treatment-
response assessment, capacity reassessment, and the pharmacological reality that antidepressants
take 2–6 weeks, antipsychotics act on agitation within hours but take days–weeks for full
antipsychotic effect, and mood stabilisers need days to reach a steady-state level. *Chronic*
(months–years): relapse prevention, adherence, psychosocial rehabilitation, and — critically —
the physical health of the chronically mentally ill, whose life expectancy is reduced by roughly
15–20 years [EK], overwhelmingly from preventable cardiometabolic disease rather than from the
psychiatric illness or suicide itself (§2.11).

**The two-chart reflex.** Read the drug chart and the physical observations chart as psychiatric
data, not someone else's job. New confusion plus a newly-charted anticholinergic is the
anticholinergic until proven otherwise. New agitation in a patient who has missed doses of a
regular sedative, opioid, or alcohol is withdrawal, not "relapse of psychosis." A tachycardic,
anxious patient may have an explanation sitting on the drug chart (salbutamol, corticosteroids,
stimulants) rather than a new anxiety disorder.

**What the psychiatrist is always secretly ruling out** [EK] — the parallel list to internal
medicine's:
- **Hypoglycaemia** in any acutely agitated or confused patient — a glucose before a diagnosis.
- **Delirium wearing a psychiatric mask** — inattention plus fluctuation is the tell (§2.3).
- **Intoxication or withdrawal** (alcohol, benzodiazepines, methamphetamine, cannabis, opioids) as
  the actual driver of the mental state.
- **A prescribed or OTC drug as the cause** — steroids (mania/psychosis), isoniazid (psychosis),
  efavirenz (vivid dreams/depression/psychosis), anticholinergics (delirium), dopamine agonists
  (psychosis), mefloquine (anxiety/psychosis).
- **An endocrine or metabolic cause** — thyroid, calcium, sodium, hepatic/renal encephalopathy.
- **A CNS structural, infective, or epileptic cause** behind "the first psychotic break" — HIV,
  neurosyphilis, encephalitis, tumour, temporal lobe epilepsy.
- **The undisclosed plan** — a calm affect never excludes imminent suicide risk; explicit direct
  questioning is required every time, and a sudden, unexplained calm in a previously severely
  depressed, actively suicidal patient is a red flag for a decision having been made, not
  necessarily reassurance.

## 2. Presenting syndromes — worked to depth

> Format per syndrome: **Differential — three tiers** · **Discriminating history** ·
> **Discriminating examination** · **Investigations + interpretation nuance** · **Management
> anchors (SA STG/EML)** · **Consultant traps & pearls.**

### 2.1 The acutely agitated/aggressive patient

**Differential**
- *Common:* acute psychotic relapse (schizophrenia, schizoaffective); manic agitation;
  alcohol/methamphetamine intoxication; alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal; personality-disorder
  crisis with interpersonal precipitant; anxiety/panic misread as aggression; pain, urinary
  retention, or constipation driving agitation in an elderly or non-verbal patient.
- *Must-not-miss:* delirium from any organic cause (sepsis, hypoxia, hepatic/uraemic
  encephalopathy, raised ICP, post-ictal state) presenting as agitation before it presents as
  confusion; hypoglycaemia; NMS or serotonin syndrome in a patient already on psychotropics;
  akathisia misread as psychiatric agitation and treated with *more* antipsychotic (worsens it);
  anticholinergic toxidrome; thyroid storm; head injury with evolving intracranial bleed.
- *Zebras:* excited catatonia (looks like agitation, responds to lorazepam not antipsychotics —
  giving an antipsychotic here risks malignant catatonia/NMS overlap); anti-NMDA-receptor
  encephalitis (young patient, prodromal psychiatric symptoms, then seizures/autonomic instability/
  dyskinesias — antipsychotics poorly tolerated, often with severe EPSE); acute intermittent
  porphyria (abdominal pain, dark urine, peripheral neuropathy); autoimmune/paraneoplastic limbic
  encephalitis; a frontal lobe mass causing disinhibition.

**Discriminating history** — collateral is often the *only* history available (the agitated
patient cannot reliably self-report): tempo of onset (minutes-hours = organic/intoxication;
days-weeks = psychiatric relapse); substance-use timeline including last use/last drink (timing
predicts withdrawal window); full medication chart review including recent antipsychotic dose
increase (akathisia) or new serotonergic drug; known psychiatric diagnosis vs genuinely first
presentation; recent head injury; recent infection/fever; diabetic on insulin; known epilepsy.

**Discriminating examination** — vitals *before* anything else (temperature, HR, BP, SpO₂,
glucose — a glucose is mandatory, not optional, in any acutely agitated patient); pupils (pinpoint
= opioid, dilated = stimulant/anticholinergic); tremor character (fine = anxiety/hyperthyroid,
coarse = alcohol withdrawal/lithium); rigidity vs clonus (rigidity + hyperthermia = NMS; clonus +
hyperreflexia = serotonin syndrome, §2.8); ability to sit still and inner restlessness described
by the patient (akathisia, not "agitation"); signs of intoxication (nystagmus, ataxia, alcohol on
breath, injection marks); signs of trauma or focal neurology; level of attention and orientation
(inattention with fluctuation = delirium, §2.3, not primary psychiatric illness).

**Investigations** — glucose stat; U&E (Na, urea/creatinine); LFT; FBC; CK if there has been any
struggle, restraint, or prolonged agitation (baseline CK before assuming a later elevated CK means
NMS — struggle/restraint alone can push CK into the hundreds-to-low-thousands range [EK]); blood
alcohol/urine toxicology if available; septic screen if febrile; CT brain if head injury, focal
signs, or first presentation over age 40; consider ECG (QTc) before antipsychotic dosing if time
allows and the patient is not in extremis.

**Management anchors (SA STG/EML — rapid tranquillisation, Adult Hospital Level)** — stepwise, least
restrictive first:
1. **Verbal de-escalation and environmental modification** always attempted first and continued in
   parallel with pharmacological steps — reduce stimulation, remove other patients/objects, one
   calm clinician doing the talking, offer oral medication before injectable.
2. **Oral route offered first** if the patient is cooperative enough — oral lorazepam or an oral
   antipsychotic; avoids the trauma and staff-safety risk of forced injection.
3. **IM rapid tranquillisation** when oral is refused/not feasible and the patient is an immediate
   danger: **haloperidol 5 mg IM plus promethazine 25–50 mg IM** is the combination most widely
   used and evidence-supported in low-resource/African settings (from the WHO-endorsed TREC trial
   programme) — promethazine both sedates and substantially reduces the acute dystonia risk of
   haloperidol alone [EK, consistent with SA practice per published SA rapid-tranquillisation
   audits]. **Lorazepam 1–4 mg IM/IV** (SA STG dosing aligned to the lower end of the Maudsley
   range) is an alternative, particularly where a psychotic diagnosis is not yet established, in
   pure alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal, or where an antipsychotic is relatively contraindicated
   (e.g. suspected NMS, catatonia, unclear organic cause, stimulant intoxication with cardiac
   risk). **Midazolam IM** is a rapid-onset second-line option for severe agitation not settling
   with the above, but only where full airway/resuscitation equipment and continuous monitoring
   are immediately available (faster onset means a narrower margin before over-sedation/respiratory
   depression). **Avoid haloperidol as monotherapy in suspected stimulant (methamphetamine)
   intoxication** — it lowers seizure threshold and adds to a already-vulnerable QTc/hyperthermia
   picture; a benzodiazepine is the safer first pharmacological step in stimulant-driven agitation.
   **Never combine IM olanzapine with a benzodiazepine** given concurrently or within the same
   short window — the combination is associated with clinically significant respiratory
   depression/hypotension and is a specifically flagged contraindication [EK, Maudsley].
4. **Monitoring after any parenteral sedation**: vitals (RR, SpO₂, BP, pulse, temperature, level of
   consciousness) at minimum every 15 minutes for the first hour and regularly thereafter until
   the patient is rousable and stable; resuscitation trolley, oxygen, and IV access available in
   the room; allow at least 30–60 minutes between repeat doses before escalating further, and
   recalculate the cumulative dose given, not just the last one, before giving more.

**Restraint and the law (MHCA)** — physical/mechanical restraint and seclusion are permitted only
as a last resort, for the minimum necessary duration, must be clinically justified and documented
(indication, duration, review), require ongoing review by a medical practitioner, and are never
used as punishment or for staff convenience [EK, MHCA Regulations]. Chemical restraint (rapid
tranquillisation) is a treatment intervention with its own clinical indication and documentation
requirement, distinct from mechanical restraint. **SAPS may be involved under s40 MHCA** where a
person believed to be mentally ill and a danger to self/others is found in a public place — the
police may take the person into custody and deliver them to a health establishment (documented on
**MHCA 22**, §6) rather than to a police cell; this is a welfare provision, not a criminal charge.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- Sedating before examining is a classic junior error — a subdural, a hypoglycaemic patient, or a
  septic patient gets "settled" with an antipsychotic and the true diagnosis is missed until the
  patient deteriorates further, now under heavier sedation and with less reliable neuro
  observation.
- A calm, cooperative-looking patient a few minutes after arrival does not mean the crisis is
  over — reassess, don't just chart the medication and move on.
- Akathisia treated with *more* antipsychotic is a well-documented iatrogenic spiral — always ask
  explicitly "is this an inner restlessness, a need to move" before assuming psychotic agitation.
- Restraint duration and struggle alone can elevate CK into a range that mimics early NMS — trend
  it, don't anchor on one number, and look for the rest of the NMS picture (rigidity, hyperthermia,
  autonomic instability, mental state change) before committing to that diagnosis.
- Document risk assessment and the legal basis for any restrictive intervention every time — this
  is both good clinical practice and the MHCA/medico-legal record.

### 2.2 First-episode psychosis

**Differential**
- *Common:* schizophreniform disorder evolving to schizophrenia; substance-induced psychotic
  disorder (methamphetamine ["tik"] — see §2.9 — and cannabis are the two dominant drivers seen in
  SA emergency presentations); brief psychotic disorder (precipitated by acute stress); mood
  disorder with psychotic features (mania or severe depression with psychotic symptoms — mood
  symptoms often precede or dominate).
- *Must-not-miss:* HIV-associated psychosis (direct CNS effect, opportunistic CNS infection, or
  ART-related — efavirenz neuropsychiatric effects, §6); neurosyphilis (general paresis — cognitive
  decline, personality change, psychosis, often with pupillary/reflex signs); anti-NMDA-receptor
  (or other autoimmune) encephalitis, especially in a young patient with a rapid prodrome of
  headache/fever followed by psychiatric symptoms, then seizures, dyskinesias, and autonomic
  instability; temporal lobe epilepsy (interictal or postictal psychosis); delirium of any organic
  cause masquerading as "new psychosis" (§2.3); a CNS structural lesion (tumour, especially frontal
  or temporal); corticosteroid-induced psychosis; isoniazid-induced psychosis (pyridoxine-
  responsive); B12 deficiency.
- *Zebras:* Wilson's disease in a young patient with any movement disorder plus psychiatric
  symptoms; acute intermittent porphyria; systemic lupus erythematosus with CNS involvement;
  paraneoplastic syndromes; CNS TB (tuberculoma, TB meningitis) in a patient with HIV or known TB
  contact presenting atypically.

**The organic screen is mandatory, not optional, for every genuine first episode** — this is the
single most important operational rule in this section. No first psychotic episode is labelled
"primary" (schizophreniform/schizophrenia) until the organic and substance-induced alternatives
have been actively excluded, because the downstream consequences (diagnosis, treatment duration,
prognosis communicated to the patient/family, driving/occupational implications) are large and
hard to walk back later.

**Discriminating history** — age at onset (first psychosis after 40–45 raises organic/dementia/mood-
disorder-with-psychosis probability sharply — primary schizophrenia rarely debuts late); tempo
(abrupt over hours-days favours organic/substance/brief psychotic disorder; insidious over weeks-
months with a prodrome of social withdrawal, declining function, odd ideas favours schizophrenia);
detailed substance history including specific timing of methamphetamine or cannabis use relative to
symptom onset; HIV status and ART history including any recent efavirenz initiation; TB contact/
treatment history (isoniazid); sexual history/syphilis risk; family psychiatric history; headache,
fever, or a viral-illness-like prodrome (points toward encephalitis); seizures; mood symptoms
present before or driving the psychotic content (grandiosity in mania, mood-congruent guilt/nihilism
in psychotic depression).

**Discriminating examination** — full neurological exam including for focal signs, abnormal
movements (dyskinesias/dystonia unprovoked by medication raise anti-NMDA-R encephalitis or Wilson's),
Kayser-Fleischer rings if Wilson's suspected; signs of HIV/immunosuppression (oral thrush, wasting,
lymphadenopathy, skin conditions); neurosyphilis signs (Argyll Robertson pupils — rare but classic,
absent ankle reflexes, tabetic gait); autonomic instability (fever, labile BP/HR — encephalitis, NMS
if already treated); catatonic signs (posturing, waxy flexibility, mutism — present in a minority of
first-episode psychosis and easy to miss if not specifically examined for); full MSE with attention
to thought form (formal thought disorder), hallucination modality (auditory = classically primary
psychosis; prominent visual/tactile = organic/toxic until proven otherwise, though not absolute).

**Investigations** — FBC, U&E, LFT, calcium, TFTs, glucose; HIV test (with consent/pre-test
counselling per SA protocol); RPR/TPHA for syphilis (and if reactive, consider CSF VDRL/treponemal
testing if neurosyphilis suspected); B12/folate; urine toxicology (interpret with the window-period
caveats in §4); CT brain — indicated for first-episode psychosis with any red flag (focal
neurology, age >40–45, headache, abnormal movements, altered consciousness, seizure) and reasonable
as a baseline where accessible even without red flags in many settings; EEG if seizures suspected or
encephalopathic features present; LP if encephalitis/meningitis is on the differential (fever,
headache, meningism, rapid decline, seizures) — send opening pressure, cell count, protein, glucose,
Gram stain/culture, TB workup (GeneXpert, culture) if TB risk, and anti-NMDA-receptor antibody
testing where available and clinically indicated (send serum and CSF).

**Duration criteria — the DSM-5-TR distinctions that change the label (and the conversation with the
family)** [EK, DSM-5-TR]:
- **Brief psychotic disorder:** psychotic symptoms present for at least 1 day but **less than 1
  month**, with eventual full return to premorbid functioning.
- **Schizophreniform disorder:** the same symptom criteria as schizophrenia, but total duration
  (including prodrome, active, and residual phases) is **at least 1 month but less than 6 months**.
- **Schizophrenia:** continuous signs of disturbance persist for **at least 6 months**, including
  at least 1 month of active-phase symptoms (or less if successfully treated), with the rest being
  prodromal or residual symptoms.
Practically: a patient cannot be told "you have schizophrenia" at the point of a first admission —
the diagnosis is provisional until the duration criterion is met, and this should be communicated
honestly to the patient and family rather than prematurely closing the diagnostic question.

**Management anchors** — treat the organic/substance-induced cause where identified (HIV: initiate/
optimise ART per the 2023 guidelines with attention to CNS-penetrating regimens and drug
interactions with antipsychotics; neurosyphilis: IV benzylpenicillin per national protocol;
isoniazid psychosis: pyridoxine plus consideration of stopping/switching the TB regimen with TB
specialist input). For primary/substance-induced psychosis needing antipsychotic treatment: EML
first-line oral antipsychotics at district-hospital level are typically **haloperidol** or
**risperidone**, started low and titrated (specific mg doses per the current STG/EML edition —
[EK] for exact starting doses, verify against current formulary); avoid high-dose or multiple
antipsychotics empirically before the organic screen is back. Admission is usually required for a
genuine first episode both for safety and to complete the organic workup.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- The single most common junior error is anchoring on "first psychosis = schizophrenia" and
  skipping or truncating the organic screen because the mental state looks "classically psychotic."
- Methamphetamine-induced psychosis can be clinically indistinguishable from primary psychosis at
  a single cross-sectional assessment — the discriminator is often the *trajectory*: substance-
  induced psychosis that persists well beyond the expected clearance/withdrawal window (days to a
  couple of weeks for methamphetamine, though a minority develop a persistent psychotic disorder)
  should prompt reconsideration of a primary diagnosis.
- A patient with known HIV and a first psychotic episode needs the psychosis worked up like anyone
  else's *and* a CNS-opportunistic-infection screen — don't let the HIV diagnosis anchor you onto
  "HIV psychosis" without excluding cryptococcal meningitis, TB meningitis, CNS lymphoma, or PML.
- Catatonic first-episode psychosis is easy to miss if you don't specifically examine for it —
  and it changes management (benzodiazepine trial first, not more antipsychotic; see also 2.1's
  excited-catatonia caveat).

### 2.3 Delirium vs psychiatric illness

**Differential (why the patient looks "psychiatric")**
- *Common:* delirium from infection (UTI, pneumonia — classic in the elderly), drug effects
  (anticholinergics, opioids, benzodiazepine intoxication or withdrawal, steroids), metabolic
  derangement (hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy, hypoglycaemia,
  hypoxia); underlying dementia with an acute-on-chronic confusional state.
- *Must-not-miss:* delirium from a CNS cause (meningitis/encephalitis, stroke, subdural haematoma,
  raised ICP, seizure/postictal state, CNS TB or cryptococcal meningitis in HIV); delirium tremens
  (§2.9); Wernicke's encephalopathy; sepsis of any source with delirium as the presenting sign,
  especially in the elderly where fever/tachycardia may be blunted.
- *Zebras:* non-convulsive status epilepticus (fluctuating, subtle, easily mistaken for either
  psychiatric illness or "just confused"); thyroid storm; porphyria; paraneoplastic
  encephalopathy.

**The fluctuation tell.** Delirium's defining, discriminating feature against primary psychiatric
illness is **fluctuation over the course of hours** — lucid intervals alternating with confusion,
often worse at night ("sundowning") — combined with **impaired attention** as the core deficit,
not merely abnormal perception or mood. A primary psychotic or mood disorder does not typically
fluctuate hour-to-hour in this way; it is far more stable across a single day.

**Discriminating history/collateral** — acuity of onset (delirium: hours to days; dementia:
months to years; primary psychiatric relapse: days to weeks, more stable once established); a
clear baseline cognitive/functional level from a reliable informant; new medication or dose
change; recent illness, fall, surgery, or hospitalisation; alcohol/substance use and last-use
timing; known dementia (delirium is dramatically more likely superimposed on an already-vulnerable
brain — "acute on chronic").

**Discriminating examination — attention testing is the key bedside manoeuvre.** Test attention
directly and specifically, not by impression: digit span, spelling "WORLD" backwards, or reciting
the months of the year backwards. Impaired attention plus a fluctuating course is delirium until
proven otherwise. **The Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)** structures this: delirium is present
when **(1) acute onset and fluctuating course** AND **(2) inattention** are both present, PLUS
either **(3) disorganised thinking** OR **(4) altered level of consciousness** (§5 for full CAM
structure). Also examine for focal neurology, meningism, tremor/asterixis (metabolic
encephalopathy), signs of infection, vital sign abnormalities, and pupil size/reactivity.

**Hypoactive delirium — the one that gets missed entirely.** Hypoactive delirium (withdrawn,
slowed, quiet, appearing "depressed" or simply "settled") is at least as common as hyperactive/
agitated delirium in general hospital populations and carries a **worse** prognosis in part
because it is under-recognised — a quiet, drowsy, inattentive patient is easy to mistake for
"comfortable," "tired," or depressed, and easy to walk past on a ward round. Any acute change in
alertness or engagement — including a patient becoming *quieter* — warrants the same organic
work-up as new agitation.

**Investigations** — glucose, FBC, U&E, calcium, LFTs, TFTs, CRP, blood cultures if febrile, urine
microscopy/culture, chest X-ray, ECG; oxygen saturation; medication reconciliation is itself an
investigation — review every drug on the chart for delirium potential; CT brain if focal signs,
head injury, anticoagulation, or no clear precipitant found; LP if CNS infection suspected (with
attention to HIV status and CD4 for opportunistic causes); consider EEG if non-convulsive status
epilepticus is a possibility (subtle, fluctuating confusion without an obvious cause).

**Management anchors** — treat the underlying cause; this is the entire management, pharmacological
sedation is adjunctive only, for safety, at the lowest effective dose and shortest duration.
Non-pharmacological measures matter and are frequently under-used: reorientation, a visible clock/
calendar, consistent staff and lighting matched to time of day, correcting sensory deficits
(glasses, hearing aids), family presence, early mobilisation, treating pain and constipation,
minimising unnecessary lines/catheters/restraints. Where pharmacological control of severe agitated
delirium is unavoidable, a low-dose antipsychotic (e.g. haloperidol) is generally preferred over a
benzodiazepine (which can worsen confusion and cause paradoxical disinhibition) **except** in
alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal delirium (delirium tremens), where a benzodiazepine is the correct
and specific treatment (§2.9) — this exception is a frequent point of confusion and worth stating
explicitly every time.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- New "psychiatric" symptoms in a medical or surgical ward patient are delirium until specifically
  excluded — do not accept a psychiatry referral for "new psychosis/agitation" at face value
  without confirming attention has actually been tested.
- A known psychiatric patient can still get delirium — do not anchor on the existing diagnosis
  ("they're just relapsing") when a new organic driver (UTI, new drug, hyponatraemia) is present.
- Never treat delirium as a diagnosis of exclusion after a cursory look — it is a medical emergency
  work-up in its own right, with a mortality signal if the underlying cause is missed.
- Antipsychotics used for delirium do not treat delirium — they only manage the agitation while the
  real cause is found and fixed; charting one and stopping the search is the error.

### 2.4 The suicidal patient / self-harm

**Risk assessment that actually predicts — static vs dynamic, and the honest limits of prediction.**
No structured tool reliably predicts an individual's future suicide at the level of precision
clinicians often want; the value of a structured risk assessment is in ensuring the right questions
are asked and answered, not in producing a score that "clears" or "detains" a patient. Distinguish:
- **Static (unchangeable) risk factors:** male sex (higher completed-suicide rate; female sex has
  higher self-harm/attempt rate — the well-known sex paradox), previous suicide attempt (the single
  strongest predictor of a future attempt), family history of suicide, history of childhood abuse,
  older age at first presentation, chronic physical illness.
- **Dynamic (modifiable, and the ones that actually drive today's clinical decision) risk factors:**
  current suicidal ideation with a plan and means, current intent, hopelessness, active substance
  intoxication, acute psychosocial crisis (relationship breakdown, financial/legal crisis,
  bereavement), active psychiatric symptoms (command hallucinations, severe depression, mixed
  affective states), access to lethal means (firearms, pesticides/organophosphates, a large
  paracetamol supply), recent discharge from psychiatric admission (the highest-risk window of the
  entire illness course, especially the first 1–2 weeks), social isolation, recent self-harm.
- **The paradoxical-risk-during-recovery point** (already flagged in §1): as energy and
  psychomotor drive return in a recovering severe depression, a previously too-depressed-to-act
  patient may become able to act on ideation that was present but unenactable — mood rating
  improving is not, by itself, reassurance that risk has fallen.

**SAD PERSONS and its limitations.** SAD PERSONS (**S**ex male, **A**ge <19 or >45, **D**epression,
**P**revious attempt, **E**thanol/substance use, **R**ational thinking loss (psychosis), **S**ocial
supports lacking, **O**rganised plan, **N**o spouse, **S**ickness (chronic illness)) is a useful
checklist for ensuring a complete history is taken, but it has **poor predictive validity as a
scoring/triage instrument** — it was never validated to reliably stratify who will go on to attempt
or complete suicide, and using the numeric score alone to decide disposition (e.g. "score <6,
discharge") is a well-documented misuse [EK, consistent with the wider psychiatric-emergency-
medicine literature]. It should prompt a thorough, individualised clinical risk formulation, not
substitute for one.

**Discriminating history** — direct, explicit questioning about suicidal ideation, plan, intent,
and access to means, every time, without euphemism, and without the false belief that asking
"puts the idea in their head"; the index episode in detail (what preceded it, what the patient
believed would happen, whether they expected to be found/intervened on, whether they told anyone,
regret at survival vs regret at being found); prior attempts (number, lethality, method — a switch
to a more lethal method over successive attempts is a red flag); current stressors; substance use
around the time of the act; a specific, explicit assessment of current ideation *right now*, in
this interview, not just at the time of the index act.

**Discriminating examination** — full MSE with particular attention to affect (congruent
hopelessness vs an unexpected, unexplained calm — the latter can signal a decision having been
made, not resolution, §1), psychomotor state, presence of command hallucinations, insight into the
act and its consequences, cognitive state (is capacity to engage in safety planning intact).
Physical examination for evidence of self-harm (old scars, current injuries) and for medical
sequelae of the current act (see overdose co-management below).

**Safety planning** — a specific, individualised, written or documented plan collaboratively
developed with the patient (not merely a verbal "no-harm contract," which has no evidence of
efficacy and can create false reassurance): recognising personal warning signs; internal coping
strategies; people and social settings that provide distraction; people to contact for help;
professionals/services to contact in crisis; means restriction (removing or securing access to the
specific method used or considered — this is one of the few interventions with genuine evidence
behind it); and a plan for making the immediate environment safer.

**Medical co-management pointers — the two SA-relevant overdose patterns:**
- **Paracetamol overdose:** timing of ingestion relative to presentation drives everything — the
  Rumack-Matthew nomogram is only interpretable from **4 hours post-ingestion**; N-acetylcysteine
  treatment thresholds and regimen are a medical-team decision but the psychiatrist must know
  ingestion time, quantity, and co-ingestants (especially other analgesics, alcohol) to inform both
  the medical and psychiatric picture; a psychiatric assessment for capacity/risk is still required
  even while the medical treatment is running, and should not be delayed to "wait until medically
  stable" if the patient is able to engage — but must not override active medical resuscitation
  priorities.
- **Organophosphate/pesticide poisoning:** still a significant rural/agricultural self-harm method
  in parts of SA; presents with the cholinergic toxidrome (miosis, salivation, lacrimation, GI
  hypermotility, bronchorrhoea, bradycardia, muscle fasciculation/weakness) and is a medical
  emergency (atropine titration, consideration of pralidoxime, airway protection given
  bronchorrhoea/weakness risk) that must be stabilised before any formal psychiatric interview is
  attempted; the psychiatric risk assessment happens once the patient can meaningfully participate,
  but early collateral and means-restriction planning (the household's remaining pesticide supply)
  should start immediately.

**Management anchors** — admission (with the patient's consent where they have capacity and agree,
or under the MHCA where they lack capacity or refuse and remain a danger to self, §6) for
active suicidal intent with a plan and means, or where the acute stressor and risk cannot be
safely contained as an outpatient; involve family/support system in safety planning with consent;
treat the underlying psychiatric disorder (depression, psychosis) definitively, not just the crisis;
outpatient follow-up arranged *before* discharge, not left for the patient to organise; means
restriction discussed explicitly with the patient and family.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- A flat, unremarkable-sounding "I'm fine now" after a serious attempt is not reassurance by
  itself — always re-elicit current ideation directly and explicitly, and cross-check against
  collateral and the MSE.
- Discharging on the strength of a checklist score (SAD PERSONS or similar) without an
  individualised formulation is a recognised systems failure, not sound practice.
- The highest-risk period after any psychiatric admission is the first 1–2 weeks post-discharge —
  discharge planning is a risk-reduction intervention, not an administrative afterthought.
- Never assume a calm affect excludes imminent risk — it can mean the opposite (§1).

### 2.5 Mood: depression

**Differential**
- *Common:* major depressive episode (single or recurrent); adjustment disorder with depressed
  mood; dysthymia/persistent depressive disorder; grief (see §3 for the discriminator against
  depression); depression secondary to a chronic medical illness.
- *Must-not-miss:* bipolar depression presenting as "just depression" (the antidepressant-
  monotherapy trap below); depression as the presenting feature of hypothyroidism; substance-
  induced depressive disorder (alcohol is a depressant in its own right, independent of any primary
  mood disorder); medication-induced depression — **efavirenz** (vivid dreams, mood disturbance,
  depression, and rarely psychosis/suicidality, typically in the first weeks of therapy but can
  persist or emerge later — a common and under-recognised driver in SA's ART-treated population,
  §6) and **isoniazid** (depression and psychosis, both pyridoxine-responsive mechanistically related
  to B6 antagonism); postpartum depression (distinct from and more severe/persistent than postpartum
  blues, §2.10); depression with psychotic features (mood-congruent nihilistic/guilt delusions,
  sometimes Cotard-type "I am dead/rotting" delusions) — a higher-acuity presentation with higher
  suicide risk and different treatment (needs an antipsychotic or ECT, not antidepressant alone).
- *Zebras:* depression as the presenting feature of a CNS lesion (especially frontal); Cushing's
  syndrome/steroid-induced depression; pancreatic carcinoma (classically associated with depression
  preceding the diagnosis); HIV-associated depression via direct CNS mechanisms as distinct from a
  reactive/psychosocial depression to the diagnosis itself — both are common in SA and not mutually
  exclusive.

**Discriminating history** — full depressive symptom inventory (mood, anhedonia, sleep, appetite/
weight, energy, concentration, guilt/worthlessness, psychomotor change, suicidality) with duration
≥2 weeks for a formal episode; **explicit screening for any past hypomanic/manic episode** — this
single question, asked properly (not just "have you ever felt manic" but specific behavioural
probes: unusual elevated/irritable mood with decreased need for sleep, increased goal-directed
activity, spending, risk-taking, grandiosity, for days at a time) is the discriminator that
prevents the single most consequential error in this section (below); ART history and timing of
any efavirenz initiation relative to symptom onset; TB treatment history; postpartum timing; a
collateral account of episodic vs continuous course (bipolar depressive episodes are typically more
episodic with clearer inter-episode recovery than unipolar recurrent depression, though this is a
soft discriminator, not absolute).

**Discriminating examination** — MSE: psychomotor retardation or agitation, affect congruence,
evidence of self-neglect, any psychotic features (mood-congruent delusions/hallucinations); physical
exam for hypothyroid signs (bradycardia, dry skin, delayed reflexes, goitre), signs of chronic
illness/wasting (HIV, malignancy), and Cushingoid features if steroid exposure.

**Investigations** — TFTs (hypothyroidism is a genuinely common and reversible mimic — always
check); FBC (anaemia contributing to fatigue); U&E, calcium; HIV status if unknown; B12/folate;
review of the drug chart specifically for efavirenz and isoniazid; PHQ-9 (§5) as a structured
severity/monitoring tool, not a standalone diagnostic instrument.

**The bipolar depression trap — antidepressant monotherapy switching risk.** A patient presenting
with a depressive episode who in fact has an undiagnosed or unelicited bipolar diathesis, started
on an antidepressant **without a mood stabiliser or antipsychotic on board**, carries a recognised
risk of a **switch into mania or a mixed state**, and of accelerating the overall cycling frequency
of the underlying bipolar illness over time [EK, consistent with Maudsley/major bipolar-treatment
guidance]. This is why the single explicit screening question above is not optional — every new
depressive presentation being considered for antidepressant treatment needs a deliberate,
documented screen for past hypomania/mania first. Where bipolar depression is identified or
suspected, the treatment approach differs (mood stabiliser or an antipsychotic with antidepressant
properties, per specialist guidance, generally not an antidepressant alone).

**Management anchors** — EML antidepressants at district-hospital level are typically an SSRI (e.g.
fluoxetine) and amitriptyline (widely used and EML-listed for depression in SA primary/district
care [EK — verify current dose against the in-force STG/EML edition]); full antidepressant effect
takes **2–6 weeks**, with the patient counselled explicitly about this delay and about a possible
early increase in agitation/anxiety in the first 1–2 weeks; severe depression with psychotic
features, high suicide risk, catatonia, or failure to eat/drink is an admission and often an
ECT-consideration scenario, not an outpatient antidepressant trial; psychotherapy (where available)
alongside pharmacotherapy for moderate-severe episodes.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- Treating every "low mood" with an antidepressant without checking TFTs and without the bipolar
  screen is a two-part error that recurs constantly in general practice and general-hospital
  liaison referrals.
- A patient recently started on efavirenz presenting with new depression/suicidality deserves a
  specific conversation with the treating ART clinician about a regimen switch, not just a
  psychiatric medication added on top.
- Psychotic depression is a different, higher-acuity illness from non-psychotic depression — treat
  it as such, do not just add "some psychotic symptoms" as a footnote to a standard depression plan.

### 2.6 Mania / bipolar disorder

**Differential**
- *Common:* bipolar I manic episode; bipolar II hypomanic episode; mixed features episode
  (simultaneous manic and depressive symptoms — higher suicide risk, easy to under-recognise if
  only screening for "classic" euphoric mania).
- *Must-not-miss:* organic mania — corticosteroid-induced mania (common, dose-related, often early
  in a steroid course); hyperthyroidism; CNS causes (stroke — particularly right-hemisphere lesions,
  frontal/temporal tumour, multiple sclerosis, traumatic brain injury, HIV-associated CNS disease);
  substance-induced mania/hypomania (methamphetamine, cocaine, cannabis, and — importantly —
  antidepressant-induced switch in an undiagnosed bipolar patient, §2.5); post-partum mania/mixed
  state as part of postpartum psychosis (§2.10, psychiatric emergency).
- *Zebras:* frontotemporal dementia presenting with disinhibition mistaken for mania in an older
  patient (a first "manic" episode after 40–45 should always trigger this consideration); Huntington's
  disease; neurosyphilis (general paresis can present with grandiosity/disinhibition); paraneoplastic
  syndromes.

**Discriminating history** — elevated or irritable mood plus at least three (four if only irritable)
of: grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas/racing thoughts,
distractibility, increased goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation, excessive involvement
in risky activities (spending, sexual indiscretion, business decisions), for at least 1 week (or
any duration if hospitalisation required) for mania, at least 4 days for hypomania [EK, DSM-5-TR];
recent steroid course; thyroid symptoms; substance use timeline (stimulants especially); recent
antidepressant initiation/dose increase; postpartum timing; family history of bipolar disorder;
prior depressive episodes (the great majority of bipolar patients present first with depression,
not mania — always ask about past mood elevation when assessing any depression, as above).

**Discriminating examination** — pressured, difficult-to-interrupt speech; flight of ideas
(logical links between rapidly shifting topics, distinct from the loosened associations of
psychosis, though the two can co-occur in mania with psychotic features); grandiose or
mood-congruent psychotic content if present; psychomotor agitation; signs of thyrotoxicosis
(tremor, tachycardia, lid lag, goitre); focal neurological signs; signs of steroid use (Cushingoid
habitus) or a steroid prescription on the chart.

**Investigations** — TFTs; U&E, calcium, glucose; FBC; urine toxicology; **baseline workup before
starting lithium** (see below); CT brain for a first manic episode after 40–45 or with any focal/
atypical feature; review of all current medications (steroids, and any recently started or
increased antidepressant/dopaminergic drug).

**Lithium — the initiation workup, levels, and toxicity bands.** Baseline before starting: renal
function (U&E, eGFR — lithium is renally excreted and nephrotoxic with long-term use), thyroid
function (lithium can cause hypothyroidism), calcium (lithium can cause hyperparathyroidism/
hypercalcaemia), pregnancy test in women of childbearing potential (teratogenic — cardiac
malformations, classically Ebstein's anomaly, though absolute risk is lower than once believed —
still requires a specific risk-benefit discussion), ECG in those with cardiac risk factors, and
weight/BMI at baseline [EK, consistent with Maudsley/standard practice]. **Therapeutic range:**
approximately **0.6–1.2 mmol/L** for acute mania (lower end, e.g. **0.4–0.8 mmol/L**, often
targeted for maintenance and in older adults) [EK]. **Level timing:** levels are drawn **12 hours
post-dose** (a trough level) — a level drawn at any other time is uninterpretable against the
standard therapeutic range and is a very common practical error. **Toxicity bands** [EK, standard
toxicology teaching, consistent across multiple clinical references]: levels from roughly
**1.5 mmol/L** upward begin to show early toxicity (coarse tremor, GI upset, ataxia, drowsiness,
muscle weakness); **2.0 mmol/L and above** is associated with more severe toxicity (confusion,
worsening ataxia, hyperreflexia, potential seizures, cardiac conduction changes); **2.5 mmol/L and
above** (and certainly above 3.0–4.0 in the acute-on-chronic setting) is severe, life-threatening
toxicity with a real risk of seizures, coma, and death, and is the range where **haemodialysis** is
actively considered, particularly with renal impairment, deteriorating conscious level, or
inability to tolerate oral fluids/further oral lithium clearance. Toxicity can occur at
"therapeutic" levels if renal function has acutely deteriorated (dehydration, diuretics, NSAIDs,
ACE-inhibitors/ARBs all reduce lithium clearance and are classic precipitants of acute-on-chronic
toxicity in a previously stable patient) — always ask about recent illness, reduced intake, diarrhoea/
vomiting, or a new interacting drug in any patient on lithium presenting unwell.

**Valproate in women of childbearing potential — a specific, high-stakes prescribing caution.**
Valproate carries a substantial teratogenic risk (neural tube defects and other malformations) and
an associated risk of neurodevelopmental impairment in children exposed in utero, and is therefore
avoided as first-line in women of childbearing potential wherever an alternative is reasonable;
where it is used, it requires explicit discussion of contraception and the risks, and should be
avoided in pregnancy except where no other option controls the illness and the risk-benefit has
been carefully documented [EK, consistent with international regulatory guidance and Maudsley].

**Lamotrigine and SJS.** Lamotrigine (used for bipolar depression/maintenance rather than acute
mania, where it has little role given its slow titration) carries a dose- and titration-rate-
dependent risk of serious skin reactions including **Stevens-Johnson syndrome**; the risk is
substantially reduced by slow titration per the prescribing schedule and is a major reason
lamotrigine is never rapidly loaded — any new rash in a patient on lamotrigine, especially during
the titration phase, requires the drug to be stopped and the patient assessed urgently, not merely
observed.

**Management anchors** — acute mania: antipsychotic (e.g. haloperidol or risperidone, EML options)
often used for rapid control of agitation/psychotic features alongside initiation of a mood
stabiliser (lithium or valproate, factoring in the childbearing-potential caution above); admission
is usual for a first manic episode or for any episode with significant risk (financial/sexual/
occupational harm from disinhibited behaviour, psychosis, exhaustion) given the impaired insight
that is characteristic of mania.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- A first "manic" presentation after 40–45 is an organic-mania workup, not a bipolar-disorder
  diagnosis, until proven otherwise.
- Lithium toxicity in a previously stable patient is usually precipitated by a new drug or
  dehydration/illness reducing renal clearance — always ask, don't just re-check the level in
  isolation.
- Drawing a lithium level at the wrong time (not 12h post-dose) and acting on it is a common,
  avoidable error.
- Mixed features are easy to miss if only screening for euphoric mania — ask about depressive
  symptoms co-occurring with the manic ones, and note the higher suicide risk in mixed states.

### 2.7 Anxiety spectrum incl. panic — and its medical mimics

**Differential**
- *Common:* panic disorder; generalised anxiety disorder; social anxiety disorder; specific phobia;
  adjustment disorder with anxiety; health anxiety.
- *Must-not-miss — the medical mimics, which must be actively excluded before a panic/anxiety
  diagnosis is accepted, especially at a first presentation:* **thyrotoxicosis** (tremor, heat
  intolerance, weight loss, tachycardia, lid lag — TFTs on any new-onset significant anxiety);
  **cardiac arrhythmia** (palpitations with panic-like presentation — an ECG, and Holter monitoring
  where episodic, before accepting "just anxiety"); **pulmonary embolism** (acute dyspnoea,
  tachycardia, chest pain, anxiety/sense of doom — a real and dangerous mimic, particularly with
  risk factors: recent immobilisation, pregnancy/postpartum, oestrogen-containing contraception,
  malignancy); **hypoglycaemia** (autonomic symptoms of anxiety/panic — tremor, sweating,
  palpitations — check a glucose, especially in a diabetic on insulin/sulphonylureas); **phaeochromocytoma**
  (rare but classic zebra — episodic severe anxiety/panic with headache, sweating, palpitations,
  and paroxysmal hypertension); stimulant intoxication or caffeine excess; alcohol/benzodiazepine
  withdrawal presenting as anxiety; asthma/COPD exacerbation with associated air-hunger anxiety;
  a general medical anxiety-inducing effect of corticosteroids, salbutamol, and other sympathomimetics
  (already flagged in §1's "two-chart reflex").
- *Zebras:* temporal lobe epilepsy with ictal fear/anxiety; carcinoid syndrome; vestibular
  disorders presenting with panic-like dizziness/anxiety.

**Discriminating history** — panic attacks: abrupt onset, peak within minutes, discrete episode
with a cluster of somatic symptoms (palpitations, sweating, trembling, dyspnoea, chest discomfort,
nausea, dizziness, derealisation/depersonalisation, fear of dying/losing control) and a clear
resolution, versus a more continuous, free-floating worry pattern of GAD; situational triggers
(specific/social phobia) vs unprovoked/spontaneous attacks (more typical panic disorder); weight
loss/heat intolerance (thyroid); relationship of symptoms to meals/fasting (hypoglycaemia); exertion-
related vs at-rest onset (cardiac/PE more likely with exertion or a positional/risk-factor
association); caffeine/stimulant/substance intake; family history of panic disorder or anxiety.

**Discriminating examination** — vitals (tachycardia, hypertension, tachypnoea, fever); thyroid
exam (goitre, tremor, lid lag, reflexes); cardiovascular exam and ECG for arrhythmia; signs of DVT
if PE suspected (though absence does not exclude PE); glucose if hypoglycaemia suspected; full MSE
distinguishing anticipatory anxiety/avoidance behaviour (psychiatric) from a purely somatic
symptom complex without cognitive anxiety content (raises the medical-mimic probability).

**Investigations** — TFTs, glucose, ECG (and consider troponin/D-dimer/CT-PA if the clinical
picture and risk factors support a PE work-up — this is a medical, not psychiatric, decision
pathway but the psychiatrist initiating or requesting it is often what catches a missed PE);
consider 24-hour urinary metanephrines/catecholamines if phaeochromocytoma is genuinely suspected
(episodic severe symptoms with paroxysmal hypertension); GAD-7 (§5) as a severity/monitoring tool
once organic causes are reasonably excluded.

**PTSD in the SA trauma context.** South Africa's exceptionally high rates of interpersonal
violence, sexual assault, and community/gang violence in some areas mean PTSD (and complex trauma
presentations, and trauma-related dissociative symptoms) are common and frequently under-elicited
because patients are not routinely asked about trauma exposure unless it is the presenting
complaint [EK]. Screen explicitly for: intrusive memories/flashbacks/nightmares, avoidance of
trauma reminders, negative alterations in mood/cognition (including trauma-related guilt, a common
feature after assault or in survivors of violence where others died), and hyperarousal
(hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, sleep disturbance, irritability) persisting beyond 1 month
post-trauma (acute stress reaction/disorder is the diagnosis in the first month). Comorbid
substance use as a coping mechanism, and comorbid depression, are the rule rather than the
exception and should be actively screened for, not assumed absent.

**Management anchors** — treat identified medical mimics definitively first. For primary anxiety
disorders: SSRIs are first-line pharmacotherapy (EML fluoxetine or similar, allowing for the same
2–6 week delay to effect as in depression, and counselling that anxiety can transiently worsen in
the first 1–2 weeks of SSRI initiation); benzodiazepines have a role for short-term, time-limited
relief of severe acute anxiety but carry dependence risk with regular use beyond a few weeks and
are not first-line maintenance treatment; psychotherapy (CBT-type approaches) where available is
first-line or adjunctive for panic disorder, GAD, and PTSD.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- Labelling recurrent palpitations-with-anxiety as "panic disorder" without an ECG (and, where the
  story fits, further cardiac work-up) is a recurring source of missed arrhythmia.
- A first presentation of "panic" in a patient with risk factors for PE deserves the PE work-up
  pathway, not reassurance.
- Trauma exposure and PTSD symptoms are under-elicited unless asked about directly — build the
  screen into routine history-taking in the SA context rather than waiting for the patient to
  volunteer it.

### 2.8 Treatment-emergent psychiatric emergencies

**Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)**
- *Full criteria (clinical, no single confirmatory test):* recent (typically days to weeks, classically
  within the first 2 weeks of starting or increasing the dose of an antipsychotic, though it can
  occur at any point) dopamine-antagonist exposure, **severe rigidity** ("lead-pipe"), **hyperthermia**
  (often >38°C, can be markedly higher), **autonomic instability** (labile BP, tachycardia, diaphoresis,
  tachypnoea), and **altered mental status** (confusion to coma), with supportive lab findings of a
  markedly **elevated CK** (often into the thousands to tens of thousands range — far higher than
  the modest elevation seen from struggle/restraint alone, §2.1), leukocytosis, and deranged LFTs
  [EK, standard toxicology/psychiatric-emergency teaching].
- *Management:* stop the offending antipsychotic immediately; supportive care (IV fluids, active
  cooling, monitor renal function given rhabdomyolysis/AKI risk); **dantrolene** for severe cases
  (acts directly on skeletal muscle to reduce rigidity/hyperthermia); **bromocriptine** (dopamine
  agonist, reverses the underlying dopaminergic blockade) as an alternative or adjunct; benzodiazepines
  for agitation/rigidity in milder cases; ICU-level supportive care for severe presentations;
  re-challenge with an antipsychotic (if needed later) only after full recovery, with a different
  agent, at a low dose, with close monitoring, and ideally not for at least 2 weeks [EK].
- *Vs serotonin syndrome discriminators:* **time course** — serotonin syndrome develops rapidly
  (hours) after starting/increasing a serotonergic drug or a drug interaction (e.g. an MAOI
  combination, tramadol added to an SSRI), whereas NMS develops more subacutely over days to a
  couple of weeks after antipsychotic exposure. **Neuromuscular findings** — serotonin syndrome
  produces **hyperreflexia and clonus** (especially lower-limb, and inducible clonus is the single
  most discriminating sign, forming the core of the **Hunter criteria**: clonus — spontaneous,
  inducible, or ocular — plus agitation/diaphoresis/tremor in the setting of a serotonergic agent —
  reported at roughly 84% sensitivity and 97% specificity against expert toxicologist diagnosis
  [EK]); NMS produces **rigidity without clonus and with reduced/absent reflexes**. Both share
  hyperthermia and autonomic instability, which is why the two are conflated at the bedside without
  deliberately checking for clonus.

**Acute dystonia**
- Sudden, involuntary, sustained muscle contraction — classically oculogyric crisis, torticollis,
  laryngospasm (the dangerous one — airway risk), or opisthotonus — typically within hours to a
  few days of starting or increasing an antipsychotic (especially high-potency first-generation
  agents like haloperidol), and much more common in young men and in antipsychotic-naive patients.
- *Management:* an anticholinergic — **biperiden** (EML anti-dystonia agent in SA practice) IM/IV,
  or **promethazine** (also anticholinergic/antihistaminic, doubles as the sedative co-administered
  in rapid tranquillisation, §2.1) — resolves symptoms rapidly, usually within 15–30 minutes; if
  laryngospasm/airway compromise, treat as an emergency with airway support available.

**Akathisia — the misdiagnosed-as-agitation trap.** Subjective inner restlessness and an inability
to stay still (constant shifting weight, pacing, rocking), typically emerging days to weeks after
starting or increasing an antipsychotic dose (though it can occur with any dose change including
dose reduction of some drugs). **The critical error, flagged twice already in this dossier because
it is that consequential:** akathisia is frequently misread as worsening psychotic agitation, which
prompts a clinician to *increase* the antipsychotic dose — which worsens the akathisia, creating a
dangerous escalating spiral, and is also a recognised contributor to increased suicidality in some
patients who experience it as intolerable. The discriminator is asking the patient directly whether
the restlessness is an internal, uncomfortable drive to move (akathisia) as opposed to a
psychotic/manic drive; management is to reduce the causative antipsychotic dose if feasible, and/or
treat with a beta-blocker (e.g. propranolol) or a benzodiazepine [EK, standard practice] — not more
antipsychotic.

**Serotonin syndrome** — see NMS comparison above for full picture; typically from SSRI/SNRI
overdose, combination of serotonergic agents (SSRI + tramadol, SSRI + MAOI — a dangerous and
sometimes fatal combination requiring a washout period between the two drug classes), or
serotonergic-drug interactions. Management: stop the causative agent(s), supportive care/cooling,
benzodiazepines for agitation, **cyproheptadine** (serotonin antagonist) for moderate-severe cases,
ICU-level care for severe presentations with marked hyperthermia/autonomic instability.

**Lithium toxicity** — see full detail under §2.6 (levels, bands, dialysis indications). Key
discrimination point restated here: **acute** overdose (e.g. deliberate self-poisoning in a patient
not usually on lithium, or a large single ingestion) tends to produce predominantly GI symptoms
early with neurotoxicity lagging, because lithium needs time to redistribute into the CNS, whereas
**chronic** toxicity (a patient established on lithium who becomes toxic due to reduced clearance —
dehydration, new NSAID/ACE-inhibitor/diuretic, deteriorating renal function) tends to present with
neurotoxicity (confusion, ataxia, tremor) at a lower absolute serum level, because the CNS is
already equilibrated with a higher tissue burden — meaning the serum number alone, without knowing
whether this is acute or chronic exposure, can be misleading, and clinical severity should weigh
at least as heavily as the level in the decision to dialyse.

**Clozapine emergencies**
- **Agranulocytosis** — the reason for mandatory haematological monitoring: **weekly full blood
  counts for the first 18 weeks**, then **fortnightly to week 52**, then **monthly for as long as
  the patient remains on clozapine** [EK, standard international clozapine monitoring protocol,
  reflected in SA specialist-unit practice per published SA clozapine-monitoring audits] — any
  significant drop in neutrophil count triggers a defined action pathway (increased monitoring
  frequency, and clozapine discontinuation at a defined severe-neutropenia/agranulocytosis
  threshold), and clozapine is stopped immediately, not tapered, if agranulocytosis is confirmed.
- **Myocarditis** — the highest-risk window is the **first 6 weeks** of treatment; presents with
  tachycardia (often the earliest and most sensitive sign), fever, chest pain, dyspnoea, and
  troponin/CRP elevation; a baseline and serial troponin/CRP/ECG protocol during initiation is
  standard practice in units with the resources to do it, and any unexplained tachycardia,
  fever, or cardiac symptom in the first 6 weeks of clozapine needs active cardiac work-up, not
  reassurance.
- **GI hypomotility** — clozapine's anticholinergic/antiserotonergic effect on gut motility can
  produce severe constipation progressing to ileus, bowel obstruction, and (rarely) fatal bowel
  ischaemia/perforation — this is a genuinely dangerous and under-recognised clozapine risk;
  proactive bowel-regimen prescribing and a low threshold to investigate new constipation or
  abdominal symptoms in any clozapine patient is standard practice, not an optional extra.
- **Seizures** — dose-related and more likely at higher doses/rapid titration; does not
  automatically mandate stopping clozapine but requires dose review, EEG consideration, and often
  an anticonvulsant (with attention to interactions).

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- Rigidity without clonus, subacute onset = NMS. Clonus with hyperreflexia, acute onset after a
  serotonergic change = serotonin syndrome. Say both out loud at the bedside before treating.
- Struggle-related CK elevation is real but rarely reaches the very high levels seen in true NMS —
  use the whole clinical picture, not the CK number alone.
- Any new tachycardia in the first 6 weeks of clozapine is myocarditis until excluded.
- Akathisia treated with more antipsychotic is one of the most preventable iatrogenic harms in this
  entire dossier — ask the direct question every time a patient looks "agitated" on antipsychotic
  treatment.

### 2.9 Substance use presentations

**Alcohol withdrawal**
- *Timeline:* early symptoms (tremor, anxiety, sweating, tachycardia, insomnia) from **6–12 hours**
  after the last drink/significant reduction; **withdrawal seizures** typically **12–48 hours**;
  **alcoholic hallucinosis** (visual, auditory, or tactile hallucinations with otherwise clear
  sensorium) around **12–48 hours**; **delirium tremens (DTs)** typically **48–96 hours**, and is a
  medical emergency with a meaningful mortality risk if untreated (severe autonomic instability,
  fluctuating confusion, agitation, hallucinations, fever, tachycardia, hypertension) [EK, standard
  addiction-medicine teaching].
- **CIWA-Ar bands** (§5 for full instrument): total score guides management intensity — broadly,
  **≤10 = mild** (may not need pharmacological treatment, supportive care/monitoring may suffice),
  **10–15 (some formulations 11–15) = moderate** (benzodiazepine treatment indicated), **≥15–16 =
  severe** (higher risk of seizures/DTs, needs intensive monitoring, often inpatient/higher level of
  care) [EK].
- **Thiamine before glucose — the rule that prevents Wernicke's.** Any patient at risk of thiamine
  deficiency (alcohol use disorder, malnutrition) who is given glucose (dextrose infusion, or even
  a meal) **before** thiamine replacement risks precipitating or worsening **Wernicke's
  encephalopathy**, because glucose metabolism consumes the remaining thiamine stores. Practice:
  give (or at minimum ensure) parenteral thiamine before or simultaneously with any glucose-
  containing fluid in this population — this is a one-line rule with outsized clinical importance
  and a classic exam/practice trap precisely because it is counterintuitive to reach for glucose
  first in a confused, possibly hypoglycaemic-looking patient.
- **The Wernicke triad** — confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus — is present together in
  only a minority of cases (often cited around 10%) [EK], meaning the diagnosis must be suspected
  and treated presumptively on a lower threshold (any one element, in an at-risk patient) rather
  than waiting for the full triad, because untreated Wernicke's progresses to the largely
  irreversible amnestic Korsakoff syndrome.
- *Management anchors:* benzodiazepines (e.g. diazepam or lorazepam per a symptom-triggered or
  fixed-schedule regimen, EML-guided dosing — [EK] verify exact SA STG dosing schedule) as the
  mainstay; parenteral thiamine (high-dose, before/with glucose) for at-risk or Wernicke-suspected
  patients; supportive care, electrolyte correction (magnesium, potassium, phosphate commonly
  depleted); DTs specifically requires often much higher benzodiazepine doses than standard
  withdrawal and close monitoring, sometimes at a higher level of care than a general ward.

**Methamphetamine ("tik") intoxication/psychosis** — sympathomimetic toxidrome (tachycardia,
hypertension, hyperthermia, dilated pupils, agitation, diaphoresis); psychosis can be
indistinguishable cross-sectionally from primary psychosis (paranoia, auditory and tactile
hallucinations — formication, "meth mites/bugs," is a relatively characteristic tactile
hallucination); risk of hyperthermia, seizures, cardiac arrhythmia/ischaemia, and rhabdomyolysis in
severe intoxication. Management: benzodiazepines first-line for agitation (avoid antipsychotic
monotherapy, §2.1); active cooling if hyperthermic; cardiac monitoring; antipsychotic can be added
for persistent psychotic symptoms once acute intoxication risk is managed. **SA epidemiology:**
methamphetamine dominates the substance-use treatment-admission burden in the Western Cape — around
**43.5% of all specialist substance-use treatment admissions in the Western Cape in early 2025**,
the highest of any province and broadly consistent with the elevated levels recorded there over the
past decade [SACENDU-reported figures via secondary reporting, WebSearch — [EK]-adjacent, treat the
precise percentage as illustrative-current rather than pinned to a specific verified primary
document].

**Opioid withdrawal (including "nyaope"/"whoonga")** — **nyaope** (also called **whoonga** in some
regions, particularly KwaZulu-Natal) is a low-grade street drug mixture common in parts of SA,
typically containing heroin (or other opioids) combined variably with other substances (cannabis
and other adulterants — composition is inconsistent and unpredictable street-to-street) [EK];
withdrawal produces the classic opioid picture: dysphoria, myalgia, rhinorrhoea/lacrimation,
piloerection, GI cramping/diarrhoea, mydriasis, tachycardia, yawning, insomnia — uncomfortable but
**not typically life-threatening** in the way alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal can be (a key
teaching point: opioid withdrawal feels dramatic but rarely kills; alcohol/benzo withdrawal can
kill). **COWS (Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale)** bands (§5): **5–12 mild**, **13–24 moderate**,
**25–36 moderately severe**, **≥37 severe** [EK]. Management: symptomatic (e.g. an alpha-2 agonist
such as clonidine for autonomic symptoms, antiemetics, antidiarrhoeals, analgesia) where opioid
substitution/agonist therapy is not accessible or not chosen; opioid substitution therapy (e.g.
methadone or buprenorphine where available) is the evidence-preferred approach for opioid use
disorder generally but access varies significantly across SA settings, and a district hospital may
have to manage withdrawal symptomatically without substitution access.

**Benzodiazepine dependence** — withdrawal can be as dangerous as alcohol withdrawal (seizure and
delirium risk), particularly after abrupt cessation of high-dose or long-term use; management is a
**slow, tapered dose reduction**, not abrupt cessation, ideally converting to a longer-acting
benzodiazepine equivalent-dose taper where the specific short-acting agent used is unclear or
erratic.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- Treating opioid withdrawal as a medical emergency and alcohol/benzo withdrawal as "just
  uncomfortable" is backwards — get the relative danger the right way round.
- A tik-induced psychosis that persists well past the expected clearance window deserves
  reconsideration as an unmasked or precipitated primary psychotic disorder, not an assumption that
  "it's just the drugs, it'll settle."
- Thiamine before glucose, every time, in any at-risk patient — this is one of the highest-yield
  one-line rules in the entire dossier.

### 2.10 The perinatal patient

**Postpartum blues vs postpartum depression vs postpartum psychosis — a severity and timing
spectrum, not three unrelated diagnoses:**
- **Postpartum ("baby") blues:** very common (a substantial majority of postpartum women experience
  some degree of this [EK]), onset typically **day 3–5** postpartum, mild, self-limiting tearfulness,
  mood lability, and anxiety, resolving spontaneously within **about 2 weeks** without treatment
  beyond support and reassurance. Persistence beyond 2 weeks, or a severity beyond mild lability,
  should prompt reassessment for postpartum depression rather than being dismissed as "just the
  blues."
- **Postpartum depression:** a major depressive episode with onset within (conventionally) the
  first weeks to months postpartum (DSM-5-TR's "peripartum onset" specifier applies to onset during
  pregnancy or within 4 weeks postpartum, though clinically depression presenting later in the first
  postpartum year is still recognised and treated as postpartum-related depression) — more
  persistent, more functionally impairing than blues, includes the full depressive symptom set,
  and carries suicide risk that must be actively assessed, alongside the specific and important
  screen for **intrusive thoughts of harming the infant** (common as ego-dystonic, distressing
  intrusive thoughts in postpartum OCD/anxiety and non-psychotic depression — distinguish this from
  the far more dangerous psychotic infanticidal ideation of postpartum psychosis below; ego-dystonic
  intrusive thoughts that horrify the mother are a different risk category from psychotic command-
  type ideation).
- **Postpartum psychosis — a psychiatric emergency.** Onset typically abrupt, within the **first 2
  weeks postpartum** (often within days), with rapid fluctuation, confusion, disorganised behaviour,
  mood symptoms (mania, depression, or a mixed/rapidly shifting picture), and psychotic features
  (delusions often related to the infant — including delusions that the baby is defective, possessed,
  or in danger, or command hallucinations) [EK]. This carries a significant risk of both **maternal
  suicide** and **infanticide**, and mandates **emergency admission** (mother and, where the
  admitting unit/system allows, ideally in a setting supporting mother-infant contact under
  supervision, though safety of the infant is the immediate over-riding priority) — this is not a
  presentation to manage as an outpatient while "monitoring." Any woman with a personal or family
  history of bipolar disorder or a previous postpartum psychosis episode carries a substantially
  elevated risk in a subsequent pregnancy and should have this flagged prospectively for close
  monitoring in the immediate postpartum period.

**Medication in pregnancy and lactation — the practical anchors:**
- **Lithium:** teratogenic risk (cardiac malformations, classically Ebstein's anomaly, though
  absolute risk is lower than historically taught) — the decision to continue lithium through
  pregnancy is a specialist risk-benefit discussion weighing relapse risk (which is itself
  substantial and dangerous, particularly in bipolar disorder, if lithium is abruptly stopped)
  against teratogenic risk; if continued, requires more frequent level monitoring through pregnancy
  (changing renal clearance/volume of distribution) and around delivery (risk of neonatal toxicity
  and maternal dehydration/haemorrhage-related clearance changes peripartum); generally continued
  cautiously if the mother is well-stabilised on it and the risk of relapse off it is judged high,
  with specialist input.
- **Valproate:** avoided in pregnancy wherever possible given the substantial teratogenic and
  neurodevelopmental risk already detailed in §2.6 — this is one of the clearest "avoid unless truly
  no alternative" recommendations in psychiatric prescribing.
- **SSRIs:** generally considered a reasonable option in pregnancy where an antidepressant is
  needed, weighed against the risk of untreated maternal depression (which itself carries real
  risks to the pregnancy and infant); some agents have more established safety data than others,
  and a small, specific note re paroxetine's historically flagged (though debated) first-trimester
  cardiac-malformation signal is worth being aware of, without over-stating certainty. In lactation,
  most SSRIs are considered compatible with breastfeeding at low transfer into breast milk
  [EK — specialist perinatal psychiatry/Maudsley-level guidance should be consulted for individual
  agent choice; this dossier gives the framework, not a substitute for that consultation].

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- Persistent low mood beyond 2 weeks postpartum is not "just the blues" — actively reassess.
- Any postpartum patient with rapid-onset confusion, mood instability, or infant-related delusional
  content needs same-day psychiatric emergency assessment, not a routine follow-up booking.
- Stopping a mood stabiliser abruptly in pregnancy without specialist input trades a known
  teratogenic risk for an under-appreciated relapse risk — this decision is never made unilaterally
  or reflexively.

### 2.11 Somatic symptom / functional presentations and conversion

**The diagnosis-of-exclusion discipline.** Somatic symptom disorder, illness anxiety disorder, and
conversion disorder (functional neurological symptom disorder) are genuine, distressing conditions —
they are not "faking" and should never be communicated to the patient as such — but they are, and
must remain, **diagnoses of exclusion arrived at through a positive process, not a negative one by
default.** The discipline required: a proper, adequately thorough medical/neurological work-up is
completed (matched to the specific symptom presented, not an indiscriminate battery), red flags are
actively sought and excluded, and — critically — the diagnosis is supported by **positive clinical
signs** where they exist (e.g. Hoover's sign for functional leg weakness, splitting patterns
inconsistent with a single anatomical lesion, entrainment in functional tremor) rather than resting
purely on "we couldn't find anything." A diagnosis reached purely by exhaustion of investigations,
with no positive supporting features and no coherent psychological formulation, should prompt
reconsideration rather than closure.

**Discriminating features:** symptom(s) inconsistent with known anatomical/physiological patterns;
variability with attention/distraction (functional tremor entraining to an examiner-set rhythm,
functional weakness improving with distraction techniques); a temporal relationship to psychosocial
stress or a precipitating event (though this is supportive, not required, and its absence does not
exclude the diagnosis); "la belle indifférence" (a notable lack of concern about a significant
deficit) is a classically taught but unreliable sign and should not be relied upon in isolation.

**Management anchors** — a collaborative, non-confrontational explanation of the diagnosis that
validates the symptom as real (the patient is not choosing or faking it) while explaining the
functional/psychological mechanism; multidisciplinary input (physiotherapy for functional motor
symptoms has a reasonable evidence base); psychotherapy addressing underlying stressors/trauma;
avoiding repeated, unnecessary re-investigation once a competent work-up has reasonably excluded
organic disease, since repeated investigation can reinforce illness behaviour and delay recovery-
focused treatment — but this must be balanced against genuine vigilance for a missed or evolving
organic diagnosis, which does happen and is the reason the "exclusion" step must be done properly
the first time, not skipped.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- Labelling a patient "functional/somatic" prematurely, before an adequate work-up, is a recognised
  and harmful diagnostic error with real cases of missed organic disease in the literature.
- Equally, endless re-investigation once organic disease has been reasonably excluded is its own
  harm — the skill is knowing when the exclusion process is actually complete.
- Never present the diagnosis to a patient in a way that implies they are lying or attention-
  seeking — this destroys the therapeutic relationship and worsens outcomes.

### 2.12 Neurocognitive: dementia workup incl. HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND), pseudodementia

**Differential**
- *Common:* Alzheimer's disease; vascular dementia (stepwise decline, vascular risk factors, focal
  signs); mixed Alzheimer's/vascular pathology.
- *Must-not-miss (the reversible/treatable causes — the reason a "dementia workup" exists as a
  distinct clinical exercise rather than a label applied on clinical impression alone):*
  hypothyroidism; B12/folate deficiency; neurosyphilis; normal-pressure hydrocephalus (gait
  disturbance, urinary incontinence, cognitive decline triad); chronic subdural haematoma
  (especially in the elderly or anyone on anticoagulation, even with minor or unrecalled trauma);
  a space-occupying lesion; depression presenting as cognitive impairment (**pseudodementia**,
  below); delirium superimposed on an undiagnosed dementia; medication effect (anticholinergic
  burden, benzodiazepines, opioids) mimicking or worsening cognitive impairment.
- *SA-specific and not to be missed:* **HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND)** — spans a
  spectrum from asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment through mild neurocognitive disorder to
  **HIV-associated dementia**, and can occur even in patients with good virological/immunological
  control on ART, though risk is higher with a history of low CD4 nadir and poor historical
  control; presents with predominantly subcortical features (psychomotor slowing, impaired
  executive function/attention/concentration, mood change) rather than the prominent early memory
  and cortical (aphasia/apraxia/agnosia) picture more typical of Alzheimer's — this distributional
  difference is a useful discriminator; a new or progressive cognitive complaint in a person living
  with HIV needs both a HAND-specific consideration (including CNS opportunistic infection and CNS
  penetration/adherence of the ART regimen) and the standard reversible-cause screen.
- *Zebras:* Wilson's disease (young-onset), Huntington's disease (family history, chorea),
  Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (rapid course, myoclonus), frontotemporal dementia (early personality/
  behavioural change with relatively preserved memory, often younger onset than Alzheimer's —
  frequently misdiagnosed initially as a primary psychiatric or personality change).

**Discriminating history** — tempo (gradual over years = neurodegenerative; stepwise = vascular;
subacute over weeks-months = reversible cause, HAND, or rapidly progressive dementia needing urgent
work-up; abrupt = delirium, not dementia); domain pattern from collateral (memory-first vs
personality/behaviour-first vs language-first vs visuospatial-first — different dementia subtypes);
functional trajectory (instrumental activities of daily living affected before basic ADLs in
typical dementia progression); full medication review; HIV status, CD4 nadir/current, viral load,
ART adherence and regimen; vascular risk factor history; alcohol history; family history;
mood symptoms and their temporal relationship to the cognitive complaint (did low mood precede
cognitive complaints, or vice versa — informs the pseudodementia question below).

**Discriminating examination** — full neurological exam (focal signs, gait, parkinsonism, myoclonus,
primitive reflexes); formal cognitive testing (MMSE or MoCA, §5, with the SA education-adjustment
caveat); functional assessment; mood assessment (a full depression screen in every cognitive
work-up, not an afterthought).

**Pseudodementia — depression masquerading as cognitive decline.** Classic discriminators
(each individually soft, more reliable in combination) [EK]: **rapid, well-demarcated onset** (vs
the gradual, hard-to-date onset of true dementia); patients with pseudodementia tend to **emphasise
and complain about their memory difficulty prominently and with distress** ("I can't remember
anything, it's terrible"), while patients with true dementia more often **minimise, confabulate
over, or seem unconcerned about** deficits that are objectively present (anosognosia); on testing,
depressed patients often give **"don't know" answers and put in poor effort** on cognitive tasks
they may actually be capable of, whereas dementia patients tend to attempt an answer and get it
wrong (near-miss/confabulated responses) — effort and engagement with testing is itself
diagnostically informative; a clear preceding or co-occurring major depressive episode; cognitive
performance that improves with encouragement/structure in pseudodementia, less so in true dementia.
Both can, of course, coexist (depression is also a recognised risk factor for, and early feature of,
true dementia) — the point is not that they are mutually exclusive but that the distinction changes
management (treat the depression first and reassess cognition, rather than assuming irreversible
dementia).

**Investigations** — the reversible-cause screen: TFTs, B12/folate, calcium, U&E, LFT, FBC, RPR/
TPHA (neurosyphilis), HIV test if status unknown (with CD4/viral load if positive), glucose;
neuroimaging (CT or, where available, MRI) — indicated for atypical presentation, rapid progression,
young age of onset, focal neurological signs, head injury history, or any suspicion of a structural/
reversible cause (subdural, NPH, mass); consider LP if rapidly progressive dementia, CNS infection,
or CJD is a genuine consideration; formal neuropsychological testing where available for
diagnostically unclear or medico-legally significant cases.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- Labelling cognitive decline "dementia" without the reversible-cause screen risks missing a
  treatable cause (B12, thyroid, subdural, neurosyphilis) — every new cognitive presentation earns
  the screen, not just the ones that "look" reversible.
- A depressed older patient with prominent memory complaints deserves a depression treatment trial
  and cognitive reassessment before an irreversible dementia label is applied.
- HAND does not require virological failure to occur — do not reassure a well-controlled patient
  out of the differential.
- Frontotemporal dementia is frequently misread first as a personality disorder, depression, or
  "just being difficult" because memory is relatively spared early — behavioural/personality change
  as the presenting feature, especially with disinhibition, deserves this specific consideration.

### 2.13 The eating-disordered patient

**Differential** — anorexia nervosa (restricting or binge-eating/purging subtype); bulimia nervosa;
binge-eating disorder; avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID — restriction without body-
image disturbance, important not to miss in presentations without the classic weight/shape
concerns); other specified feeding or eating disorder (atypical presentations, including atypical
anorexia nervosa at a normal or above-normal weight with significant weight loss and the full
psychological/medical picture otherwise — do not dismiss eating-disorder risk purely because the
patient is not underweight).

**Discriminating history** — detailed eating pattern, restriction/bingeing/purging behaviours
(vomiting, laxative/diuretic misuse, compulsive exercise), rate and amount of recent weight loss,
menstrual history (amenorrhoea), body-image cognitions and their centrality to self-worth, medical
red-flag symptoms (syncope, palpitations, severe fatigue, chest pain), and — specifically for
refeeding-risk stratification — recent intake pattern and duration of minimal/no intake.

**Discriminating examination and refeeding-risk stratification** — vitals (bradycardia, hypotension,
orthostatic drop, hypothermia — all signal significant physiological compromise), BMI, signs of
purging (Russell's sign on knuckles, dental erosion, parotid enlargement), lanugo hair, peripheral
oedema, cardiac exam. **NICE-aligned high-refeeding-risk criteria** [EK, widely used framework]:
**any one** of BMI <16 kg/m², unintentional weight loss >15% over 3–6 months, little or no
nutritional intake for **>10 days**, or low baseline potassium/phosphate/magnesium; **or any two**
of BMI <18.5 kg/m², unintentional weight loss >10% over 3–6 months, little or no intake for **>5
days**, or a history of alcohol/drug misuse (including insulin, diuretic, or laxative misuse).

**Refeeding syndrome — the mechanism and the thresholds.** In a starved state the body shifts to
fat/protein catabolism and intracellular phosphate becomes depleted even as serum phosphate may
look deceptively normal; reintroducing carbohydrate triggers an insulin surge that drives glucose,
phosphate, potassium, and magnesium sharply **into cells**, precipitating a rapid fall in serum
**phosphate** (the hallmark and most clinically dangerous derangement), potassium, and magnesium
within the first several days of refeeding. Clinical consequences of severe hypophosphataemia
include cardiac arrhythmia and cardiac failure, respiratory muscle weakness/failure, rhabdomyolysis,
seizures, and confusion — this is a genuinely life-threatening complication of the *treatment*, not
the starvation itself, and is why refeeding is deliberately cautious rather than aggressive in
high-risk patients. **Management anchors:** in patients identified as high risk, start refeeding
**conservatively** rather than at full calculated requirement (NICE guidance: no more than 50% of
estimated energy requirement where intake has been minimal for >5 days, with even more caution —
around 5–10 kcal/kg/day as a very cautious starting point — in the most severely malnourished,
escalating over days as electrolytes and clinical status allow) [EK]; **phosphate repletion**
guidance commonly cited in the range of **0.3–0.6 mmol/kg/day** where replacement is needed [EK];
daily electrolyte monitoring (phosphate, potassium, magnesium, glucose) for at least the first week
of refeeding, more frequently in the highest-risk patients, with proactive correction of borderline-
low levels *before* they become dangerously low; thiamine supplementation alongside refeeding
(malnourished patients are also thiamine-depleted, and the same thiamine-before/with-carbohydrate
principle from alcohol withdrawal, §2.9, applies here).

**Medical admission criteria** [EK, consistent with international eating-disorder medical-
stability guidance] — indications include: severe bradycardia, significant hypotension or
orthostatic instability, hypothermia, marked electrolyte derangement, ECG abnormality (especially
QTc prolongation, a recognised consequence of purging-related electrolyte loss and of some
psychotropic medications), very low BMI or rapid recent weight loss, syncope, and any high-risk
refeeding criteria above where refeeding needs to be medically supervised and monitored rather than
managed as an outpatient.

**Consultant traps & pearls**
- A normal or above-normal-weight patient with eating-disorder behaviours and significant recent
  weight loss can still be at high medical/refeeding risk — weight/BMI alone is an incomplete risk
  marker.
- Serum phosphate can look falsely reassuring *before* refeeding starts (intracellular depletion
  not yet reflected in serum) — the risk is in the trajectory over the first days of refeeding, not
  the pre-refeeding baseline number.
- Underfeeding out of excessive caution also has real cost (delayed recovery, prolonged
  hospitalisation) — the skill is calibrated caution in genuinely high-risk patients, not blanket
  minimal feeding for everyone.

## 3. The normals

**The normal MSE, described properly** — the structure every psychiatric encounter is measured
against:
- **Appearance and behaviour:** grooming, hygiene, dress appropriate to context/weather, eye
  contact appropriate and reciprocal, psychomotor activity within normal range (neither retarded
  nor agitated), no abnormal movements, cooperative and engaged rapport-building possible.
- **Speech:** normal rate, volume, tone, spontaneity; coherent, goal-directed, no pressure, no
  poverty.
- **Mood** (patient's subjective report, in their own words) **and affect** (clinician's objective
  observation of emotional expression): affect that is full-range, appropriate to content, and
  congruent with the stated mood is normal; a normal range of reactivity to both positive and
  negative content in conversation is expected — a flat, blunted, or incongruent affect is never
  "just their personality" without active consideration of a mental state cause.
- **Thought form:** logical, linear, goal-directed thinking with normal associations between ideas;
  normal is the *absence* of tangentiality, circumstantiality, loosening of associations, flight of
  ideas, thought blocking, or perseveration.
- **Thought content:** no delusions, no overvalued ideas dominating function, no
  suicidal/homicidal ideation on direct questioning (asked, not assumed absent because not
  volunteered), no obsessions/compulsions, no preoccupations disproportionate to context. Normal
  worry and normal grief-related preoccupation (§ below) are not thought disorder.
- **Perception:** no hallucinations in any modality, no illusions, no dissociative
  depersonalisation/derealisation beyond transient, context-appropriate stress responses.
- **Cognition:** alert, oriented to time/place/person, attention intact (able to sustain and
  direct attention through the interview and on formal testing), memory grossly intact for
  recent and remote events, normal fund of knowledge and abstraction ability for educational
  background.
- **Insight and judgment:** normal insight is an accurate, nuanced understanding of one's own
  situation, appropriate to context (not simply "agrees with the doctor") — insight exists on a
  spectrum and partial insight is common and clinically informative, not a binary present/absent
  label; normal judgment is the ability to weigh a realistic course of action and its consequences
  appropriately.

**Normal grief vs depression — the discipline of not pathologising loss.** Grief is a normal,
expected, and *not inherently pathological* response to bereavement, and shares surface features
with depression (low mood, tearfulness, sleep/appetite disturbance, preoccupation with the deceased)
that make the two easy to conflate. Discriminators [EK, consistent with DSM-5-TR's guidance on
differentiating grief from a major depressive episode]: in grief, the predominant affect is a sense
of **emptiness and loss**, typically comes in **waves** often triggered by reminders of the deceased,
with preserved capacity for positive emotion, humour, and engagement between waves; self-esteem is
generally preserved (a griever misses the person, they don't typically feel globally worthless); if
self-critical thoughts occur they are usually specifically about the relationship with the deceased
("I should have visited more") rather than global self-devaluation; thoughts of death are typically
about joining or being with the deceased rather than a wish to end one's own life from
worthlessness/hopelessness. In depression, low mood is more **persistent and pervasive**, not
predominantly tied to thoughts/reminders of the loss; anhedonia is pervasive across domains, not
just related to the loss; self-worth is often globally diminished; guilt (if present) is often
generalised rather than loss-specific. Grief that is unusually prolonged, intensely and persistently
impairing well beyond expected cultural/social norms, or that meets full depressive-episode criteria
in its own right (which grief *can* evolve into, especially in vulnerable individuals) warrants
active treatment — the point of the distinction is not to withhold care from grieving patients who
need it, but to avoid reflexively medicalising a normal, adaptive process.

**Developmental/cultural norms in the SA context — ancestral communication vs psychosis.** A large
proportion of SA patients hold explanatory frameworks for illness and experience rooted in African
traditional/ancestral belief systems that exist alongside, rather than as evidence of, biomedical
psychiatric illness. **The cultural formulation discipline** [EK, consistent with DSM-5-TR's Cultural
Formulation Interview framework and SA cross-cultural psychiatry teaching] requires actively
distinguishing:
- Experiences that are **congruent with the patient's cultural/religious reference group** — e.g.
  a sense of being called by the ancestors, hearing or sensing ancestral communication, dreams
  interpreted as ancestral messages, or a diagnosis/experience of **ukuthwasa** (the calling to
  become a traditional healer/sangoma, often accompanied by a period of illness, unusual
  experiences, and social withdrawal that is understood within the cultural framework as a calling
  rather than an illness) — from
- **Genuine psychiatric symptoms** that happen to be expressed through, or overlap with, a cultural
  idiom — where the content is not shared or endorsed by the patient's cultural/religious community,
  where there is accompanying functional decline, disorganisation, risk, or other MSE features
  inconsistent with a culturally sanctioned experience, or where collateral from within the
  patient's own cultural community (not just biomedical staff) identifies the experience as outside
  the expected/accepted range.
- **Amafufunyana** — a Xhosa-language culture-bound idiom describing a state of spirit possession,
  which in some presentations correlates clinically with what biomedicine would frame as psychosis
  or dissociative phenomena, and in others is a distinct, culturally coherent experience not
  requiring psychiatric intervention — the clinical task is not to translate the cultural term
  directly into a DSM diagnosis by default, but to conduct a genuine cultural formulation interview
  (what does the patient and their community believe is happening, what do they believe caused it,
  what help do they think would help, how is it affecting function) before deciding whether
  biomedical psychiatric intervention is indicated, and if so, to integrate rather than replace the
  patient's own explanatory framework wherever possible (§6, traditional health practitioner
  collaboration).
- The practical discriminator that most reliably still separates culturally congruent experience
  from psychiatric illness, across this whole domain: **functional impact, distress, and
  organisation of the rest of the mental state.** A patient who can describe an ancestral calling
  coherently, is not distressed to the point of dysfunction, has no other MSE abnormality, and whose
  experience is validated by their own cultural community is different from a patient with the same
  surface description but disorganised thought, poor self-care, risk, and an experience their own
  family/community does not recognise as a calling.

**Normal adolescent turbulence vs prodrome.** Adolescence is normally accompanied by mood
lability, increased risk-taking, identity exploration, some conflict with parental authority, and
fluctuating self-esteem — none of which, alone, is pathological. The discriminators that should
raise concern for an emerging prodromal psychiatric illness (most often the psychosis prodrome, but
also early mood disorder) rather than normal developmental turbulence [EK]: a clear **decline from
a previous baseline** of functioning (academic, social, self-care) rather than a stable pattern of
being "difficult"; **social withdrawal that is a departure from the adolescent's own previous
sociability**, not simply normal increased privacy-seeking; new, odd, or magical beliefs that are
not shared by peers and are held with increasing conviction; perceptual disturbances; a family
history of psychotic or severe mood disorder (raises prior probability); persistent, rather than
episodic/reactive, low mood or anxiety; any suicidal ideation, self-harm, or substance use escalation,
which always warrant direct assessment regardless of whether they are ultimately judged
"developmentally normal" in degree. The single most useful practical marker across this whole
section: **trajectory relative to the individual's own baseline**, not comparison to some abstract
population norm — a gregarious teenager becoming withdrawn is more concerning than a naturally
introverted teenager remaining introverted.

## 4. Cross-cutting investigation interpretation

**The organic screen, end-to-end — what each test is for and the traps in reading it.** This is
the single most repeated workup in the entire discipline (§1, §2.2, §2.3, §2.12) — done properly,
once, at the point it matters, rather than piecemeal or after a label has already stuck.

- **Glucose:** excludes hypoglycaemia as a driver of agitation/confusion (mandatory, first,
  fastest test available — a fingerprick, not a wait for a lab result) and flags undiagnosed
  diabetes/DKA/HHS as a cause of altered mental state or new psychiatric-looking presentation.
- **U&E:** **sodium** — both hypo- and hypernatraemia cause confusion/psychiatric-looking
  presentations; hyponatraemia is a specific trap in psychiatric patients because several
  psychotropics (SSRIs — via SIADH, particularly in the elderly; carbamazepine) and
  primary polydipsia (seen in some chronic psychotic patients) can *cause* it, so a psychiatric
  patient with new confusion needs sodium checked with the specific question "is this drug-induced
  or behaviour-induced hyponatraemia" in mind, not just "is this a relapse." **Calcium** —
  hypercalcaemia causes lethargy, depression, confusion, and psychosis ("moans, groans, stones, and
  psychiatric overtones"); hypocalcaemia causes anxiety, irritability, tetany, and rarely psychosis.
  **Urea/creatinine** — uraemic encephalopathy as a cause of altered mental state; baseline renal
  function is also mandatory before/during lithium.
- **TFTs:** hyperthyroidism mimics anxiety/mania/agitation; hypothyroidism mimics depression/
  psychomotor slowing/cognitive impairment (§2.5, §2.12); check in every new significant mood or
  anxiety presentation, not only when clinically "classic."
- **LFTs:** hepatic encephalopathy as a cause of altered mental state (look for the whole picture —
  asterixis, known liver disease, precipitant); baseline and monitoring relevance for several
  psychotropics (valproate hepatotoxicity, some antipsychotics).
- **FBC:** anaemia contributing to fatigue/low mood/cognitive complaints; macrocytosis as a clue to
  alcohol use or B12/folate deficiency; the neutrophil count specifically and repeatedly for
  clozapine (§2.8, §5); leukocytosis as a supportive (non-specific) NMS finding.
- **RPR/TPHA:** screens for syphilis exposure; RPR is a non-treponemal test (can be non-reactive in
  late/latent disease and gives false positives in several unrelated conditions — pregnancy,
  autoimmune disease, other infections); TPHA/treponemal tests are more specific and stay positive
  for life even after treatment, so a positive TPHA does not by itself confirm *active* or
  *neurological* disease — if neurosyphilis is genuinely suspected clinically (cognitive decline,
  psychosis, personality change, pupillary/reflex findings) with a reactive serology, **CSF
  examination** (VDRL/treponemal testing, cell count, protein) is needed to confirm CNS
  involvement, not serology alone.
- **HIV test:** with pre-test information/consent per SA protocol, in any first psychiatric
  presentation where status is unknown, given the breadth of direct and indirect HIV-psychiatric
  overlap (§2.2, §2.12, §6); if positive, CD4 count and viral load contextualise the risk of CNS
  opportunistic infection and inform the HAND-vs-Alzheimer's-type differential.
- **B12/folate:** deficiency causes depression, cognitive impairment, and (with B12 specifically)
  can cause psychosis and subacute combined degeneration — check in any unexplained mood, cognitive,
  or psychotic presentation, especially with any peripheral neuropathy or macrocytosis.
- **Urine toxicology — what a positive/negative result actually means, and the traps:**
  - *Window periods matter and are commonly misunderstood:* a **negative** urine toxicology screen
    does **not** exclude recent use if the substance has already cleared the detectable window, and
    a **positive** result does not tell you the substance is causing *today's* presentation — it
    only tells you it was used within the detection window. Approximate single-use detection
    windows [EK, standard toxicology teaching, ranges vary with dose/chronicity/individual
    metabolism]: amphetamines/methamphetamine roughly 1–3 days; cocaine metabolites roughly 2–4
    days; opioids (varies markedly by specific opioid) roughly 1–3 days; benzodiazepines roughly
    3–7 days for short-acting, longer for long-acting agents with chronic use; alcohol itself only
    hours (though specific alcohol biomarkers can extend the window) — a blood alcohol level, not
    urine, is the relevant acute intoxication test.
  - **Cannabis is the standout exception and the classic trap:** with **chronic, heavy use**,
    cannabis metabolites (being fat-soluble and slowly released from adipose stores) can remain
    detectable in urine for **several weeks** — commonly cited up to around 30 days or more in heavy
    chronic users [EK] — meaning a positive cannabis result in a chronic user tells you almost
    nothing about *today's* mental state and must not be used to attribute a current presentation to
    "cannabis intoxication" without a much closer look at the actual temporal relationship and other
    causes.
  - **False positives** are a genuine, recurring trap: several prescribed and OTC medications can
    cross-react on immunoassay screening tests — e.g. some antihistamines, certain antidepressants,
    and some cold/decongestant preparations have been reported to give false-positive results on
    various immunoassay panels for amphetamines or other classes [EK, standard toxicology teaching]
    — any unexpected positive result that changes management should be confirmed with a more
    specific method (e.g. GC-MS) where available and where the result materially matters, rather
    than acted on from the screening immunoassay alone.

**Therapeutic drug levels — ranges and, critically, timing:**
- **Lithium:** therapeutic range approx. **0.6–1.2 mmol/L** (acute), lower for maintenance/elderly;
  drawn **12 hours post-dose** (trough) — a level at any other time is not interpretable against
  standard ranges; toxicity bands and dialysis threshold detailed in full at §2.6.
- **Valproate:** commonly cited therapeutic range roughly **50–100 mg/L (350–700 µmol/L)** for mood
  stabilisation [EK — ranges vary by lab/reference and indication; levels are less tightly
  correlated with clinical effect in bipolar disorder than in epilepsy, and clinical response often
  guides dosing more than the level alone]; trough level (pre-dose) is the conventional timing.
- **Carbamazepine:** commonly cited therapeutic range roughly **4–12 mg/L (17–50 µmol/L)** [EK];
  trough (pre-dose) level; note carbamazepine is a potent hepatic enzyme inducer and induces its own
  metabolism over the first weeks of treatment (autoinduction), so a level and dose adequate at
  week 1 may become subtherapeutic by week 3–4 without a dose change — a specific, easily missed
  trap.
- **Clozapine:** plasma level monitoring (where available) is used mainly to check adherence, to
  investigate non-response despite an apparently adequate dose, and to investigate toxicity;
  commonly cited target range for efficacy in refractory illness is roughly **350–600 µg/L (some
  references extend to ~1000 µg/L before toxicity concern)** [EK — verify against local lab
  reference range and specialist guidance]; smoking status is clinically important because tobacco
  smoke (not nicotine itself) induces clozapine metabolism — a patient who stops smoking on
  admission (common, given ward smoking restrictions) can develop a clinically significant rise in
  clozapine level and toxicity risk within days if the dose is not proactively reviewed, and
  conversely restarting smoking after discharge can drop levels and precipitate relapse.

**CK interpretation — NMS vs IM-injection vs restraint, the trap of anchoring on one number.**
CK can be elevated by (a) a simple IM injection itself (a modest, transient rise, generally
low-hundreds range [EK]), (b) physical struggle/restraint/prolonged agitation (can reach the
low-to-mid thousands [EK]), and (c) true NMS or severe rhabdomyolysis from any cause (often reaching
the tens of thousands) — the number alone, especially a single number without a trend, without a
value before the intervention, and without correlating it against the rest of the clinical picture
(rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, mental state), is easy to over- or under-interpret;
always ask what happened physically to this patient in the hours before the sample, and trend the
value rather than reacting to one point.

**When CT brain / LP / EEG are indicated in psychiatry** — the practical thresholds used across
this dossier, gathered in one place:
- **CT brain:** any first psychotic or manic episode after age 40–45; any focal neurological sign;
  head injury (recent or with a plausible unwitnessed mechanism, e.g. a fall during intoxication);
  new seizure; rapidly progressive cognitive decline; atypical or treatment-resistant presentation
  where an organic driver has not been adequately excluded; catatonia of unclear cause.
- **Lumbar puncture:** suspected CNS infection (fever, headache, meningism, rapid decline, seizure
  — especially with HIV/immunosuppression in the picture); suspected autoimmune/paraneoplastic
  encephalitis (young patient, rapid psychiatric prodrome, then seizures/dyskinesias/autonomic
  instability — send anti-NMDA-receptor and other autoimmune antibody panels where available);
  suspected neurosyphilis with reactive serology and a clinical picture consistent with CNS
  involvement; rapidly progressive dementia where CJD or another CNS process is a genuine
  consideration. CT (to exclude a mass lesion/raised ICP) generally precedes LP where there is any
  suspicion of raised intracranial pressure or a space-occupying process, per standard safety
  practice.
- **EEG:** suspected seizure (including non-convulsive status epilepticus presenting as fluctuating
  confusion — an important and under-considered differential for "atypical delirium" or "atypical
  catatonia"); suspected encephalitis; catatonia of unclear cause (can show characteristic though
  non-diagnostic patterns); a supportive (not stand-alone-diagnostic) role in assessing unexplained
  fluctuating consciousness.

## 5. Scores & structured tools

**The MSE** — structure detailed in full at §3 (normal) and used as the universal structured
psychiatric assessment across every section of this dossier: appearance/behaviour, speech, mood/
affect, thought form, thought content, perception, cognition, insight/judgment.

**CAM (Confusion Assessment Method)** — delirium screening (§2.3). Positive (delirium present)
requires: **(1) acute onset and fluctuating course** AND **(2) inattention**, PLUS **either (3)
disorganised thinking OR (4) altered level of consciousness** [EK, standard CAM algorithm].
**4AT** — a faster bedside four-item alternative widely used where a rapid screen is needed:
alertness, **A**MT4 (age, date of birth, place, current year), attention (months of the year
backwards), and acute change or fluctuating course; scored 0 (unlikely delirium/significant
cognitive impairment) to a maximum around 12, with a defined cut-off (commonly ≥4) flagging
possible delirium requiring fuller assessment [EK].

**CIWA-Ar** — 10-item alcohol withdrawal severity scale (nausea/vomiting, tremor, paroxysmal
sweats, anxiety, agitation, tactile disturbances, auditory disturbances, visual disturbances,
headache/fullness in head, orientation/clouding of sensorium), each scored typically 0–7 (orientation
item 0–4), total score bands: **≤10 mild**, **~10–15 moderate**, **≥15–16 severe** (§2.9) [EK].

**COWS** — 11-item clinician-administered opioid withdrawal scale, total 0–48: **5–12 mild,
13–24 moderate, 25–36 moderately severe, ≥37 severe** (§2.9) [EK].

**PHQ-9** — 9-item depression severity/screening tool scored 0–3 per item (total 0–27) mapping the
DSM depressive symptom criteria plus a specific suicidality item; commonly cited severity bands
[EK]: **0–4 minimal, 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15–19 moderately severe, 20–27 severe.** Used for
monitoring treatment response over time as much as for initial screening; the suicidality item
(item 9) response is never ignored regardless of the total score.

**GAD-7** — 7-item anxiety severity/screening tool, 0–3 per item (total 0–21); commonly cited bands
[EK]: **0–4 minimal, 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15–21 severe.**

**MMSE/MoCA** — brief cognitive screening instruments (§2.12). MMSE scored out of 30, commonly
cited rough severity bands for dementia staging once a diagnosis is otherwise established [EK]:
**≥25 minimal/no impairment on this instrument alone, ~21–24 mild, ~10–20 moderate, <10 severe**
(bands vary by source and should not be used as a stand-alone diagnostic threshold). **MoCA** is
generally more sensitive to mild cognitive impairment than MMSE, particularly for executive
function and subtler deficits, with a commonly used cut-off around **<26/30** flagging possible
impairment. **The SA education-adjustment caveat is essential:** both instruments are strongly
influenced by educational attainment and language/literacy, and applying an unadjusted cut-off in a
population with variable formal education (common across SA, particularly older rural patients) risks
systematically over-diagnosing cognitive impairment in patients with limited schooling and
under-diagnosing it in highly educated patients performing below their own true baseline — an
education-adjusted interpretation (some MoCA scoring protocols add a point for ≤12 years of
education; broader clinical judgement is needed where formal adjustment norms for the local
population/language are not available) and, wherever possible, comparison against the individual's
own estimated premorbid level of function is essential rather than applying a single population
cut-off uncritically.

**Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) — concept.** A structured suicide-risk
assessment instrument distinguishing **ideation** (from a wish to be dead, through active ideation
with a method but no plan/intent, to active ideation with a specific plan and intent) from
**behaviour** (preparatory acts, aborted/interrupted attempts, actual attempts), used to structure
the risk conversation and standardise language across clinicians rather than to produce a single
predictive score (§2.4's caution about over-relying on any single instrument for prediction applies
here too — its value is in ensuring the full range of ideation/behaviour is systematically asked
about, not skipped).

**HCR-20 — concept.** A structured professional judgment instrument for violence-risk assessment
(used in forensic/high-risk settings more than routine general psychiatry) combining **H**istorical
items (e.g. previous violence, young age at first violent incident, relationship instability,
employment problems, substance use problems, major mental illness, psychopathy, early maladjustment,
personality disorder, prior supervision failure), **C**linical (current) items (insight, negative
attitudes, active symptoms of major mental illness, impulsivity, unresponsive to treatment), and
**R**isk management items (feasibility of plans, exposure to destabilisers, lack of personal
support, non-compliance with remediation, stress) — a structured-professional-judgment tool, not a
simple additive score, intended to inform (not replace) clinical judgement about violence risk
[EK].

**EPSE scales — AIMS concept.** The **Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS)** is the standard
structured instrument for rating tardive dyskinesia and other antipsychotic-induced abnormal
movements, systematically rating movement severity across facial/oral, extremity, and trunk regions
plus global severity/incapacitation items, used at baseline before starting an antipsychotic long
term and periodically thereafter to detect emerging tardive dyskinesia early [EK]. Distinct
structured tools also exist for acute extrapyramidal side effects generally (parkinsonism, dystonia,
akathisia) — the practical clinical point (already made at §2.8) is that akathisia in particular
needs a *specific*, direct question about subjective inner restlessness, since it is easily missed
on observation alone.

**Clozapine ANC (absolute neutrophil count) monitoring schedule** (§2.8 for full clinical context)
— **weekly** for the first **18 weeks**, then **fortnightly** to week **52**, then **monthly**
indefinitely while on clozapine [EK, standard international protocol reflected in SA specialist
practice]; a defined ANC threshold triggers increased-frequency monitoring, and a lower defined
threshold (agranulocytosis range) triggers immediate, non-tapered discontinuation — exact numeric
ANC action thresholds should be taken from the specific monitoring protocol/registry in force at
the prescribing site rather than assumed, as different national registries (e.g. UK vs US vs local
SA specialist-unit protocols) have used slightly different threshold conventions historically.

## 6. SA-specific reality

**This section is the discipline's defining layer.** A psychiatric assessment in South Africa is
inseparable from the legal pathway it sits inside, the resource level of the facility it happens
at, and the epidemiological/cultural context of the patient in front of you. Get the MHCA pathway
wrong and a clinically correct decision becomes an unlawful detention or an unprotected discharge;
misjudge the district-hospital reality and a plan that would work at an academic hospital fails a
patient who has no psychiatrist within reach.

### 6.1 The Mental Health Care Act 17 of 2002 pathway, end-to-end

**The three categories of care, treatment and rehabilitation** [EK, MHCA ss 25–37; primary Act
text and secondary sources consistently describe this structure — direct fetch of the gazetted Act
text returned HTTP 403 on this session, so specific section numbers below should be verified against
the current in-force Act/Regulations before being hard-coded into a clinical tool]:
- **Voluntary care:** the user consents to admission/treatment in the ordinary way any medical
  patient would; can request discharge (subject to normal clinical safety considerations, but
  without the compulsory-admission machinery below).
- **Assisted care:** for a user who is **unable to consent** due to their mental health status (lacks
  capacity for this decision) but **does not refuse** the care — a substituted-decision pathway,
  not a "the patient objects and we override them" pathway; requires an application plus the
  practitioner assessment process below.
- **Involuntary care:** for a user who **refuses** care, or who is unable to consent **and** whose
  refusal/inability combined with the risk (to self, others, or through severe self-neglect/grave
  disability) or treatment need cannot otherwise be met — the most restrictive pathway, with the
  most procedural safeguards.

**Who may apply.** For assisted or involuntary care, an application is made by a person with an
interest in the wellbeing of the user — the Act specifies categories including the user's **spouse,
next of kin, partner, associate, parent, or guardian** — made under oath/affirmation, setting out
the grounds for believing the user needs assisted or involuntary care [EK]. This application is
submitted to **the head of a health establishment**.

**The two mental health care practitioner assessments.** Before assisted or involuntary care is
approved, **two separate mental health care practitioners** (each meeting the Act's definition —
which includes psychiatrists, other medical practitioners, and other registered/qualified mental
health professionals depending on the specific role required) must examine the user **independently**
and provide their own written findings — this two-practitioner independent-assessment requirement is
a core safeguard against a single clinician's judgment (or bias) being sufficient to compel care,
and both assessments are required to support the head of the establishment's decision.

**The MHCA forms — what each one does** [EK for the numbering/purpose mapping below; treat the
higher-numbered forms, and any exact section cross-references, as needing verification against the
current Regulations before being encoded as fixed clinical-workflow logic, since automated fetch of
the primary gazetted Regulations text failed (HTTP 403) in this session]:
- **MHCA 01** — a reporting form used by any person/health establishment providing care to, or
  admitting, a mental health care user, submitted to the relevant Review Board (a general reporting/
  notification mechanism into the oversight system).
- **MHCA 02** — used to report abuse, exploitation, or degrading treatment of a mental health care
  user to the Review Board (a patient-protection reporting mechanism, distinct from the clinical
  admission pathway).
- **MHCA 04** — the **application** for assisted or involuntary care, treatment and rehabilitation,
  made under oath by the qualifying applicant (spouse/next of kin/partner/associate/parent/
  guardian) and submitted to the head of the health establishment — the document that starts the
  compulsory pathway.
- **MHCA 05** — the **independent written findings** of each of the two mental health care
  practitioners following their separate examinations of the user, submitted in support of (or
  against) the application.
- **MHCA 06** — completed during the **72-hour assessment period**, reporting on the user's physical
  and mental health status and treatment recommendations arising from that assessment.
- **MHCA 07** — the head of the health establishment's **notice of decision** on the application for
  involuntary (or assisted) care — approving or declining further care, treatment and rehabilitation
  following the 72-hour assessment.
- **MHCA 08** — used where the head of the establishment, after the 72-hour assessment, is of the
  opinion the user requires **further involuntary care on an inpatient basis** — this is the request
  submitted **to the Mental Health Review Board** for the Board's approval of continued
  involuntary care beyond the initial assessment window.
- **MHCA 09** — a related notice to the Review Board following the 72-hour assessment period,
  informing the Board of the outcome/status (the exact 08-vs-09 division of function should be
  confirmed against the current Regulations for any tool encoding this — different secondary
  sources describe the 08/09 pairing slightly differently, which is itself a signal to verify
  against the primary Regulations rather than assume).
- **MHCA 14** — used to authorise continued admission following the Review Board's subsequent
  (commonly cited as a 30-day-linked) investigation/review of an involuntary case.
- **MHCA 15** — used where a user or someone on their behalf wishes to **appeal** a health
  establishment's decision, escalating to the High Court.
- **MHCA 22** — completed when the **South African Police Service hands over** a person suspected of
  being mentally ill (or severely/profoundly intellectually disabled) and believed likely to inflict
  serious harm, to a health establishment (the operational record of the s40 SAPS pathway below).
- Numerous other forms exist across the full Regulations (periodic review reports, forms for
  specific sub-populations, forensic-linked forms, etc.) — this dossier lists the forms most
  operationally relevant to a general adult psychiatric admission pathway at ward level; a
  production clinical tool encoding the full form set should be built against the current gazetted
  Regulations text directly, not against this secondary summary alone.

**The 72-hour assessment — what it is FOR, where it happens, and whose obligation it is.** The
72-hour assessment is a **mandatory period of observation, examination, and assessment** at a
**designated health establishment** to determine whether a user referred for assisted or involuntary
care in fact meets the criteria for further compulsory care, or whether they can be released/treated
voluntarily — it exists specifically so that the compulsory-care decision is not made on the
strength of a single first assessment (often made under crisis conditions) but is confirmed or
overturned after a defined period of proper clinical observation. **Critically: this is a general
hospital-system obligation, not something that only happens on a dedicated psychiatric ward.** Any
designated health establishment — which in practice, especially in under-resourced districts, is
often the general medical/surgical ward of the local hospital, because that is where the patient
physically is and where the referral originates — is where this obligation sits; a district hospital
without a psychiatric ward, or without a psychiatrist on site, still has a legal duty to complete a
proper 72-hour assessment for a patient referred into this pathway, using the general medical staff
and facilities available, with telephonic/referral support from psychiatric services where needed
(§6.4). This is one of the most operationally important and most commonly misunderstood points in
the whole Act at ground level: junior doctors sometimes assume the 72-hour clock is "someone else's
problem" once a psychiatric referral has been made, when in fact the obligation to conduct and
document the assessment, and to act on its outcome within the statutory window, sits with the
admitting/treating team at the designated establishment regardless of speciality.

**Mental Health Review Board.** Each service area has a Review Board (typically including a
legal practitioner, a mental health care practitioner, and a community member, per the Act's
composition requirements [EK]) with functions including: approving continued involuntary care
beyond the 72-hour assessment (via MHCA 08/09 as above); receiving and investigating complaints/
reports (including MHCA 01 and MHCA 02 reports); reviewing periodic reports on involuntary and
assisted users at prescribed intervals to ensure ongoing compulsory care remains justified (not a
one-off decision left unreviewed); and hearing certain applications/appeals within its jurisdiction
before matters escalate to the High Court (MHCA 15).

**Periodic reports.** Involuntary (and assisted) status is not a permanent, unreviewed label —
the Act requires periodic clinical review and reporting on whether the user still meets criteria for
compulsory care, with the Review Board providing external oversight of this ongoing process — a
patient's status must be actively re-justified over time, not simply carried forward by inertia.

### 6.2 Criminal Procedure Act 51 of 1977, ss 77–79 — observation/state patients

Where an accused person's mental state is in question in the context of a criminal charge, the
**Criminal Procedure Act ss 77–79** governs two related but distinct questions [EK]:
- **Fitness to stand trial (capacity to understand and participate in proceedings)** — assessed
  where there is doubt about whether the accused can follow the proceedings, instruct a legal
  representative, and participate meaningfully; if found unfit, the trial process is altered
  (proceedings may be directed toward a different disposition rather than a standard trial).
- **Criminal capacity at the time of the offence** — a separate question from fitness to stand
  trial: whether the accused, at the time of the alleged offence, had the capacity to appreciate
  the wrongfulness of the act and to act in accordance with that appreciation, requiring evaluation
  of the accused's mental state at the time of the alleged offence — this can be assessed jointly
  with or separately from fitness to stand trial; a finding of lack of criminal capacity at the
  time of the offence can lead to a finding that the accused is not criminally responsible.
- **Court-directed observation** — the court can direct a period of psychiatric observation (at a
  designated facility, sometimes for a period on the order of 30 days, extendable [EK]) specifically
  to inform these questions, generating a formal report to the court.
- **State patients** — where a person is found not criminally responsible by reason of mental
  illness, or unfit to stand trial and requiring involuntary detention, they may be declared a
  **state patient** and detained/treated at a designated psychiatric facility under a distinct legal
  status from a standard MHCA involuntary user — state patient status carries its own review and
  discharge pathway (typically requiring court and/or Review Board and Department of Health
  involvement before any change in status), and management of a state patient is a specialised
  forensic-psychiatry function, not routine general adult ward practice — a district-level clinician's
  practical role is usually limited to initial identification, safe holding, and referral/liaison
  with forensic psychiatric services rather than independently managing a state patient long-term.

### 6.3 SAPS involvement — s40 MHCA

Under **s40 of the MHCA**, a police official who reasonably believes a person is mentally ill and
likely to inflict serious harm to themselves or others may **apprehend that person and take them to
a health establishment** for a 72-hour assessment, rather than to a police cell — this is a welfare/
public-safety power distinct from an arrest, and its correct use (and correct documentation, on
**MHCA 22**, §6.1) protects both the patient (who should not be held in a police cell for a mental
health crisis) and the receiving health establishment (which needs to know the legal basis on which
the patient arrived). In practice, this is one of the more common routes by which an unknown, acutely
disturbed patient with no prior psychiatric contact arrives at a district hospital casualty
department out of hours, and the admitting clinician needs to recognise the s40/MHCA-22 basis of the
referral and begin the 72-hour assessment process correctly from that point, rather than treating it
as an undocumented walk-in.

### 6.4 The district reality

**No psychiatrist on site is the norm, not the exception**, across the great majority of South
Africa's district hospitals [EK, consistent with the well-documented severe shortage and uneven
geographic distribution of psychiatrists in the SA public sector]. The practical consequences that
shape everyday decision-making:
- **Telephonic consultation** with a regional/tertiary psychiatric service (or a visiting/outreach
  psychiatrist on a fixed schedule) is frequently the only specialist input available in real time —
  meaning the district MO's own history, MSE, organic screen, and documentation quality *are* the
  data the specialist consultation is based on; a poor local assessment produces a poor telephonic
  recommendation, so the discipline of doing the organic screen and MSE properly (§1–§4) is not
  academic, it is the actual safety mechanism in a resource-constrained system.
- **Transfer criteria** — the practical threshold for transferring a patient to a facility with
  psychiatric beds/specialist input is generally some combination of: diagnostic uncertainty with an
  organic cause not excludable locally (needs CT/LP/specialist neuro-psychiatric input); a
  treatment-emergent emergency beyond local monitoring capacity (severe NMS/serotonin syndrome
  needing higher-level supportive/ICU-level care, severe lithium toxicity needing dialysis, severe
  DTs needing intensive monitoring); a legal/forensic complexity (CPA ss77-79 observation, a state
  patient); ongoing risk that cannot be safely contained with the local facility's staffing/physical
  environment (no safe seclusion space, inadequate nursing ratios for 1:1 observation); or a
  treatment need beyond the local EML/formulary and local prescriber scope (e.g. clozapine
  initiation, which requires the monitoring infrastructure of a specialist unit, §2.8/§5). The
  decision is a clinical-and-logistic judgement made in conversation with the receiving service, not
  a fixed checklist, but these are the recurring drivers.
- **The 72-hour clock runs regardless of resource level** (§6.1) — a district hospital cannot
  suspend the statutory assessment obligation because it lacks a psychiatrist; it must complete the
  assessment with the staff and support (including telephonic specialist input) available to it.

### 6.5 HIV-psychiatry integration

- **Efavirenz neuropsychiatric effects** — vivid/abnormal dreams, dizziness, sleep disturbance, and
  mood disturbance (including depression and, less commonly, psychosis or suicidality) are a
  recognised class effect, most prominent in the first weeks after initiation but persisting or
  emerging later in a meaningful minority of patients; any new-onset or worsening psychiatric
  symptom temporally linked to efavirenz initiation or dose warrants a direct conversation with the
  treating ART clinician about whether a **regimen switch** (to a non-efavirenz-based regimen) is
  appropriate, rather than only adding psychiatric medication on top of an agent that may itself be
  driving the presentation (§2.5). Current (2023) NDoH ART guidelines have in any event moved
  first-line regimens increasingly toward dolutegravir-based combinations rather than efavirenz for
  most new initiations, which has reduced but not eliminated this issue in patients still on
  legacy efavirenz-based regimens [EK — verify current first-line regimen detail against the
  in-force 2023 ART guidelines directly].
- **HAND** — spectrum of HIV-associated cognitive impairment, full detail at §2.12; can occur
  despite virological suppression; needs to be distinguished from other dementia causes and from
  CNS opportunistic disease using the standard reversible-cause screen (§4) plus HIV-specific
  work-up (CD4 nadir/current, viral load, consideration of CNS opportunistic infection where the
  presentation or immune status suggests it).
- **ART adherence in severe mental illness (SMI)** — patients with SMI (schizophrenia, bipolar
  disorder, severe depression) living with HIV face compounded adherence barriers: cognitive
  impairment, disorganisation during acute episodes, stigma (layered — both HIV- and mental-
  illness-related), and the practical disruption of psychiatric admission/relapse to a stable daily
  medication routine; integrated care (ensuring ART continuity is explicitly planned for at every
  psychiatric admission and during acute relapse, not assumed to continue by default) is a
  recognised gap and an explicit point of attention in SA HIV-psychiatry integration efforts [EK].

### 6.6 Methamphetamine epidemiology (Western Cape)

Methamphetamine ("tik") remains the dominant substance driving specialist substance-use treatment
admissions in the Western Cape, reported at **approximately 43.5% of all treatment admissions in
the province in the first half of 2025** — the highest proportion of any SA province, and broadly
consistent with the elevated levels the province has recorded through the past decade (per
SACENDU-linked reporting) [WebSearch-sourced secondary reporting — treat the specific current
percentage as illustrative of a well-established, long-running pattern rather than a figure to cite
as if directly verified against the primary SACENDU bulletin PDF, which returned HTTP 403 to direct
fetch in this session]. The clinical implication for a Western Cape (and increasingly other
provinces') clinician: methamphetamine intoxication, psychosis, and withdrawal (§2.9) should sit
high in the differential for almost any acute agitation, first-episode-psychosis-looking, or
mood-disturbance presentation in a patient from an affected community, and the organic-exclusion
reflex (§1) should specifically and routinely include a methamphetamine-use history even where not
volunteered.

### 6.7 Traditional health practitioners — collaboration, not dismissal

A large proportion of SA patients consult a traditional health practitioner (sangoma/traditional
healer, or a faith healer/prophet in some communities) before, alongside, or instead of biomedical
psychiatric care, and traditional/ancestral explanatory frameworks for illness (§3's cultural
formulation discipline) are frequently held **alongside**, not instead of, an acceptance of
biomedical treatment when both are offered respectfully [EK]. The consultant-level practice is
**collaboration, not dismissal**: actively asking about traditional healer involvement without
judgement, understanding what the patient and family believe is happening and what treatment they
have already sought or received, and — where safe and appropriate — supporting concurrent
engagement with a traditional healer rather than presenting biomedical psychiatric care as
mutually exclusive with it. This matters practically for adherence (a patient who feels their belief
system was dismissed is less likely to engage with or trust psychiatric follow-up) and for safety
(understanding what remedies/substances a traditional healer may have prescribed is relevant to a
full medication history, including potential interactions or toxicity from certain traditional
preparations).

### 6.8 Notifiable/medico-legal touchpoints

- **MHCA reporting obligations** — abuse/exploitation/degrading treatment of a mental health care
  user must be reported (MHCA 02, §6.1); admissions and certain events must be reported to the
  Review Board (MHCA 01).
- **Child protection** — standard SA child-protection reporting obligations apply in full where a
  psychiatric assessment (of a parent, a minor patient, or collateral history) reveals a child at
  risk of abuse or neglect — this sits alongside, not instead of, the psychiatric management plan.
- **Firearms and driving** — a clinician identifying a patient whose mental state creates a genuine
  safety risk around firearm possession or driving has a professional and, in some circumstances, a
  legal responsibility to address this directly (advising the patient, and escalating through the
  appropriate reporting channel where the risk is not otherwise mitigated) — a routine and
  easily-overlooked medico-legal touchpoint in everyday psychiatric practice, particularly relevant
  in acute mania, psychosis, and severe intoxication presentations.
- **Consent and capacity documentation** — every capacity assessment underlying a treatment decision
  (§1) should be explicitly documented for the specific decision assessed, not inferred later from
  the outcome — this is both good clinical practice and the primary medico-legal record if a
  decision is later challenged.
- **Death in custody/on a psychiatric ward** — any unexpected death of a mental health care user,
  particularly one under involuntary or assisted status, triggers standard medico-legal reporting
  obligations (inquest referral, internal mortality review) in addition to the clinical debrief.

### 6.9 The EML psychotropic formulary at district hospital level — what an intern can actually prescribe

At **Adult Hospital Level** (district hospital, non-specialist prescriber), the EML psychotropic
formulary is deliberately narrower than what a specialist psychiatric unit can offer, built around a
small number of well-established, affordable, widely-available agents per drug class rather than the
full range used in better-resourced settings [EK — the specific in-force list should always be
verified against the current edition of the Adult Hospital Level STG/EML, since this is a formulary
that is periodically reviewed and updated by NEMLC; the framework below reflects the well-established
general shape of SA public-sector psychotropic prescribing at this level rather than a line-by-line
current-edition citation, given that direct fetch of the current gazetted EML PDF returned HTTP 403
in this session]:
- **First-generation antipsychotics** — **haloperidol** and **chlorpromazine** are the mainstay
  EML antipsychotics at this level, both for acute (including rapid tranquillisation, §2.1) and
  maintenance use, reflecting their low cost, broad availability, and long track record, at the cost
  of a higher EPSE burden than newer agents.
- **Second-generation antipsychotics** — **risperidone** is the most consistently available
  second-generation option at district/primary level in the SA public sector, used where a
  lower-EPSE-burden option is indicated (e.g. sensitivity to EPSE, first-episode psychosis where
  minimising early treatment-emergent side effects supports engagement/adherence); other
  second-generation agents (olanzapine, quetiapine, and clozapine specifically) are generally
  reserved for specialist-initiated or higher-level-of-care prescribing, with **clozapine in
  particular never appropriately initiated at district level** given its mandatory specialist
  haematological/cardiac monitoring infrastructure requirement (§2.8, §5).
- **Antidepressants** — an SSRI (**fluoxetine** is the most consistently EML-available SSRI at this
  level) and **amitriptyline** (a tricyclic, cheap and effective but with a narrower safety margin in
  overdose and a heavier side-effect/interaction burden — a specific prescribing caution in any
  patient with active suicidal ideation and access to a large supply, given TCA cardiotoxicity in
  overdose) form the mainstay district-level antidepressant options.
- **Mood stabilisers** — **lithium** and **sodium valproate** are the district-level mainstay mood
  stabilisers (with the childbearing-potential caution on valproate, §2.6, applying with particular
  force at this level of care where specialist reproductive-psychiatry input may not be readily
  available for the risk-benefit conversation); **carbamazepine** is also available as an option;
  **lamotrigine** (for bipolar depression/maintenance specifically) has a narrower role given its
  slow titration requirement and the SJS risk, and is more likely to be a specialist-initiated
  choice than a first district-level prescribing decision.
- **Benzodiazepines** — **diazepam** and **lorazepam** are the mainstay district-level agents (for
  acute anxiety/agitation, alcohol withdrawal, and rapid tranquillisation, §2.1/§2.9), prescribed
  with the standard dependence-risk caution against open-ended maintenance use.
- **Anti-EPSE agents** — **biperiden** (anticholinergic, for acute dystonia and parkinsonian EPSE)
  is the district-level mainstay (§2.8); **promethazine** doubles as both a rapid-tranquillisation
  sedative and an anti-dystonic option given its own anticholinergic/antihistaminic action.
- **The practical prescribing discipline this implies for an intern/MO:** know this narrower list
  well rather than reaching for a specialist-level agent from training/textbook knowledge that is
  simply not stocked or not appropriate to initiate at this level of care; where the clinical
  picture genuinely needs an agent outside this list (clozapine-eligible treatment-resistant illness,
  a complex bipolar-in-pregnancy decision, a second antipsychotic trial after two adequate trials
  have failed), that is itself the signal to refer/escalate rather than improvise outside the local
  formulary and prescribing scope.
