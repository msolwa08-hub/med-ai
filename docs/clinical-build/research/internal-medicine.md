# Internal Medicine — Consultant Depth Dossier

*Target reader: the intern/MO on a SA state-hospital medical ward at 02:00, and the MedAI
engine standing behind them. This is the depth reference, not a summary. It goes deeper than
`docs/clinical-foundations.md` (Internal Medicine) — that page is the breadth scaffold; this is
the reasoning underneath it.*

## 1. The consultant's mental model

**The three questions behind every medical admission.** Before the differential, a physician is
answering: (1) *How sick, how fast?* — is this a resuscitation, a same-shift problem, or a
work-up? Tempo (hyperacute/acute/subacute/chronic) reframes the entire differential more than
any single symptom. (2) *What is the HIV/TB status?* — in a SA state hospital this is not a
subspecialty question, it is the axis the whole differential rotates on. An unknown HIV status is
an incomplete history. (3) *What is the one reversible thing I must not miss right now?* —
glucose, potassium, sepsis source, an ECG, a drug.

**The things a physician is always secretly ruling out** [EK]:
- **Hypoglycaemia** in anyone with altered mental status — a finger-prick before anything else.
- **Hyperkalaemia** in anyone with AKI, on an ACEi/ARB/spironolactone/trimethoprim, or crushed —
  an ECG before the potassium result comes back if the story fits.
- **Sepsis** hiding behind a vague presentation — lactate, source, antibiotics <1h.
- **TB** behind almost any subacute constitutional illness — it is the great mimic here.
- **A drug or a toxin** as the cause — always re-read the drug chart and ask about traditional
  medicines, OTC NSAIDs, and deliberate self-harm.
- **A completable emergency**: MI, PE, meningitis, subarachnoid, aortic dissection, ectopic (in a
  woman of childbearing age — always a βhCG).

**Tempo discipline.** Hyperacute (seconds–minutes: vascular — MI, stroke, SAH, dissection, PE,
arrhythmia, seizure). Acute (hours–days: infection, metabolic, decompensation). Subacute
(weeks: TB, malignancy, subacute infection, autoimmune, endocrine). Chronic (months: the
above, plus degenerative/organ failure). When the tempo and the proposed diagnosis disagree,
distrust the diagnosis.

**The consultant tempo of the ward round**: sickest first, then the undifferentiated, then the
"awaiting X". Every patient gets a re-stated problem list (not a diagnosis list — a *problem*
list: "AKI on CKD", "hyponatraemia 118", "new AF with RVR"), and every problem gets a plan with
a *trend to watch* and a *trigger to escalate*.

**Reason in syndromes, then in systems, then in mechanisms.** "Dyspnoea" → is it lung, heart,
blood (anaemia), acid (compensatory), or brain (central)? Only then the disease. This mechanistic
layer is what separates a consultant differential from a memorised list.

## 2. Presenting syndromes — the full set, each worked to depth

> Format per syndrome: **Differential (3 tiers)** · **Discriminating history** · **Discriminating
> exam** · **Ix + interpretation nuance** · **Management anchors (SA STG/EML)** · **Traps & pearls**.

### 2.1 Dyspnoea / shortness of breath
**Differential.**
- *Common:* pneumonia (incl. PJP in HIV), acute LVF/pulmonary oedema, COPD/asthma exacerbation,
  pleural effusion (TB, parapneumonic, malignant, transudative), anaemia, PTB.
- *Dangerous:* PE, tension pneumothorax, acute coronary syndrome presenting as dyspnoea
  ("angina-equivalent", esp. diabetic/elderly), pulmonary oedema from flash HTN/AKI, severe
  metabolic acidosis (Kussmaul — DKA, salicylate, uraemia, lactic), upper-airway obstruction /
  anaphylaxis, massive effusion/tamponade.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* PJP (subacute exertional dyspnoea + dry cough + desaturation on
  exertion + clear or ground-glass CXR in HIV, CD4<200), pulmonary hypertension (schistosomiasis,
  HIV, connective-tissue disease), lymphangitis carcinomatosa, chronic thromboembolic PH,
  neuromuscular hypoventilation (Guillain–Barré, myasthenia — check FVC not just SpO₂),
  methaemoglobinaemia (dapsone — saturation gap), high-output failure (severe anaemia,
  thyrotoxicosis, beriberi/thiamine), cardiac tamponade (TB pericarditis).

**Discriminating history.** Orthopnoea/PND/leg swelling → cardiac. Fever + productive cough →
pneumonia. Pleuritic + unilateral leg swelling/immobility/malignancy → PE. Dry cough + weeks +
HIV → PJP/TB. Wheeze + triggers → asthma/COPD. Sudden onset at rest → PE, pneumothorax,
arrhythmia. Orthopnoea that is actually *bendopnoea* or platypnoea (rare) redirects. Weight of
sputum, haemoptysis, night sweats → TB.

**Discriminating exam.** RR and *SpO₂ on exertion* (walk them — desaturation unmasks PJP/ILD).
JVP↑ + bibasal crepitations + S3 → LVF. Unilateral dullness + reduced breath sounds → effusion;
hyper-resonance + tracheal deviation → pneumothorax. Fine bibasal velcro crepitations → fibrosis.
Loud P2, RV heave, TR murmur → pulmonary HTN. Pulsus paradoxus → tamponade/severe asthma.
Kussmaul breathing (deep, sighing) with no lung pathology → metabolic acidosis — check a gas and
a glucose.

**Ix + nuance.** CXR (interstitial vs alveolar vs effusion vs pneumothorax; a *normal* CXR with
profound hypoxia = PE or PJP or shunt until proven otherwise). ABG: the A–a gradient separates
hypoventilation (normal gradient, high CO₂) from V/Q mismatch/shunt (widened gradient). ECG +
troponin (ACS, PE — S1Q3T3 is neither sensitive nor specific; sinus tachycardia is the commonest
PE ECG). NT-proBNP if available (a *normal* BNP makes cardiac cause unlikely; renal failure and
sepsis raise it non-specifically). D-dimer only useful to *exclude* PE in low-probability (Wells)
— it is useless as a rule-in and is raised by TB/HIV/malignancy/pregnancy here. Bedside POCUS:
B-lines (oedema), lung sliding (excludes pneumothorax), effusion, IVC, cardiac function.

**Management anchors.** Sit up, O₂ to target (88–92% if COPD/CO₂ retainer risk, else 94–98%).
LVF: IV furosemide, nitrates if BP allows, treat trigger (ischaemia, arrhythmia, AKI, non-
adherence), CPAP if available and failing. Pneumonia: CURB-65-directed antibiotics per STG
(amoxicillin/amoxi-clav ± macrolide ward level; ceftriaxone + macrolide if severe). Asthma/COPD:
salbutamol + ipratropium nebs, steroids, controlled O₂; magnesium in severe asthma. PE:
anticoagulate on clinical suspicion if bleeding risk acceptable while confirming.

**Traps & pearls.** *Never* attribute new hypoxia in an HIV patient to "anxiety" — walk them and
watch the sat. The commonest missed PE presentation is unexplained sinus tachycardia + hypoxia
with a clear chest. Bilateral pleural effusions are usually transudative (heart/renal/liver) —
don't tap both blindly; a unilateral effusion in SA is TB until proven otherwise. In a wheezing
patient with a raised JVP, "cardiac asthma" (LVF) masquerades as bronchospasm — the treatment is
opposite. Kussmaul respiration is a metabolic sign, not a respiratory disease.

### 2.2 Chest pain (medical)
**Differential.**
- *Common:* musculoskeletal/costochondritis, GORD/oesophageal spasm, stable/unstable angina,
  pericarditis, anxiety.
- *Dangerous (the "big five" to exclude):* ACS (STEMI/NSTEMI/UA), aortic dissection, PE,
  tension pneumothorax, oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave). Add tamponade, myocarditis.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* TB pericarditis (effusive-constrictive, common in HIV),
  cocaine/methamphetamine-associated coronary spasm, Takotsubo, herpes zoster (before the rash),
  sickle chest, prinzmetal/vasospastic angina, coronary aneurysm/vasculitis in young.

**Discriminating history.** Crushing/pressure, radiation to jaw/arm, exertional, diaphoresis,
nausea → ischaemia. *Tearing, maximal at onset, radiating to back, unequal limbs* → dissection.
Pleuritic, positional (worse lying, better sitting forward) → pericarditis. Pleuritic + dyspnoea +
VTE risk → PE. Burning, post-prandial, relieved by antacid → GORD (but never let this exclude
ACS in a diabetic). Reproducible on palpation → musculoskeletal (but 5% of MIs have chest-wall
tenderness — palpation reproducing the *exact* pain is reassuring, not exclusionary).

**Discriminating exam.** BP in *both arms* (>20 mmHg differential → dissection). Pericardial rub
(triphasic, positional). Muffled heart sounds + raised JVP + hypotension = Beck's triad
(tamponade). Signs of DVT. Unilateral hyperresonance (pneumothorax). New murmur (dissection into
aortic valve, papillary muscle rupture). Kussmaul's sign / pulsus paradoxus (constriction /
tamponade — think TB pericarditis).

**Ix + nuance.** ECG immediately and *serially* (a normal first ECG never excludes ACS — repeat
at 15–30 min if pain continues; look for hyperacute T waves, reciprocal change, posterior MI via
V7–V9 and tall R + ST-depression in V1–V3, and aVR elevation with widespread depression = left
main/triple vessel). Troponin: high-sensitivity 0/1h or 0/3h *delta* — a single value early is
uninterpretable; it is the *rise/fall* that means MI. Trop is raised (without ACS) by PE,
myocarditis, sepsis, renal failure, tachyarrhythmia, HF — interpret against the story. CXR (wide
mediastinum → dissection; pneumothorax; effusion). D-dimer/CTPA for PE. Echo for pericardial
effusion, RV strain, wall-motion, aortic root.

**Management anchors.** STEMI: MONA is outdated dogma — the priority is *reperfusion*. Load
aspirin 300 mg + clopidogrel (or ticagrelor), anticoagulate (enoxaparin/heparin), and
**thrombolyse** (tenecteplase/streptokinase per STG) if within window and no PCI access — most SA
state hospitals thrombolyse; know your referral pathway for primary PCI. NSTEMI/UA: dual
antiplatelet, anticoagulation, anti-anginal, risk-stratify (GRACE), refer for angiography.
Pericarditis: high-dose NSAID + colchicine (but *investigate for TB* in SA before assuming
idiopathic; large effusion → tap/drain). Dissection: control HR then BP (labetalol/esmolol target
SBP 100–120, HR <60), urgent CT + vascular/cardiothoracic referral, analgesia.

**Traps & pearls.** Diabetics, women, elderly and CKD patients have atypical/silent MI — a low
threshold for an ECG. *Do not* give thrombolysis for "chest pain + ST elevation" without
excluding dissection (thrombolysing a dissection is lethal) and pericarditis. Posterior and right-
ventricular MI are routinely missed — get V4R and V7–V9 for inferior STEMIs. In SA, a young HIV
patient with pericarditis and a globular heart on CXR is TB pericarditis — steroids per STG plus
TB treatment; drain if tamponading.

### 2.3 Collapse / syncope
**Differential.**
- *Common:* vasovagal (neurocardiogenic), orthostatic hypotension (volume depletion, drugs,
  autonomic), situational (micturition, cough), carotid sinus.
- *Dangerous (cardiac — the killers):* arrhythmia (VT, complete heart block, sick sinus, long-QT
  torsades), structural (aortic stenosis, HOCM, PE, tamponade, dissection, MI), massive GI/aortic
  bleed presenting as collapse.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* Stokes–Adams attacks (sudden, no warning, rapid recovery),
  subclavian steal, Addisonian crisis, hypoglycaemia (not true syncope but presents as collapse),
  seizure vs syncope overlap (convulsive syncope), ectopic pregnancy (always βhCG in a woman),
  aortic dissection, PE, autonomic neuropathy (diabetic, HIV, amyloid).

**Discriminating history.** *Prodrome* (nausea, warmth, tunnel vision, sweating → vasovagal;
none → cardiac/arrhythmia — the dangerous one). Exertional syncope → aortic stenosis/HOCM/
ischaemia — a red flag. Palpitations before → arrhythmia. Postural trigger → orthostatic.
Duration of loss and *recovery speed* (instant recovery favours cardiac/vasovagal; prolonged
post-ictal confusion favours seizure). Tongue-biting (lateral), incontinence, and witnessed
tonic-clonic favour seizure. Family history of sudden death <40 → channelopathy/HOCM. Drug list
(QT-prolongers, antihypertensives, insulin).

**Discriminating exam.** Lying/standing BP (drop >20 systolic / >10 diastolic, or symptoms →
orthostatic). Ejection systolic murmur radiating to carotids + slow-rising pulse → aortic
stenosis. Irregular pulse, bradycardia, cannon a-waves → heart block. Signs of anaemia/GI bleed
(PR exam — melaena). Focal neurology → structural/stroke. Injury pattern (facial injury without
protective response → true LOC).

**Ix + nuance.** ECG is *mandatory and the highest-yield test* — look for: complete/2nd-degree
heart block, long QT (QTc >480 M / >500 F — torsades risk), short PR + delta (WPW), Brugada
pattern (coved ST V1–V2), pathological Q waves, LVH (AS/HOCM), S1Q3T3/RV strain (PE), delta-delta
epsilon (ARVC). Glucose. FBC (occult bleed). βhCG. Troponin if cardiac. Echo for structural.
Consider 24h Holter/telemetry if recurrent unexplained. Tilt-table (rarely available).

**Management anchors.** Risk-stratify: the San Francisco Syncope Rule / canadian rules flag
high-risk (abnormal ECG, HF history, Hb<90, SBP<90, dyspnoea). Admit and monitor cardiac syncope,
exertional syncope, syncope with abnormal ECG or structural disease. Treat the cause:
pace symptomatic high-grade block; correct volume/drugs for orthostatic; refer AS for valve.

**Traps & pearls.** *Exertional syncope and syncope-without-prodrome are cardiac until proven
otherwise* — do not discharge. Never diagnose "vasovagal" in an older patient with a new murmur or
an abnormal ECG. A single myoclonic jerk during syncope (convulsive syncope) is NOT epilepsy —
don't start anticonvulsants. In a woman of reproductive age, collapse = ectopic until βhCG
excludes it. Always check a glucose.

### 2.4 Seizures
**Differential (of the *cause*, since the seizure is a symptom).**
- *Common:* known epilepsy with sub-therapeutic levels/non-adherence/sleep deprivation, alcohol
  withdrawal (6–48h after last drink), hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, fever/infection.
- *Dangerous:* meningitis/encephalitis (esp. HIV — cryptococcal, TB, toxoplasma), CNS mass
  (tuberculoma, toxo, lymphoma, met, abscess), intracranial haemorrhage/stroke, hypertensive
  encephalopathy/PRES, eclampsia (pregnant/postpartum — always ask LMP), status epilepticus.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* CNS toxoplasmosis and tuberculoma (the two commonest space-
  occupying causes of seizures in SA HIV patients), neurocysticercosis (a leading cause of adult-
  onset epilepsy in SA — ring/calcified lesions), cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, autoimmune/
  limbic encephalitis, INH-induced seizures (needs pyridoxine), drug/toxin (tramadol, TCA,
  bupropion, theophylline, organophosphate), non-epileptic (psychogenic) seizures, posterior
  reversible encephalopathy (PRES).

**Discriminating history.** Known epilepsy + missed doses. Alcohol pattern (last drink, prior
withdrawal seizures/DTs). Fever, headache, neck stiffness, immunosuppression → CNS infection.
Focal onset (aura, unilateral) → structural lesion. Pregnancy/postpartum + hypertension →
eclampsia. Drug ingestion/overdose. First-ever seizure in an adult always warrants imaging and
a cause hunt — it is not "epilepsy" until structural/metabolic causes are excluded.

**Discriminating exam.** Post-ictal Todd's paresis (transient focal weakness — mimics stroke).
Meningism, focal neurology (lateralising → mass/stroke), papilloedema (raised ICP), fundal signs.
Tongue-biting, incontinence. Stigmata of chronic liver disease/alcohol. Skin (neurofibromatosis,
tuberous sclerosis, cysticerccal nodules). BP (hypertensive encephalopathy/PRES/eclampsia).

**Ix + nuance.** *Glucose first, always.* Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, urea (uraemia). FBC, CRP. Anticonvulsant
levels (phenytoin, valproate, carbamazepine, phenobarb). βhCG. HIV test. CT brain *before LP* if
focal signs/immunosuppressed/reduced GCS/papilloedema (risk of coning). LP for
meningitis/encephalitis — send CrAg, India ink, TB GeneXpert Ultra + culture, cell count,
protein/glucose, and *always an opening pressure* in HIV (cryptococcal raises it — therapeutic
LPs save lives). EEG for classification/non-convulsive status (rarely emergent-available).

**Management anchors (status epilepticus — a time-critical ladder).** ABC, O₂, glucose, thiamine
(if alcohol/malnourished). Benzodiazepine first-line: IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (or diazepam 10 mg
IV, or IM midazolam/buccal if no line) — repeat once at 5 min. Then load **phenytoin 20 mg/kg IV**
(≤50 mg/min, cardiac monitoring) or phenobarbital/valproate per STG. Refractory (>30 min) →
intubation + anaesthetic infusion (midazolam/propofol/thiopentone), ICU. Treat the cause in
parallel (glucose, Na⁺, empirical ceftriaxone + aciclovir if meningoencephalitis, add TB/crypto
cover per HIV context). Pyridoxine if INH toxicity suspected.

**Traps & pearls.** Every seizing patient gets a glucose — hypoglycaemic seizure is instantly
reversible and instantly fatal if missed. First adult seizure ≠ epilepsy; image and search for a
cause. In SA HIV: seizures + focal signs → CT for toxoplasma/tuberculoma; a raised opening
pressure in cryptococcal meningitis needs *serial therapeutic LPs*, not just antifungals. Don't
forget eclampsia — magnesium, not a benzo-only pathway, and deliver. Non-convulsive status
presents as unexplained coma — consider EEG. Never load phenytoin without cardiac monitoring
(hypotension, arrhythmia).

### 2.5 Altered mental status / delirium
**Differential — use a structured net (AEIOU-TIPS / the metabolic-structural-infective split).**
- *Common:* infection/sepsis (UTI, pneumonia, any), hypoglycaemia/hyperglycaemia (DKA/HHS),
  hypoxia/hypercarbia, electrolyte derangement (Na⁺, Ca²⁺), uraemia, hepatic encephalopathy,
  drugs/alcohol/withdrawal, post-ictal, hypotension/hypoperfusion.
- *Dangerous:* meningitis/encephalitis, intracranial haemorrhage/stroke, raised ICP/mass,
  status (non-convulsive), thyroid storm/myxoedema coma, Addisonian crisis, Wernicke's,
  hypertensive/PRES, CO poisoning, serotonin syndrome/NMS.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* cryptococcal/TB meningitis (subacute AMS in HIV), HIV-associated
  neurocognitive disorder / CNS IRIS, cerebral malaria (travel), thiamine deficiency (Wernicke's
  — the triad is often incomplete), heavy-metal/traditional-medicine toxicity, autoimmune
  encephalitis, dialysis disequilibrium, hyperammonaemia (valproate, urea-cycle).

**Discriminating history (collateral is the investigation).** Time-course (delirium = acute,
fluctuating, inattention; dementia = chronic; the two coexist). Fever/infective symptoms. Drug and
alcohol history, recent changes, missed dialysis, insulin/OHA. Baseline cognition. Trauma/fall.
Known liver/renal/thyroid disease. HIV status and OI history.

**Discriminating exam.** Level and *fluctuation* + inattention (delirium hallmark — test serial
7s/months backward). Vitals incl. temperature, glucose, SpO₂. Pupils (pinpoint — opioids/pontine;
dilated — anticholinergic/sympathomimetic). Asterixis (hepatic/uraemic/CO₂). Focal neurology,
meningism, papilloedema. Signs of liver disease, myxoedema, hydration. Needle marks, medic-alert.

**Ix + nuance.** Glucose, Na⁺/K⁺/Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺/PO₄, urea/creatinine, LFTs + ammonia (hepatic), TFTs,
FBC/CRP, ABG (CO₂, lactate, acid-base), septic screen incl. urine and CXR, HIV. CT brain if focal
signs/trauma/reduced GCS/no metabolic cause. LP if CNS infection suspected (CrAg, Xpert Ultra,
cell count) — CT first if focal/immunosuppressed. Drug levels/toxicology, carboxyhaemoglobin.

**Management anchors.** Treat reversible causes immediately: glucose (after thiamine in
alcohol/malnourished — *thiamine first* to avoid precipitating Wernicke's), naloxone for opioid,
correct Na⁺ *slowly*, oxygen, antibiotics for sepsis/meningitis. Delirium supportive care:
treat the cause, reorientate, minimise sedation, review the drug chart (anticholinergics, opioids,
benzos), correct sensory deficits, mobilise, sleep hygiene. Antipsychotic (haloperidol low-dose)
only for dangerous agitation — never as a substitute for finding the cause; avoid in Parkinson's/
Lewy body (use quetiapine) and prolonged QT.

**Traps & pearls.** Delirium is a *medical emergency and a marker of underlying acute illness*,
not a psychiatric label — "sundowning" and "just confused" are dangerous shorthand. Always check a
glucose and a temperature. Thiamine BEFORE glucose in the alcoholic/malnourished. Hypoactive
delirium (quiet, withdrawn) is more common and more missed than the agitated form and carries
worse prognosis. In HIV with subacute AMS + headache, do the LP with an opening pressure —
cryptococcus. Never sedate an undifferentiated confused patient into a missed diagnosis.

### 2.6 Headache
**Differential.**
- *Common:* tension-type, migraine, medication-overuse, sinusitis, cervicogenic, viral.
- *Dangerous:* subarachnoid haemorrhage (thunderclap), meningitis/encephalitis, raised ICP/mass,
  cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, giant cell arteritis (>50, jaw claudication, ESR↑), acute
  angle-closure glaucoma, hypertensive emergency/PRES, pituitary apoplexy, carbon monoxide.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* cryptococcal/TB meningitis (subacute, HIV), CVST (postpartum,
  prothrombotic, dehydration), low-pressure headache (post-LP), reversible cerebral vasoconstriction
  syndrome, idiopathic intracranial hypertension (young obese woman + papilloedema),
  toxoplasma/tuberculoma with raised ICP, temporal arteritis (vision-threatening — steroids
  *before* biopsy).

**Discriminating history — the SNOOP red flags.** *S*ystemic (fever, weight loss, HIV, cancer);
*N*eurological (focal signs, seizures, altered consciousness); *O*nset sudden/thunderclap (peak
<1 min → SAH); *O*lder (>50 new headache → GCA, mass); *P*attern change/*P*ositional/*P*recipitated
by Valsalva/*P*apilloedema/*P*regnancy. Worst-ever, "thunderclap", maximal at onset → SAH.
Early-morning, worse lying/coughing, vomiting → raised ICP. Visual halos + eye pain → glaucoma.

**Discriminating exam.** Meningism, fever. Focal neurology, papilloedema, reduced GCS. Temporal
artery tenderness/pulselessness (GCA). Red painful eye, fixed mid-dilated pupil (glaucoma). BP.
Fundoscopy always. Neck stiffness. Kernig/Brudzinski.

**Ix + nuance.** Thunderclap → CT brain (sensitivity for SAH ~98% <6h, falls after) → if negative
and suspicion persists, **LP at ≥12h looking for xanthochromia** (bilirubin from RBC breakdown —
distinguishes true SAH from a traumatic tap; RBC count falling tube-1-to-tube-4 is not reliable).
LP for meningitis (CrAg, Xpert, India ink, cell count, opening pressure). ESR/CRP for GCA (start
steroids empirically if suspected — don't wait for biopsy). CT venogram for CVST. IOP for glaucoma.

**Management anchors.** SAH → neurosurgery/referral, nimodipine, BP control, analgesia. Meningitis
→ empirical ceftriaxone (+ ampicillin if listeria risk, + aciclovir if encephalitis features) *do
not delay for LP*; steroids (dexamethasone) with/before first antibiotic in bacterial meningitis.
GCA → high-dose prednisone immediately. Migraine → NSAID/paracetamol + antiemetic + triptan;
avoid opioids. Cryptococcal meningitis → amphotericin B + flucytosine/fluconazole induction per
ART/AHD guideline + serial therapeutic LPs for raised pressure.

**Traps & pearls.** The worst headache of a patient's life that peaks in seconds is SAH until a
CT *and* LP say otherwise — a normal CT after 6h does not exclude it. New headache >50 with jaw
claudication/visual symptoms is GCA — steroids now save the eye. In HIV, a subacute headache +
fever = LP with an opening pressure (cryptococcus, TB). Don't lumbar-puncture a focal/immunosupp-
ressed patient before CT. Fundoscopy is not optional.

### 2.7 Weakness / stroke
**Differential.**
- *Common:* ischaemic stroke, haemorrhagic stroke, TIA, Bell's palsy (isolated LMN VII),
  hypoglycaemia (a stroke mimic), post-ictal Todd's paresis, migraine with aura.
- *Dangerous:* haemorrhagic stroke/SAH, basilar/posterior circulation stroke (misdiagnosed as
  vertigo/labyrinthitis — the deadly miss), cord compression (myelopathy — bladder/bowel + level),
  Guillain–Barré (ascending, areflexic — check FVC), myasthenic crisis, hypertensive emergency,
  hypokalaemic/hyperkalaemic paralysis.
- *Zebras — the young stroke workup (<45–50, or no vascular risk factors):* **HIV vasculopathy/
  vasculitis** (a leading cause of young stroke in SA), **antiphospholipid syndrome** (recurrent
  thrombosis, miscarriages, prolonged aPTT), **carotid/vertebral dissection** (neck pain/trauma/
  chiropractic, Horner's), **infective endocarditis** with septic emboli (fever, murmur, IVDU),
  **cardioembolism** (AF, PFO, rheumatic mitral stenosis — common here), **CNS vasculitis** (PACNS,
  TB/syphilitic/varicella vasculitis), **thrombophilia**, **sickle cell**, **cocaine/meth**,
  **CVST**, **Fabry**, **MELAS** (mitochondrial), **moyamoya**, **paradoxical embolism**.

**Discriminating history.** Sudden onset, focal, maximal at onset → stroke. Preceding neck pain/
trauma → dissection. AF/palpitations, rheumatic heart disease, prosthetic valve → cardioembolic.
Fevers, IVDU, dental work → endocarditis. Recurrent thrombosis/miscarriage → APS. HIV status,
CD4, ART. Age and vascular risk factors (HTN, DM, smoking, dyslipidaemia). Ascending symmetrical
weakness + recent GI/resp illness → GBS.

**Discriminating exam.** UMN (spastic, hyperreflexic, upgoing plantar) vs LMN (flaccid,
areflexic, fasciculations) — localises. Cortical signs (dysphasia, neglect, gaze deviation) →
large-vessel. Crossed signs (ipsilateral face, contralateral body) / vertigo/ataxia/diplopia/
dysarthria → posterior circulation/brainstem. A sensory *level* + sphincter involvement → cord
compression (a surgical emergency). Areflexia + ascending → GBS. Fatigable ptosis/diplopia →
myasthenia. Murmur + fever + splinters → endocarditis. Horner's + neck pain → dissection.

**Ix + nuance.** Glucose (mimic). *Urgent non-contrast CT brain* — distinguishes haemorrhage
(bright) from ischaemia (early ischaemic change subtle: loss of grey-white, insular ribbon,
hyperdense MCA sign; a normal early CT does not exclude infarct). ECG (AF), troponin. In young/
cryptogenic stroke: echo (endocarditis, thrombus, PFO/bubble study), carotid/vertebral imaging
(dissection), vasculitis and thrombophilia screen (ESR/CRP, ANA/ANCA, antiphospholipid antibodies
— lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-β2GPI, repeat at 12 weeks), HIV + CD4, syphilis
serology (RPR/TPHA), blood cultures, MRI/MRA/vessel-wall imaging where available. Cord compression
→ urgent MRI spine.

**Management anchors.** Acute ischaemic stroke: confirm no haemorrhage, thrombolysis (alteplase)
*if within 4.5h and criteria met and available* — most SA state hospitals have limited access;
follow local stroke-unit pathway. Aspirin 300 mg once haemorrhage excluded. Admit to stroke unit,
swallow assessment (NBM until safe — aspiration kills), BP: permissive hypertension in ischaemic
(don't lower unless >220/120 or thrombolysis planned <185/110), tighter control in haemorrhagic.
Glucose, DVT prophylaxis (mechanical initially in haemorrhage), early mobilisation. Cord
compression → dexamethasone + urgent surgical/oncology referral. GBS → IVIG/plasma exchange, FVC
monitoring, ICU if FVC falling.

**Traps & pearls.** Posterior-circulation stroke masquerades as "vertigo/gastroenteritis" — the
5 Ds (dizziness, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, dysmetria) and HINTS exam catch it; a young
person with isolated vertigo + one central sign is a stroke. Always glucose. In SA, an HIV patient
with a stroke gets an HIV-vasculopathy, syphilis, TB-vasculitis and endocarditis work-up — not
just "atherosclerosis". Cord compression with a sensory level and urinary retention is a
time-critical emergency, not "leg weakness for the morning". A "Bell's palsy" that spares the
forehead is a *stroke* (UMN VII).

### 2.8 Fever / sepsis and PUO
**Differential (acute sepsis).**
- *Common:* pneumonia, UTI/pyelonephritis, skin/soft-tissue (cellulitis, abscess), intra-
  abdominal, meningitis, malaria (travel/endemic), TB, line/device infection.
- *Dangerous:* septic shock, necrotising fasciitis, meningococcaemia, endocarditis, febrile
  neutropenia, toxic shock, cerebral malaria, disseminated TB (may present as sepsis with a
  clear chest).
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* the *acutely unwell HIV patient* (see 2.19), disseminated TB /
  TB-IRIS, cryptococcaemia, disseminated NTM (MAC), CMV, histoplasmosis, enteric fever, rickettsia
  (tick-bite fever — eschar), leptospirosis, brucellosis, viral haemorrhagic fever (travel/contact
  — notifiable, isolate).

**PUO framework (fever >38.3°C, >3 weeks, undiagnosed after initial workup).** Four buckets:
**infection** (TB — the commonest cause in SA, abscess, endocarditis, HIV-related OI, brucella,
osteomyelitis, EBV/CMV), **malignancy** (lymphoma, leukaemia, renal cell, hepatoma, atrial
myxoma), **autoimmune/inflammatory** (adult-onset Still's, SLE, vasculitis — GCA in elderly,
polyarteritis, sarcoid, IBD), and **miscellaneous** (drug fever, DVT/PE, thyroiditis,
haematoma, factitious, familial fevers). In SA, TB and HIV-related pathology dominate — an empiric
TB work-up (and often empiric TB treatment after multidisciplinary discussion) is a legitimate
PUO endpoint.

**Discriminating history.** Localising symptoms by system. Travel, tick bites, animal/water
contact, TB contact, sexual history, IVDU, prosthetic material, immunosuppression, drugs,
occupational. Pattern of fever (rarely specific but relapsing/tertian → malaria). Night sweats +
weight loss → TB/lymphoma. Rigors → bacteraemia.

**Discriminating exam — repeated and complete.** New murmur (endocarditis), splinter
haemorrhages, Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, Roth spots. Lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly.
Skin (eschar, rash, needle marks). Joints. Fundoscopy. Rectal/pelvic. Thyroid. Temporal arteries.
Repeat the exam daily in PUO — signs evolve.

**Ix + nuance.** Cultures ×2–3 *before antibiotics* (blood, urine, sputum, CSF as indicated).
Lactate (sepsis severity/clearance). FBC + differential (neutrophilia, left shift; lymphopenia/
eosinophilia clues; pancytopenia → marrow/HIV/TB). CRP/ESR (very high ESR → TB, endocarditis,
GCA, myeloma, lymphoma). CXR, urinalysis. HIV + CD4, TB work-up (Xpert Ultra sputum, urine LAM if
CD4 low/seriously ill). Malaria smear/RDT ×3 if endemic/travel. Echo (endocarditis — TTE then TOE).
LDH/uric acid (lymphoma/tumour lysis). Autoimmune screen (ANA, ANCA, RF, complement). CT
chest/abdo/pelvis for occult source/malignancy. Biopsy (node, marrow, liver, temporal artery) as
directed.

**Management anchors (sepsis — the first hour).** Recognise (qSOFA ≥2, NEWS, lactate). *Sepsis
Six / Surviving Sepsis*: cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics <1h (per suspected source and STG —
e.g. ceftriaxone ± as source dictates; add gentamicin/piptazo/meropenem for severe/resistant),
IV fluid bolus 30 mL/kg balanced crystalloid (titrate — beware in HF/renal/malnourished),
lactate, urine output, source control (drain the abscess, remove the line). Vasopressors
(noradrenaline) if fluid-refractory hypotension → HDU/ICU. Reassess and de-escalate on cultures.

**Traps & pearls.** In SA, "sepsis of unknown source" with a clear CXR and lymphopenia is
disseminated TB until excluded — urine LAM and empiric consideration. Don't anchor on the first
source — a UTI on dipstick does not explain a rigoring, hypotensive patient (asymptomatic
bacteriuria is common). Antibiotics before cultures blinds the workup — draw first if it does not
delay treatment >45 min. Neutropenic fever is an emergency — antibiotics immediately, don't wait
for the source. A "drug fever" is a diagnosis of exclusion, but stopping the culprit is diagnostic.

### 2.9 Cough / haemoptysis
**Differential.**
- *Common cough:* URTI, acute bronchitis, pneumonia, post-nasal drip, asthma/COPD, GORD, ACE-
  inhibitor cough, **PTB** (any cough >2 weeks in SA = TB work-up).
- *Haemoptysis — common:* TB (the leading cause in SA), bronchiectasis, pneumonia, lung cancer,
  PE/infarct, bronchitis.
- *Dangerous:* massive haemoptysis (>200 mL/24h or airway compromise — asphyxiation, not
  exsanguination, kills), lung cancer, PE, aspergilloma in an old TB cavity, Goodpasture/
  ANCA vasculitis (pulmonary-renal syndrome), mitral stenosis, arteriovenous malformation.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* aspergilloma (mycetoma in a healed TB cavity — a classic SA
  cause of recurrent haemoptysis), pulmonary Kaposi sarcoma (HIV), pulmonary vasculitis
  (GPA/MPA — haemoptysis + haematuria + raised creatinine = pulmonary-renal, do ANCA + anti-GBM),
  bronchiectasis (post-TB), pseudohaemoptysis (haematemesis/nasopharyngeal).

**Discriminating history.** Duration + constitutional symptoms (cough >2wk + weight loss + night
sweats + fever = TB). Smoking/pack-years + haemoptysis + weight loss >40yr → cancer. Prior TB
(bronchiectasis, aspergilloma). Volume and character of blood (frothy/mixed with sputum vs coffee-
ground vomit). Haematuria (pulmonary-renal). ACEi use (dry cough). Occupational/environmental.

**Discriminating exam.** Cachexia, clubbing, lymphadenopathy (cancer/TB). Localised crepitations/
bronchial breathing (consolidation), coarse crepitations (bronchiectasis). Signs of RHF (cor
pulmonale). Kaposi skin/palate lesions. Signs of vasculitis (rash, nasal crusting, renal).

**Ix + nuance.** CXR (cavitation, upper-lobe infiltrate → TB; mass; effusion; fungal ball with
air-crescent). Sputum for **Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra** (initial TB test — detects MTB + rifampicin
resistance; "trace" positive interpreted with clinical context), microscopy/culture. HIV + CD4,
urine LAM if advanced HIV. FBC/coag (bleeding diathesis). Renal function + urinalysis + ANCA/
anti-GBM if pulmonary-renal suspected. CT chest (bronchiectasis, mass, aspergilloma, PE if
CTPA). Bronchoscopy (localise bleeding, biopsy). Aspergillus precipitins/IgG.

**Management anchors.** Cough >2 weeks → TB work-up per National TB Guidelines. Confirmed
drug-sensitive TB → standard 2HRZE/4HR (fixed-dose combination) with pyridoxine; DR-TB per NICD
all-oral regimens. Massive haemoptysis: airway is priority — position bleeding side down,
O₂, secure airway, resuscitate, correct coagulopathy, IV tranexamic acid, urgent bronchoscopy/
bronchial artery embolisation/surgery referral. Treat cause. Stop ACEi for ACEi cough.

**Traps & pearls.** Any cough >2 weeks in SA is TB until sputum says otherwise — sending one Xpert
is the single highest-yield action. Massive haemoptysis patients asphyxiate on blood in the good
lung — lie them *bad-side-down*. Recurrent haemoptysis years after "cured" TB = aspergilloma or
bronchiectasis, not necessarily relapse. Haemoptysis + haematuria + AKI = pulmonary-renal
syndrome — this is a vasculitis emergency (plasma exchange, immunosuppression), do the ANCA/anti-
GBM same day.

### 2.10 Weight loss (unintentional)
**Differential.**
- *Common:* TB, HIV (wasting), malignancy (GI, lung, lymphoma, pancreas), uncontrolled diabetes,
  hyperthyroidism, depression, poverty/food insecurity, malabsorption, chronic infection.
- *Dangerous:* occult malignancy, disseminated TB, adrenal insufficiency, advanced HIV.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* thyrotoxicosis (apathetic in elderly), Addison's (weight loss +
  hyperpigmentation + hyponatraemia + hyperkalaemia + postural drop), phaeochromocytoma, coeliac/
  chronic pancreatitis (malabsorption), chronic mesenteric ischaemia (post-prandial pain + food
  fear), lymphoma, chronic infection (endocarditis, brucella, HIV-related).

**Discriminating history.** Quantify (>5% in 6–12 months is significant). Appetite (preserved
appetite + weight loss → hyperthyroid, malabsorption, diabetes; anorexia → malignancy/depression).
Localising GI symptoms (dysphagia, altered bowel habit, blood). Night sweats/fever/cough → TB/
lymphoma. HIV status. Diarrhoea/steatorrhoea → malabsorption. Heat intolerance/palpitations/
tremor → thyroid. Mood/sleep → depression. Access to food (social).

**Discriminating exam.** Cachexia, temporal wasting, muscle bulk. Lymphadenopathy,
hepatosplenomegaly, masses, PR (rectal cancer, melaena). Thyroid (goitre, lid-lag, tremor).
Hyperpigmentation/postural drop (Addison's). Signs of chronic disease. Oral thrush (HIV).

**Ix + nuance.** HIV + CD4, TB work-up (Xpert, CXR, urine LAM). FBC (anaemia, cytopenias), ESR/
CRP, U&E (Na↓/K↑ → Addison's), glucose/HbA1c, LFTs, TFTs, calcium, LDH. Faecal occult blood,
appropriate imaging (CXR, USS abdomen, CT). Age-appropriate cancer screening/endoscopy. Short
synacthen if Addison's suspected. Coeliac serology if malabsorption.

**Management anchors.** Directed at cause. In SA the pragmatic first sweep is HIV + TB + a look
for malignancy and thyroid/diabetes. Nutritional support and social/dietetics referral. Empirical
TB treatment only after adequate effort to confirm (multi-disciplinary).

**Traps & pearls.** Weight loss + preserved/increased appetite narrows sharply to
hyperthyroidism, uncontrolled diabetes, or malabsorption. In SA, TB and HIV are the default first
thoughts and are treatable — never let a cancer work-up delay a TB diagnosis or vice-versa; do
both. Apathetic hyperthyroidism in the elderly presents as weight loss + AF + depression, not
classic thyrotoxicosis. Don't miss Addison's: the electrolyte pattern (low Na, high K, low
glucose, postural drop) is the clue.

### 2.11 Jaundice
**Differential — by mechanism (the split that drives the workup).**
- *Pre-hepatic (unconjugated, haemolytic):* haemolysis (malaria, autoimmune, G6PD, sickle,
  microangiopathic — TTP/HUS/DIC), ineffective erythropoiesis, Gilbert's.
- *Hepatic (mixed/conjugated):* viral hepatitis (A/B/C/E), alcoholic hepatitis, **drug-induced
  liver injury** (TB drugs — INH/RIF/PZA, ARVs, traditional medicines, paracetamol), autoimmune
  hepatitis, cirrhosis decompensation, ischaemic hepatitis, infiltration (TB, lymphoma,
  amyloid), sepsis-associated.
- *Post-hepatic (obstructive, conjugated + pale stool/dark urine):* gallstones (CBD),
  cholangiocarcinoma, pancreatic head cancer (painless jaundice + palpable gallbladder =
  Courvoisier), stricture, PSC, node compression.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* TB DILI (extremely common on SA TB treatment — the classic
  presentation of a jaundiced patient recently started on TB meds), ART hepatotoxicity, traditional-
  medicine hepatotoxicity, cholangitis (Charcot's triad: fever + jaundice + RUQ pain — an
  emergency), acute-on-chronic liver failure, hepatic vein thrombosis (Budd–Chiari), Wilson's
  (young), leptospirosis (Weil's — jaundice + AKI + haemorrhage).

**Discriminating history.** Pale stools + dark urine → obstructive/conjugated. Alcohol quantity.
Drug/TB-treatment/traditional-medicine history and timing (DILI is temporal). Prodrome/contacts
(viral hepatitis). RUQ pain + fever + rigors → cholangitis. Painless progressive jaundice + weight
loss → malignancy. Prior liver disease, transfusions, IVDU, sexual history.

**Discriminating exam.** Stigmata of chronic liver disease (spider naevi, palmar erythema,
gynaecomastia, caput, ascites, splenomegaly). Hepatomegaly (tender → hepatitis/congestion; hard/
nodular → malignancy/cirrhosis). Palpable non-tender gallbladder (Courvoisier → malignant
obstruction). Fever + RUQ tenderness (cholangitis/cholecystitis). Asterixis + encephalopathy
(liver failure). Signs of haemolysis (pallor + jaundice, splenomegaly).

**Ix + nuance.** Split bilirubin (conjugated vs unconjugated). LFT *pattern*: hepatocellular
(ALT/AST ≫ ALP, ratios — AST:ALT >2 with modest rises → alcoholic; ALT in thousands → viral/
ischaemic/paracetamol) vs cholestatic (ALP + GGT ≫ ALT; GGT confirms hepatic origin of ALP).
**Synthetic function = INR + albumin + glucose** — a rising INR and encephalopathy define liver
*failure* (urgent). Haemolysis screen (LDH↑, haptoglobin↓, reticulocytes↑, blood film, Coombs).
Viral serology (HBsAg, anti-HCV, HAV/HEV IgM). USS abdomen (dilated ducts → obstruction; then
MRCP/ERCP). Paracetamol level + nomogram. Malaria smear. Autoimmune (ANA, ASMA, anti-LKM, IgG).

**Management anchors.** DILI on TB treatment → *stop hepatotoxic drugs* (INH/RIF/PZA) once ALT
>3× ULN with symptoms or >5× asymptomatic (per STG), supportive care, reintroduce sequentially
once recovered per protocol; consult before stopping if TB is severe (may need non-hepatotoxic
bridging regimen). Paracetamol overdose → N-acetylcysteine per nomogram. Cholangitis → resuscitate,
antibiotics, urgent biliary drainage (ERCP). Alcoholic hepatitis → supportive, thiamine, nutrition,
consider steroids by Maddrey's DF ≥32. Acute liver failure → transfer/hepatology, manage
encephalopathy, coagulopathy, cerebral oedema.

**Traps & pearls.** The jaundiced patient on TB treatment is DILI until proven otherwise — check
timing and *stop the drugs* early; don't attribute it to "the TB". Charcot's triad = ascending
cholangitis = an emergency needing drainage, not just antibiotics. INR is the vital sign of the
liver — a rising INR in a jaundiced patient means synthetic failure, transfer. Painless jaundice
+ weight loss = pancreatic/biliary cancer until imaged. Don't give vitamin K and assume — a
coagulopathy from synthetic failure won't fully correct.

### 2.12 GI bleeding
**Differential.**
- *Upper (haematemesis/melaena):* peptic ulcer disease (commonest), **oesophageal/gastric varices**
  (portal HTN — cirrhosis, schistosomiasis, portal vein thrombosis), gastritis/erosions (NSAIDs,
  alcohol, stress), Mallory–Weiss tear, oesophagitis, malignancy, Dieulafoy, aortoenteric fistula.
- *Lower (haematochezia):* diverticular, angiodysplasia, colitis (infective, ischaemic, IBD),
  haemorrhoids/anal, colorectal cancer, post-polypectomy; brisk upper bleed can present as
  haematochezia.
- *Dangerous:* variceal haemorrhage (high mortality), massive bleed with shock, aortoenteric
  fistula (herald bleed with aortic graft).
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* schistosomal periportal fibrosis causing varices (endemic SA/
  Africa), HIV-related (CMV colitis, KS, lymphoma), coagulopathy-driven bleed (warfarin/DOAC/
  liver), hookworm (chronic iron-deficiency from occult loss).

**Discriminating history.** Haematemesis (bright/coffee-ground) + melaena → upper. Fresh PR blood
→ lower (or brisk upper). Alcohol/known liver disease/prior varices → variceal. NSAID/steroid use,
dyspepsia → PUD. Retching then haematemesis → Mallory–Weiss. Weight loss/altered bowel habit →
malignancy. Anticoagulants. Prior aortic surgery. Postural symptoms/syncope = significant volume
loss.

**Discriminating exam.** Haemodynamics first (HR, BP, postural drop, capillary refill — shock
before pallor). Stigmata of chronic liver disease/portal HTN (a variceal bleeder). PR exam
(melaena vs fresh blood vs mass). Signs of anaemia.

**Ix + nuance.** FBC (Hb may be *normal early* — it lags acute loss; a low MCV suggests chronic
loss), U&E (**urea disproportionately raised relative to creatinine in upper GIB** — digested blood
protein load + hypovolaemia), LFTs/INR (liver disease, coagulopathy), group & crossmatch, VBG/
lactate. Calculate **Glasgow–Blatchford** (0–1 → consider outpatient; higher → admit/scope) and
**Rockall**. Urgent endoscopy (within 24h; within 12h and after resuscitation for variceal/
unstable).

**Management anchors.** Resuscitate first (two large-bore cannulae, crystalloid, blood —
restrictive transfusion target Hb ~7 g/dL, higher ~8 if cardiac disease; correct coagulopathy/
platelets). Variceal: **terlipressin** (splanchnic vasoconstrictor) + prophylactic antibiotics
(ceftriaxone — reduces mortality) + urgent endoscopic band ligation; balloon tamponade
(Sengstaken) as a bridge if exsanguinating. Non-variceal/PUD: PPI infusion, endoscopic haemostasis,
stop NSAIDs, test/treat H. pylori. Reverse anticoagulation.

**Traps & pearls.** Hb is normal in the first hours of an acute bleed — resuscitate to the
*haemodynamics and the story*, not the first Hb. A raised urea:creatinine ratio with melaena
points upstream. Every suspected variceal bleeder gets antibiotics — it independently reduces
mortality. Restrictive transfusion (target 7 g/dL) *reduces* mortality in variceal bleeding —
over-transfusion raises portal pressure and rebleeds. In SA, portal hypertension is often
schistosomal, not alcoholic — a normal liver with big varices.

### 2.13 Diarrhoea / vomiting
**Differential.**
- *Common acute diarrhoea:* viral gastroenteritis, bacterial (E. coli, Salmonella, Shigella,
  Campylobacter), food poisoning (toxin — Staph, Bacillus), Clostridioides difficile (recent
  antibiotics), traveller's.
- *Chronic/persistent:* TB (intestinal), HIV-related (**cryptosporidium, isospora/cystoisospora,
  microsporidia, CMV colitis, MAC** — by CD4), IBD, malabsorption, colorectal cancer, thyrotoxicosis,
  chronic pancreatitis, giardia, amoebiasis.
- *Dangerous:* cholera (profuse rice-water, dehydration, notifiable), dysentery (bloody — Shigella,
  amoebic, EHEC → HUS), C. difficile colitis/toxic megacolon, severe dehydration/AKI, ischaemic
  colitis.
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* HIV enteropathy and the CD4-stratified OIs, TB enteritis/
  peritonitis, amoebic liver abscess, thyrotoxic diarrhoea, VIPoma/carcinoid (secretory), overflow
  from constipation, drug-induced (metformin, laxatives, colchicine, PPI-associated).

**Discriminating history.** Duration (acute <2wk vs chronic >4wk). Blood/mucus (dysentery/colitis/
cancer). Volume + character (rice-water → cholera; steatorrhoea → malabsorption; nocturnal/
weight loss → organic). Recent antibiotics (C. diff). Travel, food, contacts, water source. HIV
status + CD4. Vomiting predominance + no diarrhoea → consider non-GI (raised ICP, DKA, pregnancy,
drugs, obstruction). Fever, tenesmus.

**Discriminating exam.** Hydration/volume status (mucous membranes, skin turgor, JVP, postural
BP, urine output) — this drives management. Abdominal tenderness/distension/peritonism (colitis,
megacolon, obstruction). PR (blood, mass). Signs of thyrotoxicosis, HIV.

**Ix + nuance.** U&E (AKI, hypokalaemia from losses, hyponatraemia), VBG (metabolic acidosis from
losses/hypoperfusion — often a normal-anion-gap/hyperchloraemic acidosis from bicarbonate loss),
FBC/CRP. Stool: microscopy/culture/OCP, C. diff toxin/PCR, and in HIV — modified ZN for
crypto/isospora, and consider colonoscopy + biopsy (CMV) if CD4 low and persistent. Blood cultures
if febrile/systemic. Thyroid if chronic.

**Management anchors.** **Rehydration is the treatment** — ORS/oral if tolerating, IV isotonic
crystalloid if dehydrated/shocked, replace K⁺. Most infective diarrhoea is self-limiting and needs
*no* antibiotics; treat specifically: dysentery/systemic (ciprofloxacin/ceftriaxone per STG),
cholera (rehydration + doxy/azithro, notify), amoebiasis/giardia (metronidazole), C. diff
(stop the offending antibiotic, oral vancomycin/metronidazole per severity). HIV OIs: crypto →
optimise ART (immune reconstitution is the real cure), CMV colitis → ganciclovir, isospora →
cotrimoxazole. Avoid antimotility agents in bloody diarrhoea/possible C. diff/toxic colitis.

**Traps & pearls.** Vomiting *without* diarrhoea is often not gastroenteritis — think DKA, raised
ICP, pregnancy, obstruction, drugs, MI (inferior). Persistent diarrhoea in advanced HIV needs a
CD4-directed OI work-up, not just "gastro". Bloody diarrhoea + AKI + low platelets in a child/young
adult = HUS (EHEC) — don't give antibiotics (may worsen it). Cholera is notifiable — rehydrate
aggressively (losses can be litres/hour) and report. Hypokalaemia from GI losses can be profound.

### 2.14 Abdominal pain (medical causes)
**Differential — the *medical* mimics of a surgical abdomen (never forget these).**
- *Common:* gastritis/PUD, biliary colic/cholecystitis, pancreatitis, gastroenteritis, UTI/
  pyelonephritis, renal colic, constipation, mesenteric adenitis.
- *Dangerous medical:* **DKA** (abdominal pain + vomiting — check a glucose/ketones before the
  laparotomy), **inferior MI** (epigastric pain), **AAA** (back/abdo pain + collapse — a vascular
  emergency), mesenteric ischaemia (pain out of proportion, AF/vascular disease, lactate↑),
  **Addisonian crisis**, hypercalcaemia, DKA, **lead/porphyria** (acute intermittent porphyria).
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* diabetic ketoacidosis, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (ascites
  + fever + abdo pain — *tap the ascites*), TB peritonitis (ascites, doughy abdomen, high-protein/
  lymphocytic tap, raised ADA), sickle crisis, familial Mediterranean fever, acute intermittent
  porphyria (pain + neuro + hyponatraemia + dark urine), lead poisoning, herpes zoster,
  diabetic radiculopathy, mesenteric ischaemia, hypercalcaemic crisis, adrenal crisis.

**Discriminating history.** Diabetic + polyuria/vomiting → DKA. Alcohol/gallstones + epigastric
radiating to back → pancreatitis. Cardiac risk + epigastric + diaphoresis → MI. AF/vascular +
severe pain, soft abdomen → mesenteric ischaemia. Ascites + fever → SBP. Postural drop + pigment
+ vomiting → Addison. Family history + recurrent pain → FMF/porphyria. Menstrual/βhCG.

**Discriminating exam.** Vitals + glucose. Peritonism (guarding, rebound — surgical) vs soft
abdomen with severe pain (ischaemia, medical). Ascites (shifting dullness — tap it). Pulsatile
mass (AAA). Signs of DKA (Kussmaul, acetone breath, dehydration). Hyperpigmentation. Flank
(pyelonephritis, colic).

**Ix + nuance.** Glucose + ketones (DKA — do this first in any diabetic with abdo pain). ECG +
troponin (inferior MI). Lipase/amylase (pancreatitis — lipase more specific; level does not
correlate with severity — use Glasgow-Imrie/APACHE). U&E, calcium (hypercalcaemia, and Imrie),
lactate (ischaemia — a normal lactate does not exclude early mesenteric ischaemia), VBG. Ascitic
tap: **SBP if PMN ≥250/mm³**; SAAG ≥11 g/L → portal HTN; ADA/lymphocytes → TB peritonitis.
Urinalysis + βhCG. Imaging: erect CXR (free air), USS, CT (AAA, ischaemia, pancreatitis).

**Management anchors.** Treat the specific medical cause (DKA protocol, MI pathway, SBP →
ceftriaxone + albumin, pancreatitis → aggressive fluids + analgesia + treat cause + monitor for
complications, adrenal crisis → hydrocortisone + fluids). Do not send a DKA or an MI to theatre.
Surgical referral for peritonism, AAA, ischaemia.

**Traps & pearls.** *Always check a glucose in a diabetic with abdominal pain* — DKA causes a
rigid, tender abdomen that resolves with insulin and fluids; a laparotomy on a DKA kills. Inferior
MI presents as epigastric pain — ECG anyone middle-aged with upper-abdo pain. Ascites + any
abdominal symptom or fever or encephalopathy → diagnostic tap for SBP (PMN ≥250). Pain out of
proportion to a soft abdomen in an AF/vasculopath = mesenteric ischaemia — high lactate late,
act early. In SA, new ascites with lymphocytic high-protein fluid and raised ADA is TB
peritonitis.

### 2.15 Oedema
**Differential.**
- *Generalised (raised venous/oncotic/renal):* heart failure (raised JVP), cirrhosis (low albumin,
  portal HTN), nephrotic syndrome (proteinuria, low albumin), renal failure (fluid overload),
  malnutrition/protein loss (kwashiorkor pattern), severe anaemia.
- *Localised:* DVT (unilateral — always exclude), cellulitis, lymphoedema (filariasis, post-
  radiation, malignant node), venous insufficiency, compartment.
- *Dangerous:* bilateral pleural effusions + oedema (decompensated failure), nephrotic with VTE/
  infection, SVC obstruction (facial/arm oedema — malignancy/TB nodes), angioedema (ACEi,
  hereditary — airway).
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* constrictive pericarditis (TB — raised JVP + Kussmaul + ascites
  out of proportion to peripheral oedema), nephrotic syndrome causes in SA (HIVAN, membranous,
  FSGS, minimal change, amyloid, diabetic nephropathy), calcium-channel-blocker oedema (amlodipine —
  a common benign cause), hypothyroid myxoedema, beri-beri (wet — high output), protein-losing
  enteropathy, drug (NSAID, pioglitazone, steroids).

**Discriminating history.** Orthopnoea/PND/cardiac history → HF. Frothy urine/known renal → nephrotic.
Alcohol/liver → cirrhosis. Unilateral + calf pain → DVT. Drugs (amlodipine, NSAIDs). Facial/
periorbital + morning worse → renal/nephrotic (vs cardiac worse in evening/dependent).

**Discriminating exam.** **JVP is the pivotal sign** — raised → cardiac/renal/fluid overload;
normal/low → hypoalbuminaemia (liver, nephrotic, malnutrition). Ascites, stigmata of liver
disease. Periorbital oedema (renal). Unilateral warm tender calf (DVT/cellulitis). Pericardial
signs (Kussmaul, pulsus paradoxus → constriction). Signs of hypothyroidism.

**Ix + nuance.** Urinalysis (proteinuria — quantify with **protein:creatinine ratio or 24h; >3.5
g/24h = nephrotic range**). Albumin, U&E/eGFR, LFTs, FBC. **Urine ACR/PCR**, lipids (nephrotic).
Echo (HF, constriction, effusion). Doppler for DVT. Screen nephrotic causes: HIV (HIVAN), HbA1c,
ANA/complement, hepatitis serology, and biopsy where indicated. NT-proBNP if cardiac uncertain.

**Management anchors.** Treat cause. HF: diuresis, ACEi/ARB, beta-blocker, aldosterone antagonist,
manage precipitant. Nephrotic: ACEi/ARB (reduce proteinuria), diuretics, statin, treat cause,
VTE prophylaxis (nephrotics are hypercoagulable), salt restriction; HIVAN → ART is the treatment.
Cirrhotic ascites: salt restriction + spironolactone ± furosemide, tap if tense/SBP suspected.

**Traps & pearls.** The JVP splits the differential: high → the heart/kidney/overload; low →
low albumin. Unilateral leg oedema is a DVT until Doppler says otherwise — don't call it
"cellulitis" and give antibiotics without considering clot. Bilateral pitting oedema with a normal
heart and normal albumin — look at the drug chart (amlodipine). In SA, nephrotic syndrome in a young
HIV patient is HIVAN (collapsing FSGS) — ART changes the trajectory. Facial + arm oedema + dilated
chest veins = SVC obstruction (often mediastinal TB nodes or lymphoma).

### 2.16 Palpitations / arrhythmia
**Differential.**
- *Common:* sinus tachycardia (fever, anaemia, anxiety, hypovolaemia, thyrotoxicosis, PE — treat
  the *cause*), atrial fibrillation/flutter, ectopics (benign), SVT (AVNRT/AVRT).
- *Dangerous:* VT/VF, complete heart block/bradyarrhythmia, WPW with AF (pre-excited — avoid
  AV-nodal blockers), long-QT torsades, Brugada, arrhythmia from electrolytes (K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺).
- *Zebras/consultant-considers:* thyrotoxic AF, rheumatic mitral stenosis with AF (common in SA),
  cardiomyopathy (HIV, peripartum, alcohol, TB pericardial), channelopathies, digoxin toxicity,
  phaeochromocytoma, drug-induced QT prolongation (many — check the chart), electrolyte-driven
  (hypokalaemia + hypomagnesaemia → torsades).

**Discriminating history.** Regular vs irregular, fast vs slow, sudden on/off (SVT) vs gradual
(sinus). Associated syncope/chest pain/dyspnoea (dangerous — structural/ischaemic). Triggers
(caffeine, alcohol, thyroid symptoms). Family history sudden death. Drugs. Rheumatic fever history.

**Discriminating exam.** Rate and *rhythm* (irregularly irregular → AF). BP/haemodynamic
stability. Signs of thyrotoxicosis, HF, mitral stenosis (loud S1, opening snap, mid-diastolic
murmur), volume/anaemia.

**Ix + nuance.** **12-lead ECG during symptoms is diagnostic** — rate, regularity, P-wave
relationship, QRS width (broad-complex tachycardia = VT until proven otherwise, *especially* with
structural disease), delta wave (WPW), QT interval, ischaemia. K⁺/Mg²⁺/Ca²⁺, TFTs, FBC (anaemia),
troponin if ischaemic. Holter/event monitor for paroxysmal. Echo (structural). Digoxin level if
toxicity suspected.

**Management anchors.** *Unstable* (shock, chest pain, HF, reduced consciousness) → synchronised
DC cardioversion (VF/pulseless VT → defibrillate + ALS). Stable narrow-complex regular SVT → vagal
manoeuvres → adenosine. **AF**: rate control (beta-blocker or diltiazem; digoxin if HF/hypotensive)
vs rhythm control by duration/stability; anticoagulate by **CHA₂DS₂-VASc** (weighed against
**HAS-BLED**) — warfarin (INR 2–3, cheapest/available in SA) or DOAC. VT → amiodarone/lidocaine +
correct electrolytes + address ischaemia. Torsades → IV magnesium, correct K⁺, stop QT drugs,
overdrive pacing. WPW + AF → avoid AV-nodal blockers (adenosine/verapamil/digoxin can precipitate
VF) — use procainamide/cardioversion. Treat the *cause* of sinus tachycardia, don't rate-control it.

**Traps & pearls.** A broad-complex tachycardia is VT until proven otherwise — treating it as SVT
with a nodal blocker can kill; when in doubt and unstable, cardiovert. Correct magnesium *and*
potassium together in torsades. Rate-controlling a compensatory sinus tachycardia (sepsis,
hypovolaemia, PE) removes the compensation — find and fix the cause. New AF: hunt the precipitant
(thyroid, sepsis, PE, ischaemia, alcohol, electrolytes, mitral valve). Anticoagulation decision is
CHA₂DS₂-VASc, not gestalt.

### 2.17 Hypertension / hypertensive emergency
**Differential / framing.**
- *Hypertensive **emergency*** = severe HTN (often >180/120) **with acute target-organ damage**:
  hypertensive encephalopathy, acute LVF/pulmonary oedema, ACS, aortic dissection, AKI, acute
  hypertensive retinopathy (grade 3–4 — flames, cotton-wool, papilloedema), eclampsia,
  microangiopathic haemolysis. Emergency = *the organ damage*, not the number.
- *Hypertensive **urgency*** = severe HTN without acute end-organ damage — lower *gradually* over
  days with oral agents; no IV, no rapid drop.
- *Secondary HTN to consider (young, resistant, or crisis):* renal (parenchymal — commonest;
  renovascular — fibromuscular in young, atherosclerotic RAS), endocrine (Conn's — hypertension +
  hypokalaemia + metabolic alkalosis; phaeochromocytoma — paroxysms, headache, sweating,
  palpitations; Cushing's; thyroid; acromegaly), coarctation (radio-femoral delay, young),
  drugs (NSAIDs, steroids, sympathomimetics, OCP, traditional remedies), OSA.

**Discriminating history.** Chest pain (ACS/dissection), dyspnoea/orthopnoea (LVF), headache/
visual/confusion/seizure (encephalopathy/PRES), focal neurology (stroke — different BP targets),
pregnancy (eclampsia). Adherence to antihypertensives. Young/resistant/hypokalaemic → secondary
screen. Paroxysmal symptoms → phaeo.

**Discriminating exam.** BP both arms (dissection). Fundoscopy (retinopathy grade — flame
haemorrhages, exudates, papilloedema = emergency). Signs of LVF (crepitations, raised JVP, gallop).
Focal neurology. Radio-femoral delay (coarctation). Renal bruit (renovascular).

**Ix + nuance.** ECG (LVH, ischaemia), troponin, U&E/creatinine (AKI), urinalysis (proteinuria,
haematuria — renal cause/damage), FBC + blood film (microangiopathic haemolysis — schistocytes),
CXR (LVF, wide mediastinum), fundoscopy. If emergency with neuro features → CT brain (exclude
stroke/bleed — changes BP target). Secondary screen where indicated: renin/aldosterone (Conn's —
off interfering drugs), plasma/urine metanephrines (phaeo), renal imaging/Doppler.

**Management anchors.** *Emergency*: admit/HDU, **controlled** BP reduction — lower MAP by ~20–25%
in the first hour (not to normal — over-rapid drop causes watershed infarction), then toward
160/100 over 2–6h. Agents per organ: labetalol or nitroprusside/GTN infusions (SA STG — often IV
labetalol, or hydralazine, or GTN for LVF/ischaemia), esmolol/labetalol + vasodilator for
dissection (β-block first to avoid reflex tachycardia). Eclampsia → **magnesium sulphate** +
controlled BP + delivery. Acute ischaemic stroke → *permissive hypertension* (only treat
>220/120, or <185/110 if thrombolysing). Encephalopathy/PRES → careful reduction, the syndrome
reverses. *Urgency*: restart/uptitrate oral agents (amlodipine, ACEi/ARB, HCTZ per STG), no
rapid IV lowering, follow-up.

**Traps & pearls.** Treat the *patient and the organ*, not the number — asymptomatic severe HTN
(urgency) is harmed by rapid IV lowering. In hypertensive emergency, drop MAP ~25% max in hour
one — over-correction infarcts brain, heart, kidney. In acute *ischaemic* stroke, high BP is
protective — don't lower it. HTN + hypokalaemia + alkalosis (not on diuretics) = Conn's. Phaeo:
never β-block before α-block (unopposed α → crisis). Sublingual nifedipine is dangerous (uncontrolled
drop) — do not use.

### 2.18 Poisoning / overdose
**Differential / approach.** Treat as *toxidrome + specific antidote + supportive care*. Always:
airway/breathing/circulation, glucose, ECG, paracetamol + salicylate levels (common co-ingestants,
often occult), βhCG, and a risk assessment (agent, dose, time, intent).
- **Toxidromes:** *cholinergic/organophosphate* (SLUDGE + bradycardia/bronchorrhoea — atropine +
  pralidoxime; common in SA — pesticides/"street pesticide" ingestion) · *anticholinergic* (hot,
  dry, dilated, delirious, retention) · *sympathomimetic* (agitated, hypertensive, tachy,
  diaphoretic — cocaine/meth/amphetamine) · *opioid* (pinpoint pupils, low RR, coma — naloxone) ·
  *sedative* (benzo/alcohol) · *serotonin syndrome* (clonus, hyperthermia, agitation) · *NMS*.
- **Specific important ingestions (with antidotes):** paracetamol → **N-acetylcysteine** (per
  nomogram/timing); opioids → naloxone; benzodiazepines → flumazenil (rarely — seizure risk);
  organophosphates → **atropine + pralidoxime**; TCA overdose → **sodium bicarbonate** (for QRS
  >100 ms/arrhythmia); iron → desferrioxamine; methanol/ethylene glycol → fomepizole/ethanol +
  dialysis (high osmolar + anion gap); digoxin → Digibind; β-blocker/CCB → glucagon/high-dose
  insulin-euglycaemia/calcium; carbon monoxide → 100% O₂; warfarin → vitamin K/PCC; INH → pyridoxine;
  cyanide → hydroxocobalamin; sulfonylurea → octreotide + glucose.
- **SA-specific:** paraffin (kerosene) ingestion (aspiration pneumonitis — *do not induce
  vomiting*), organophosphate/carbamate pesticides, traditional-medicine poisoning (herbal
  intoxication, cardiac glycoside plants, potassium dichromate), paracetamol, TCA, rat poison
  (superwarfarins).

**Discriminating Ix + nuance.** ABG/VBG + electrolytes → **anion gap + osmolar gap** (see §4):
high anion-gap metabolic acidosis (MUDPILES); a raised osmolar gap points to toxic alcohols
(methanol, ethylene glycol). ECG (QRS widening → TCA/Na-channel; QT → arrhythmia risk). Paracetamol
and salicylate levels *routinely* (silent, treatable). Glucose. Specific levels (digoxin, lithium,
carbamazepine, iron). Renal/LFTs.

**Management anchors.** Resuscitate; decontaminate (activated charcoal within ~1h if airway safe
and agent adsorbs — not for alcohols/metals/caustics); enhance elimination (urinary alkalinisation
for salicylate/phenobarb; haemodialysis for salicylate, lithium, methanol/ethylene glycol,
metformin lactic acidosis, valproate, theophylline); antidote; supportive care; **psychiatric risk
assessment and referral** for deliberate self-harm. Contact Poisons Information.

**Traps & pearls.** Always send a paracetamol level in any overdose — it is silent for 24h then
kills the liver, and NAC works if given early. Check QRS in TCA overdose — >100 ms predicts
seizures/arrhythmia, give bicarbonate. Organophosphate poisoning needs *large, escalating* atropine
(titrated to secretions, not pupils). Never induce vomiting in paraffin/caustic ingestion. A raised
anion gap + raised osmolar gap = toxic alcohol until excluded. Every intentional overdose needs a
mental-health assessment before discharge.

### 2.19 The acutely unwell HIV patient (Advanced HIV Disease)
**Framing.** AHD = CD4 <200 or WHO stage 3/4 (or <5yr). Run the differential *by CD4 stratum* and
always screen the four AHD killers: **TB, cryptococcal meningitis, severe bacterial infection,
PJP.** Deliver the WHO/SA AHD package: rapid TB screen (incl. urine LAM if CD4 <200 or seriously
ill), **CrAg screening if CD4 <100 (SA reflex ≤100, some ≤200)**, cotrimoxazole prophylaxis,
fluconazole pre-emptive/therapy, and ART (timed to OI).

**Differential by CD4:**
- **Any CD4:** TB (pulmonary + extrapulmonary — the single biggest cause), bacterial pneumonia,
  bacteraemia, HIV itself (seroconversion, HIVAN).
- **CD4 <200:** PJP (subacute dyspnoea, dry cough, exertional desaturation, raised LDH, clear/
  ground-glass CXR — treat with high-dose cotrimoxazole + steroids if PaO₂ <70/desaturating),
  disseminated TB, oesophageal candidiasis.
- **CD4 <100:** **cryptococcal meningitis** (subacute headache/fever/AMS + raised opening pressure —
  CrAg, India ink; amphotericin B + flucytosine/fluconazole induction, *serial therapeutic LPs*),
  toxoplasma encephalitis (ring-enhancing lesions + focal signs — sulfadiazine/pyrimethamine or
  cotrimoxazole), CMV (retinitis, colitis, encephalitis), disseminated TB.
- **CD4 <50:** disseminated **MAC** (fevers, wasting, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, raised ALP),
  CMV end-organ disease, CNS lymphoma, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (JC virus).
- **IRIS** (paradoxical worsening after ART start — TB-IRIS, cryptococcal-IRIS): worsening at
  2–8 weeks after ART; treat the OI, continue ART (usually), steroids for severe TB-IRIS.

**Discriminating Ix.** CD4 + VL, TB work-up (Xpert Ultra + culture + **urine LAM**), serum CrAg,
LP with opening pressure + CrAg/India ink/Xpert if CNS, CXR, LDH (PJP/lymphoma), blood cultures
(incl. mycobacterial/fungal), CMV where indicated, toxo serology + CT brain for focal signs.

**Management anchors / ART timing.** Start/optimise ART; **timing relative to OI matters**: most
OIs → start ART within 2 weeks; **TB meningitis → delay ART ~4–8 weeks** (IRIS mortality);
**cryptococcal meningitis → delay ART ~4–6 weeks** after antifungal induction (early ART increases
mortality). CD4 <50 with non-CNS OI → within 2 weeks. Cotrimoxazole prophylaxis (CD4 <200).
Default regimen **TLD** (tenofovir/lamivudine/dolutegravir); note rifampicin–dolutegravir
interaction (double the DTG dose during rifampicin-based TB treatment per guideline).

**Traps & pearls.** In cryptococcal meningitis the *pressure* kills — measure the opening pressure
and do serial therapeutic LPs; antifungals alone are not enough. Don't start ART too early in TB
or cryptococcal meningitis (fatal IRIS). Urine LAM is most useful in the sick, low-CD4 inpatient —
use it. A "clear chest" with hypoxia in low CD4 is PJP; walk them and check LDH. Always give
cotrimoxazole prophylaxis and screen CrAg at CD4 <100.

### 2.20 The acutely unwell diabetic (DKA / HHS / hypoglycaemia)
**DKA** (typically T1DM/ketosis-prone: glucose usually >11, ketones/ketonaemia ≥3, **HAGMA pH <7.3
or bicarbonate <15**). Precipitants: infection, non-adherence/insulin omission, new-onset, MI,
pregnancy, drugs. **HHS** (typically T2DM/elderly: glucose often >30–33, **osmolality >320**,
*minimal ketosis*, no significant acidosis, profound dehydration, higher mortality, prone to
thrombosis and altered mental status). Mixed pictures occur.

**DKA management (the SA/consensus protocol):**
1. **Fluids first** — isotonic saline (~1 L in first hour, then titrated; add dextrose when glucose
   <14 to allow continued insulin without hypoglycaemia). Correct the deficit over 24–48h; beware
   over-rapid correction (cerebral oedema, esp. young).
2. **Potassium before/with insulin** — insulin drives K⁺ into cells. If **K⁺ <3.3 → replace K⁺ and
   *delay insulin*** until >3.3 (insulin into a hypokalaemic patient causes fatal arrhythmia). K⁺
   3.3–5.0 → add 20–30 mmol KCl per litre. K⁺ >5.0 → no K⁺ yet, recheck.
3. **Insulin** — fixed-rate IV infusion ~0.1 U/kg/h (some protocols a bolus then infusion). Target
   ketone fall ~0.5 mmol/L/h and bicarbonate rise; glucose fall ~3 mmol/L/h.
4. **Monitor** — hourly glucose, K⁺ 2h after insulin then 4-hourly, VBG/ketones/bicarbonate for
   resolution (ketones <0.6, pH >7.3, bicarbonate >15/18, anion gap normalised). Continue IV
   insulin until resolved and eating; overlap with subcutaneous insulin.
5. **Treat the precipitant** (sepsis, MI). VTE prophylaxis (esp. HHS). Do *not* give bicarbonate
   routinely (only considered if pH <6.9). Beware the *normalising* glucose with persistent
   acidosis — the marker of resolution is the *anion gap/ketones*, not the glucose.

**HHS management:** slower, gentler fluid replacement (large deficits but risk of cerebral oedema/
central pontine if too fast), lower-dose insulin (often start after fluids as glucose falls with
fluid alone), aggressive K⁺ and thrombo-prophylaxis, treat precipitant, correct osmolality
gradually (Na⁺ change <10/24h).

**Hypoglycaemia** (<3.9, symptomatic <3.0): the instantly reversible killer. Causes — insulin/
sulfonylurea (long-acting sulfonylurea hypos recur — admit + IV dextrose + octreotide), alcohol,
sepsis, Addison's, liver failure, malnutrition, insulinoma. Treat: oral glucose if conscious; **IV
50% dextrose (or 10% infusion)** if not; glucagon IM if no access; *thiamine before glucose* in
the alcoholic/malnourished. Find and treat the cause; watch for recurrence (sulfonylurea).

**Traps & pearls.** Check K⁺ *before* starting insulin in DKA — hypokalaemia + insulin = arrest.
The glucose normalises before the DKA resolves — keep insulin going (with dextrose cover) until
ketones/anion gap clear. HHS is a prothrombotic, high-mortality state — anticoagulate and correct
slowly. Every altered/collapsed patient gets a finger-prick glucose. Sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia
relapses — admit and give octreotide. Euglycaemic DKA occurs on SGLT2 inhibitors (glucose can be
near-normal — check ketones/gas).

## 3. The normals (what "normal" looks like on this ward)

**Vitals / bedside** [EK]: HR 60–100; RR 12–20 (>22 is a red flag / qSOFA point); BP <140/90
(target varies); SpO₂ 94–98% (88–92% target in CO₂ retainers); Temp 36.5–37.5°C; GCS 15;
capillary glucose 4–7 fasting / <11 random; urine output ≥0.5 mL/kg/h.

**FBC** [EK]: Hb M 13–17, F 12–15 g/dL; MCV 80–100 fL; WCC 4–11 ×10⁹/L; neutrophils 2–7.5;
lymphocytes 1–4; platelets 150–400 ×10⁹/L. Reticulocytes 0.5–2.5%.

**U&E / renal** [EK]: Na⁺ 135–145; K⁺ 3.5–5.0; urea 2.5–7.0; creatinine ~60–110 µmol/L (sex/
muscle-dependent); eGFR >90; Cl⁻ 98–107; HCO₃⁻ 22–28 mmol/L; Ca²⁺ (corrected) 2.20–2.60; PO₄
0.8–1.5; Mg²⁺ 0.7–1.0 mmol/L; corrected Ca²⁺ = measured + 0.02×(40 − albumin g/L).

**ABG (arterial)** [EK]: pH 7.35–7.45; PaCO₂ 4.7–6.0 kPa (35–45 mmHg); PaO₂ >10.6 kPa (80 mmHg);
HCO₃⁻ 22–26; base excess ±2; lactate <2 mmol/L (>4 = severe); anion gap 8–12 (or 10–14 incl. K⁺);
normal A–a gradient ≈ (age/4)+4 mmHg.

**LFTs** [EK]: ALT/AST ~<40 U/L; ALP ~30–130; GGT ~<50; bilirubin <21 µmol/L (conjugated <5);
albumin 35–50 g/L; INR ~1.0.

**Endocrine/metabolic** [EK]: HbA1c <6.0% normal, target usually <7% in DM; TSH 0.4–4.0 mU/L;
free T4 ~9–25 pmol/L; fasting glucose 3.9–5.5; cortisol (9am) high; troponin per assay 99th
centile.

**Inflammatory / other** [EK]: CRP <10 mg/L; ESR <20 (age/sex adjusted — roughly age/2 for men,
(age+10)/2 for women as upper limit); ferritin 30–300 (M) / 15–200 (F); INR 0.9–1.2.

**CSF (normal)** [EK]: opening pressure 10–20 cmH₂O; clear; WCC <5 (all mononuclear); protein
0.15–0.45 g/L; glucose ~⅔ serum (>2.5, ratio >0.6); no organisms. *Bacterial:* ↑↑neutrophils,
↑protein, ↓↓glucose, ↑pressure. *TB/fungal:* ↑lymphocytes, ↑↑protein, ↓glucose, very high
pressure (crypto). *Viral:* ↑lymphocytes, mildly ↑protein, normal glucose.

**Ascites** [EK]: **SAAG ≥11 g/L → portal HTN** (cirrhosis, cardiac, Budd–Chiari); SAAG <11 →
exudate (TB, malignancy, pancreatic). PMN ≥250/mm³ = SBP. High ADA + lymphocytes → TB.

**Pleural fluid — Light's criteria (exudate if any):** pleural/serum protein >0.5; pleural/serum
LDH >0.6; pleural LDH >⅔ upper serum limit. [EK]

## 4. Cross-cutting investigation interpretation (deep)

### 4.1 Acid–base — the full stepwise interpretation
**Step 1 — pH:** acidaemia (<7.35) or alkalaemia (>7.45). **Step 2 — primary driver:** look at
CO₂ and HCO₃⁻. Low HCO₃⁻ + acidaemia → metabolic acidosis; high CO₂ + acidaemia → respiratory
acidosis (and mirror for alkalosis). **Step 3 — compensation** (expected, calculate it — deviation
means a *second* disorder):
- Metabolic acidosis: **Winter's formula** — expected PaCO₂ (mmHg) = 1.5×HCO₃⁻ + 8 (±2). If actual
  CO₂ higher → concurrent respiratory acidosis; lower → concurrent respiratory alkalosis.
- Metabolic alkalosis: expected PaCO₂ ≈ 0.7×HCO₃⁻ + 20.
- Respiratory acidosis: acute HCO₃⁻ rises ~1 per 10 mmHg CO₂; chronic ~4 per 10.
- Respiratory alkalosis: acute HCO₃⁻ falls ~2 per 10; chronic ~5 per 10.

**Step 4 — anion gap** = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻); normal 8–12 (correct for albumin: add ~2.5 per 10 g/L
albumin below 40 — a low albumin *hides* a high gap). **High-anion-gap metabolic acidosis
(HAGMA) — MUDPILES/GOLDMARK:** Methanol, Uraemia, DKA/ketoacidosis (alcoholic, starvation),
Propylene glycol/Paracetamol, Iron/INH, Lactic acidosis (sepsis, ischaemia, metformin), Ethylene
glycol, Salicylate. **Normal-anion-gap (hyperchloraemic) metabolic acidosis — the RTA/GI split
(see 4.2).**

**Step 5 — delta-delta (Δ/Δ)** for a HAGMA, to detect a *mixed* metabolic disorder:
Δ ratio = (measured AG − 12) / (24 − measured HCO₃⁻).
- ≈1–2 → pure HAGMA.
- **<1 → concurrent normal-anion-gap acidosis** (the HCO₃⁻ fell more than the gap rose — e.g. HAGMA
  + diarrhoea/RTA).
- **>2 → concurrent metabolic alkalosis or pre-existing high HCO₃⁻** (e.g. DKA + vomiting, or on a
  background of respiratory acidosis with renal compensation).

**Osmolar gap** = measured osmolality − calculated (2×Na⁺ + urea + glucose [+ 1.25×ethanol if
included]); normal <10 mOsm/kg. **A raised osmolar gap + HAGMA = toxic alcohol (methanol/ethylene
glycol)** until excluded (also mannitol, alcohols). A high osmolar gap with a *normal* gap early
can precede the acidosis.

### 4.2 Renal tubular acidosis — the complete workup (the depth-bar case)
**Trigger:** a **normal-anion-gap (hyperchloraemic) metabolic acidosis** not explained by
diarrhoea. Split the two causes of NAGMA with the **urine anion gap (UAG) = (urine Na⁺ + urine K⁺)
− urine Cl⁻** — a surrogate for urinary NH₄⁺ (the kidney's acid excretion):
- **Negative UAG (e.g. −20 to −50) → appropriate renal acid excretion → GI bicarbonate loss**
  (diarrhoea, ileostomy, ureteric diversion). The kidney is working; the acid loss is extrarenal.
- **Positive UAG (0 to +20+) → impaired renal NH₄⁺ excretion → renal tubular acidosis.**
  (Caveat: UAG is unreliable when the urine anion is a non-Cl⁻ anion — e.g. DKA/ketones,
  toluene/hippurate — then use the **urine osmolar gap** to estimate NH₄⁺ instead.)

**Then discriminate the RTA type** (serum K⁺ + urine pH are the pivots):

| Feature | **Type 1 (distal)** | **Type 2 (proximal)** | **Type 4 (hyperkalaemic)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Defect | ↓distal H⁺ secretion (α-intercalated cell) | ↓proximal HCO₃⁻ reabsorption | aldosterone deficiency/resistance |
| **Serum K⁺** | **Low** (hypokalaemic) | **Low** (hypokalaemic) | **High** (hyperkalaemic) |
| **Urine pH** | **>5.5** (can't acidify, even when acidotic) | Variable; **<5.5** once HCO₃⁻ below threshold (can acidify) | Usually **<5.5** |
| Serum HCO₃⁻ | Can be very low (<10) | Moderate (12–20, self-limited by threshold) | Mildly low (16–22) |
| Stones/bone | **Nephrocalcinosis, calcium stones**, rickets/osteomalacia | Osteomalacia, **Fanconi** (glycosuria, phosphaturia, aminoaciduria, uricosuria) | none characteristic |
| Urine citrate | Low (promotes stones) | normal/high | — |
| Causes | Sjögren, SLE, RA, amphotericin B, hypercalciuria, hereditary | myeloma, Fanconi, acetazolamide, tenofovir, heavy metals, Wilson | diabetic nephropathy, ACEi/ARB, K-sparing diuretics, **trimethoprim**, NSAIDs, Addison's, obstructive uropathy, HIV |

**Confirmatory tests:** urine pH on a fresh acidified sample (a urine pH >5.5 with systemic
acidosis = distal RTA); **fractional excretion of HCO₃⁻ after a bicarbonate load — >15% = proximal
(type 2)**, <5% distal; the fludrocortisone/furosemide or ammonium chloride loading tests where
needed; serum aldosterone/renin for type 4. **Management:** type 1 → oral bicarbonate/citrate + K⁺
(citrate helps stones); type 2 → large HCO₃⁻ doses + K⁺ (potassium citrate) + treat Fanconi cause
(stop tenofovir); type 4 → treat cause, low-K⁺ diet, stop offending drugs, fludrocortisone if
hypoaldosterone, loop diuretic. *SA relevance:* **tenofovir (in TLD) causes proximal tubulopathy/
type 2 RTA and Fanconi**, and **trimethoprim/cotrimoxazole causes type-4-pattern hyperkalaemia** —
both extremely common on the medical wards here.

### 4.3 Hyponatraemia — the full algorithm
**Step 1 — is it true hypotonic hyponatraemia?** Measure/estimate **serum osmolality**:
- *Hyperosmolar* (glucose high → each 5.5 mmol/L glucose above normal lowers Na⁺ ~2.4 mmol/L —
  correct the number; also mannitol) — pseudo-emergency, treat the cause.
- *Isosmolar* (pseudohyponatraemia — hyperlipidaemia, paraproteinaemia) — a lab artefact.
- *Hypoosmolar* (<275) → true hyponatraemia → proceed.

**Step 2 — urine osmolality:** <100 mOsm/kg → appropriate ADH suppression → **primary polydipsia /
low solute (beer potomania, tea-and-toast)**. >100 → ADH acting → assess volume.

**Step 3 — volume status + urine Na⁺:**
- **Hypovolaemic** (dry, postural drop): urine Na⁺ **<30 → extrarenal loss** (D&V, sweating, third-
  spacing); urine Na⁺ **>30 → renal loss** (diuretics — thiazides classically, salt-wasting
  nephropathy, **Addison's** [with ↑K⁺], cerebral salt wasting).
- **Euvolaemic:** urine Na⁺ >30, urine osm >100 → **SIADH** (after excluding hypothyroid and
  adrenal insufficiency).
- **Hypervolaemic** (oedema): urine Na⁺ **<30** → cardiac failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic (avid
  retention); urine Na⁺ >30 → renal failure.

**SIADH criteria** [EK + grounded]: hypotonic hyponatraemia (osm <275), **urine osm >100** (usually
>300), **urine Na⁺ >30**, clinical **euvolaemia**, normal thyroid and adrenal and renal function,
no diuretics. **Causes:** CNS (meningitis, TB, tumour, SAH, stroke), pulmonary (pneumonia, TB, PJP,
lung cancer — SCLC), drugs (carbamazepine, SSRIs, antipsychotics, cyclophosphamide, PPIs), malignancy
(SCLC, ectopic ADH), pain/nausea/post-op. **Fractional excretion of urate** is high in SIADH and
low in hypovolaemia — a useful discriminator; a *low uric acid* supports SIADH.

**Cerebral salt wasting (CSW)** vs SIADH: CSW occurs with CNS pathology (SAH, TB meningitis,
neurosurgery), is **hypovolaemic** with **renal salt + water loss and high urine Na⁺/output** —
the crucial difference from euvolaemic SIADH is **volume depletion**; treatment is *salt and volume
replacement* (the opposite of SIADH's fluid restriction — get this wrong and you harm). CSW is
over-diagnosed; most CNS hyponatraemia is SIADH.

**Correction-rate safety (osmotic demyelination):** raise Na⁺ by **4–6 mmol/L in the first hours
if symptomatic**, and **not more than 8–10 mmol/L per 24h** (≤8 if high-risk: chronic hyponatraemia,
alcoholic, malnourished, hypokalaemic, liver disease). Over-rapid correction → **osmotic
demyelination syndrome** (locked-in, days later). **Severe symptomatic** (seizures, coma) →
hypertonic **3% saline** 100–150 mL boluses to raise Na⁺ 4–6 mmol/L then stop and reassess.
SIADH chronic → fluid restriction ± salt/urea; hypovolaemic → isotonic saline (which turns off ADH
and can then over-correct — watch closely). If over-corrected → re-lower with dextrose ± DDAVP.

### 4.4 Potassium
**Hyperkalaemia** — causes: renal failure/AKI, drugs (**ACEi/ARB, spironolactone/amiloride,
trimethoprim, NSAIDs, heparin, β-blockers**), tissue breakdown (rhabdomyolysis, tumour lysis,
haemolysis, burns), acidosis (shift), Addison's/type-4 RTA, **pseudohyperkalaemia** (haemolysed/
delayed sample, thrombocytosis — repeat before treating a well patient). **ECG progression:**
peaked T waves → PR prolongation/flattened P → wide QRS → sine wave → VF/asystole (ECG changes and
K⁺ level correlate poorly — treat the ECG). **Emergency Rx:** (1) **calcium gluconate 10% 10 mL IV**
(membrane stabilisation — repeat, works in minutes, doesn't lower K⁺); (2) **insulin 10 U + 50 mL
50% dextrose** (shifts K⁺, watch for hypoglycaemia); (3) **salbutamol nebs** (shift); (4) *remove*
K⁺ — furosemide if making urine, resonium (slow), **dialysis if refractory/anuric**; stop the
offending drugs; treat cause.

**Hypokalaemia** — causes: GI loss (D&V — with metabolic acidosis if diarrhoea, alkalosis if
vomiting), renal loss (diuretics, hyperaldosteronism/Conn's, Cushing's, Bartter/Gitelman, RTA type
1/2, hypomagnesaemia — *refractory hypokalaemia is hypomagnesaemia until Mg replaced*), shift
(insulin, β-agonists, alkalosis, refeeding, thyrotoxic periodic paralysis). **ECG:** flattened T,
U waves, ST depression, long QT → torsades/arrhythmia (dangerous with digoxin). **Rx:** replace K⁺
(oral if mild; IV if <3.0/symptomatic, max ~10 mmol/h peripherally, cardiac monitoring if faster),
**always replace Mg²⁺ concurrently**, treat cause.

### 4.5 Calcium and magnesium
**Hypercalcaemia** [EK]: "bones, stones, groans, moans" (thirst, polyuria, constipation, confusion,
short QT). Causes — **primary hyperparathyroidism** (commonest outpatient) and **malignancy**
(commonest inpatient — PTHrP, bony mets, myeloma), plus granulomatous (**TB, sarcoid** — 1,25-vit-D),
thiazides, milk-alkali, thyrotoxicosis, immobilisation, vitamin D/A. **Workup:** corrected Ca²⁺,
**PTH** (high/inappropriate → hyperparathyroid; suppressed → malignancy/granuloma → check PTHrP,
myeloma screen, vit D metabolites, ACE), phosphate (low in PTH-driven), ALP, renal function.
**Rx:** aggressive IV **saline rehydration** (0.9% — the cornerstone), then **bisphosphonate**
(zoledronate) for malignancy; calcitonin for rapid effect; steroids for granulomatous/vit-D;
treat cause. Avoid loop diuretics unless overloaded.

**Hypocalcaemia** [EK]: perioral/peripheral paraesthesia, **Chvostek/Trousseau**, tetany, long QT,
seizures. Causes — hypoparathyroidism (post-thyroidectomy), **vitamin D deficiency**, CKD,
hypomagnesaemia, pancreatitis, tumour lysis, alkalosis (ionised drop). Rx: IV calcium gluconate if
symptomatic/severe; correct Mg²⁺ and vitamin D.

**Hypomagnesaemia** [EK]: causes — GI loss (diarrhoea, PPI, malabsorption), renal loss (diuretics,
alcohol, aminoglycosides, **amphotericin, tenofovir**, cisplatin), refeeding, DKA. Effects:
**refractory hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia** (Mg needed for their handling), arrhythmia (torsades,
prolonged QT), tremor, seizures, weakness. Rx: IV magnesium sulphate; *replace Mg before you can
fix the K⁺/Ca²⁺.*

### 4.6 Anaemia — micro/normo/macrocytic
**Step 1 — confirm and characterise with MCV, then reticulocytes and film.**
- **Microcytic (MCV <80):** iron deficiency (commonest — chronic blood loss: GI, menstrual,
  hookworm; also nutritional), thalassaemia (Mentzer index RBC-rich, target cells, family/ethnicity),
  anaemia of chronic disease (can be micro or normo — TB, HIV, malignancy, inflammation),
  sideroblastic. **Iron studies:** **iron deficiency = low ferritin, low iron, high TIBC/transferrin,
  low transferrin saturation**; **ACD = low/normal iron, low TIBC, normal/high ferritin** (ferritin
  is an acute-phase reactant — a "normal" ferritin does not exclude iron deficiency in inflammation;
  <30 is diagnostic, 30–100 with inflammation is equivocal — check transferrin saturation/soluble
  transferrin receptor). Thalassaemia: disproportionately low MCV for the Hb, normal/high red-cell
  count, raised HbA2 (β-thal).
- **Normocytic (80–100):** acute blood loss, ACD, CKD (↓EPO), haemolysis (retic↑, LDH↑,
  haptoglobin↓, bilirubin↑; film — schistocytes → MAHA/TTP/HUS/DIC/valve, spherocytes → autoimmune/
  hereditary, sickle cells, bite cells → G6PD), marrow (aplasia, infiltration), mixed deficiency,
  early iron/B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, pregnancy dilution.
- **Macrocytic (>100):** **megaloblastic** (B12/folate deficiency — hypersegmented neutrophils,
  oval macrocytes, high LDH, low retic; B12: pernicious anaemia, ileal disease, metformin, vegan;
  folate: poor diet, alcohol, pregnancy, methotrexate) vs **non-megaloblastic** (alcohol, liver
  disease, hypothyroidism, reticulocytosis, myelodysplasia, **zidovudine/AZT and other drugs**).

**SA context** [EK]: iron deficiency (GI loss, hookworm, menorrhagia, poor intake), **ACD from TB/
HIV/chronic infection**, HIV itself (marrow suppression, parvovirus B19 in immunosuppressed →
pure red cell aplasia), TB (ACD + marrow involvement), **AZT-induced macrocytosis/anaemia**, B12/
folate in the malnourished and alcohol-dependent. Always consider *the disease behind the anaemia*
(occult malignancy, TB, HIV) rather than just replacing iron.

**Workup:** FBC + **MCV + reticulocyte count** (the fork: high retic → loss/haemolysis; low retic →
production problem) + **blood film**, iron studies, B12/folate, LDH/haptoglobin/bilirubin/DAT if
haemolysis, renal/liver/thyroid, HIV, and *find the source* (GI work-up in iron deficiency in a man
or post-menopausal woman = colonoscopy/OGD until proven otherwise).

### 4.7 AKI — pre/renal/post + urine indices
**Define/stage (KDIGO 2012):** **Stage 1** — creatinine ×1.5–1.9 baseline *or* rise ≥26.5 µmol/L,
*or* urine <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6–12h. **Stage 2** — ×2.0–2.9, *or* urine <0.5 mL/kg/h ≥12h. **Stage 3**
— ×3.0, *or* creatinine ≥353.6 µmol/L, *or* RRT started, *or* urine <0.3 mL/kg/h ≥24h *or* anuria
≥12h. (Stage by the *worse* of creatinine or urine output.)

**Localise — pre/intrinsic/post:**
- **Pre-renal** (hypoperfusion — hypovolaemia, sepsis, cardiorenal, hepatorenal, drugs [NSAIDs
  constrict afferent, ACEi/ARB dilate efferent]): **FeNa <1%**, **urine osmolality >500**, urine
  Na⁺ <20, urea:creatinine ratio high, bland urine, responds to volume. (FeNa unreliable on
  diuretics — use **FeUrea <35%** instead.)
- **Intrinsic** — see below: FeNa >2%, urine osm <350 (isosthenuric), active sediment.
- **Post-renal** (obstruction — BPH, stones, retroperitoneal/pelvic malignancy, neurogenic bladder,
  blocked catheter): palpable bladder, anuria/fluctuating output, **hydronephrosis on USS** — a
  *reversible* cause, so **image the kidneys/bladder in every AKI without an obvious pre-renal
  cause** and catheterise.

**FeNa** = (urine Na × plasma creatinine) / (plasma Na × urine creatinine) × 100.

**Intrinsic-renal differential** (once pre/post excluded — examine the *sediment* and the story):
- **ATN** (commonest intrinsic — ischaemic or nephrotoxic: prolonged pre-renal, sepsis, contrast,
  aminoglycosides, amphotericin, tenofovir, rhabdomyolysis [check CK, myoglobinuria — dipstick blood
  positive, no RBC], haemolysis): muddy-brown granular casts, FeNa >2%.
- **Glomerulonephritis** (nephritic — haematuria, RBC casts, dysmorphic RBC, proteinuria, HTN,
  oedema): post-infectious, IgA, **HIV-associated (HIVAN — collapsing FSGS, big kidneys, heavy
  proteinuria)**, lupus, ANCA vasculitis (pauci-immune RPGN — pulmonary-renal), anti-GBM. Do
  ANA/dsDNA/complement/ANCA/anti-GBM/hepatitis serology, and *biopsy*.
- **Acute interstitial nephritis** (drugs — NSAIDs, PPIs, antibiotics [penicillins, cephalosporins,
  rifampicin], allopurinol; infection; autoimmune): sterile pyuria, white-cell casts, eosinophiluria,
  rash/fever/eosinophilia — stop the drug ± steroids.
- **Myeloma / cast nephropathy** (older patient, AKI + anaemia + hypercalcaemia + high protein gap
  + back pain): serum/urine electrophoresis, free light chains, **the raised globulin/protein gap
  is the clue** — send it.
- **Vascular** (renal artery/vein, thrombotic microangiopathy — HUS/TTP/malignant HTN/scleroderma:
  MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI).

**Management anchors** [EK/STG]: STOP nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, ACEi/ARB, aminoglycosides, contrast),
optimise volume and perfusion (fluid *if* pre-renal/hypovolaemic — but avoid overload; there is no
role for "renal-dose" dopamine or routine diuretics to protect the kidney), relieve obstruction,
treat the cause (sepsis, GN), review *every drug dose for renal clearance*, monitor K⁺/acidosis/
fluid. **Dialysis indications — AEIOU:** refractory **A**cidosis, **E**lectrolytes (refractory
hyperkalaemia), **I**ntoxication (dialysable — see tox), **O**verload (refractory pulmonary
oedema), **U**raemia (encephalopathy, pericarditis, bleeding). Track the creatinine trajectory and
urine output daily — the trend *is* the decision.

### 4.8 LFT patterns and the liver-failure workup
**Pattern recognition:**
- **Hepatocellular** (ALT/AST ≫ ALP): viral, drug (paracetamol, TB drugs, statins), ischaemic
  (ALT in thousands, rapid rise/fall — "shock liver"), autoimmune, alcohol (**AST:ALT >2**, GGT↑,
  usually modest transaminase rise). ALT >1000 → narrow list: viral, ischaemic, paracetamol,
  autoimmune.
- **Cholestatic** (ALP + GGT ≫ ALT): obstruction (stones, tumour, stricture — dilated ducts on
  USS), drug-induced cholestasis, PBC/PSC, infiltration (TB, sarcoid, mets), sepsis. **GGT confirms
  the ALP is hepatic** (vs bony — check with isolated ALP rise).
- **Isolated hyperbilirubinaemia:** unconjugated → haemolysis or Gilbert's; conjugated → Dubin–
  Johnson/Rotor or early cholestasis.

**Synthetic function / liver failure:** **INR and albumin and glucose** — a rising INR (not
corrected by vitamin K) + hypoalbuminaemia + hypoglycaemia = failing synthesis. **Acute liver
failure** = coagulopathy (INR ≥1.5) + encephalopathy in a patient without pre-existing cirrhosis —
a transfer emergency (watch for cerebral oedema, hypoglycaemia, infection, AKI). Grade
encephalopathy (West Haven I–IV; check ammonia, asterixis). **Workup:** paracetamol level, viral
serology (A/B/C/E), autoimmune (ANA, ASMA, LKM, IgG), ferritin/caeruloplasmin (Wilson in young),
USS ± Doppler (Budd–Chiari), and *stop hepatotoxins* (in SA, TB drugs and traditional medicines
lead the list). Use **Child–Pugh** and **MELD** for chronic-disease severity/prognosis (see §5).

### 4.9 The raised protein gap, pancytopenia, and young stroke (cross-cutting)
- **Raised globulin / protein gap** (globulin = total protein − albumin; wide gap): think **myeloma**
  (SPEP/UPEP, free light chains, β2-microglobulin, skeletal survey, calcium, renal), chronic
  infection/inflammation (TB, HIV — polyclonal), autoimmune, chronic liver disease, amyloid. A wide
  gap + AKI + anaemia + hypercalcaemia + bone pain = myeloma until excluded.
- **Pancytopenia** (all three lineages down): **marrow** (aplastic, infiltration — leukaemia,
  lymphoma, **TB**, mets, myelofibrosis, myelodysplasia), **destruction/sequestration**
  (hypersplenism — portal HTN, splenomegaly), **ineffective haematopoiesis** (B12/folate),
  **consumption** (sepsis, DIC, HLH), **drugs** (chemo, AZT, cotrimoxazole, methotrexate),
  **HIV** (direct + OI marrow involvement + drugs). Workup: film + reticulocytes, B12/folate,
  HIV, LFT/spleen size, LDH/ferritin/triglycerides (HLH), and **bone marrow aspirate/trephine**
  (in SA always send for TB culture/GeneXpert on the marrow). HLH: fever + cytopenias +
  splenomegaly + ferritin↑↑ (>500–10 000) + high triglycerides + low fibrinogen — often TB/HIV/
  lymphoma-driven here, a haemophagocytic emergency.
- **Young stroke workup** (see 2.7): **HIV vasculopathy, antiphospholipid syndrome (lupus
  anticoagulant/anticardiolipin/anti-β2GPI — repeat at 12 weeks), dissection, infective
  endocarditis, cardioembolism (rheumatic MS, AF, PFO), CNS/systemic vasculitis (ANCA, TB,
  syphilis, varicella), thrombophilia, sickle cell, cocaine/meth, CVST, Fabry, MELAS.** Every young
  stroke gets HIV, syphilis serology, echo, vessel imaging, and a thrombophilia/vasculitis screen.

## 5. Scores, charts, and structured tools

**CURB-65** (pneumonia severity/disposition): 1 point each — **C**onfusion (AMTS ≤8 or new
disorientation); **U**rea >7 mmol/L; **R**R ≥30; **B**P systolic <90 or diastolic ≤60; age **≥65**.
0–1 → outpatient; 2 → consider admission/short stay; **3–5 → admit, consider ICU** (30-day mortality
rises steeply: ~0.7% at 0 → ~22% at 4 → ~57% at 5). (CRB-65 without urea for community use.)

**qSOFA** (bedside sepsis flag, ≥2 = higher risk): RR ≥22; altered mentation (GCS <15); SBP ≤100.
**SOFA** (organ dysfunction 0–4 each): PaO₂/FiO₂, platelets, bilirubin, MAP/vasopressors, GCS,
creatinine/urine output. Sepsis = infection + SOFA rise ≥2.

**NEWS2 / TEWS** (SA vitals track — SATS/TEWS in EM): aggregate HR, RR, SpO₂, O₂ requirement, temp,
BP, consciousness → escalation triggers.

**GCS** (3–15): **Eye** 4 spontaneous / 3 to voice / 2 to pain / 1 none. **Verbal** 5 oriented / 4
confused / 3 inappropriate words / 2 sounds / 1 none. **Motor** 6 obeys / 5 localises / 4 withdraws
/ 3 abnormal flexion / 2 extension / 1 none. Report as E_V_M_. ≤8 → secure the airway.

**Wells (PE):** clinical signs DVT (3), PE most likely diagnosis (3), HR >100 (1.5), immobilisation/
surgery (1.5), previous DVT/PE (1.5), haemoptysis (1), malignancy (1). ≤4 unlikely (→ D-dimer;
consider **PERC**) / >4 likely (→ CTPA). **Wells (DVT):** cancer, paralysis/immobilisation, bedridden/
surgery, tenderness along veins, whole-leg swelling, calf >3 cm, pitting oedema, collateral veins,
prior DVT (each 1), alternative diagnosis (−2).

**CHA₂DS₂-VASc** (AF stroke risk → anticoagulate): **C**HF 1, **H**TN 1, **A**ge ≥75 **2**, **D**M 1,
**S**troke/TIA/thromboembolism **2**, **V**ascular disease 1, **A**ge 65–74 1, **S**ex (female) 1.
Anticoagulate if ≥2 (men) / ≥3 (women); 0 (M)/1 (F) → none. **HAS-BLED** (bleeding risk): HTN,
abnormal renal/liver, stroke, bleeding history, labile INR, elderly >65, drugs/alcohol (≥3 = high
— address modifiable factors, don't withhold anticoagulation on the score alone).

**Glasgow–Blatchford (GBS)** (UGIB — need for intervention; **0–1 → consider outpatient**): urea,
Hb, SBP, HR ≥100, melaena, syncope, hepatic disease, cardiac failure. **Rockall** (pre/post-scope
mortality): age, shock, comorbidity, diagnosis, stigmata of recent haemorrhage.

**Child–Pugh** (cirrhosis severity — bilirubin, albumin, INR, ascites, encephalopathy; each 1–3):
A 5–6 (best), B 7–9, C 10–15 (worst). **MELD** = 3.78×ln(bilirubin) + 11.2×ln(INR) + 9.57×ln(creatinine)
+ 6.43 (+ Na⁺ in MELD-Na) — transplant/prognosis; higher = worse 90-day mortality.

**Glasgow–Imrie / Ranson** (pancreatitis severity, ≥3 = severe): **PANCREAS** — PaO₂ <8 kPa, Age
>55, Neutrophils (WCC >15), Calcium <2, Renal urea >16, Enzymes (LDH >600/AST >200), Albumin <32,
Sugar (glucose >10). Also **APACHE II**, **CTSI** (CT severity), **BISAP**.

**Others in daily use** [EK]: **CIWA-Ar** (alcohol withdrawal severity → benzodiazepine dosing),
**CKD staging** (eGFR G1–G5 + albuminuria A1–A3), **AMTS/MMSE/MoCA** (cognition), **NIHSS** (stroke
severity), **PSI/PORT** (pneumonia alternative to CURB), **4Ts** (HIT), **Wells + PERC**, **DKA
resolution parameters** (ketones <0.6, pH >7.3, HCO₃⁻ >15–18, anion gap normalised), **corrected
calcium**, **anion gap / delta-delta / osmolar gap / FeNa / UAG / SAAG / Light's** (all in §4).

## 6. SA-specific reality

**TB/HIV integration** [2023 ART; National TB Guidelines 2023]:
- Every HIV+ patient screened for TB **at every visit** (WHO 4-symptom screen: cough, fever, night
  sweats, weight loss). Symptom-negative → **TB preventive therapy (TPT)** once active TB excluded.
- **Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra** is the initial diagnostic TB test (detects MTB + rifampicin resistance;
  higher sensitivity, "trace" calls need clinical interpretation, especially in prior-TB patients).
- **Urine LF-LAM** in advanced HIV: recommended for inpatients with **CD4 ≤200 or seriously ill
  (any CD4)**, and outpatients **CD4 ≤100 or symptomatic** — cheap, rapid, catches disseminated TB
  the sputum misses. Use it in the sick low-CD4 inpatient.
- **DR-TB (NICD 2023)**: all-oral shorter regimens — **BPaLM/BPaL** (bedaquiline, pretomanid,
  linezolid ± moxifloxacin) era; baseline and monitoring **ECG (QT — bedaquiline/moxi/clofazimine),
  LFTs, and monitor for linezolid toxicity (cytopenias, neuropathy, optic neuritis)**.
- **ART timing with TB:** most OIs → ART within 2 weeks; **CD4 <50 → within 2 weeks**; **TB
  meningitis → delay ~4–8 weeks**; **cryptococcal meningitis → delay ~4–6 weeks** (IRIS mortality).
- **Rifampicin–dolutegravir interaction:** double the DTG dose (extra 50 mg 12h apart) during
  rifampicin-based TB treatment.

**EML / STG formulary specifics** (Adult Hospital Level) [EK/STG]:
- **ART:** default first-line **TLD** (tenofovir/lamivudine/dolutegravir); switch legacy
  efavirenz/NNRTI patients per guideline; check renal function on tenofovir (proximal tubulopathy/
  RTA), avoid tenofovir in significant renal impairment (use TAF/alternatives where available).
- **OI prophylaxis:** **cotrimoxazole** if CD4 <200 (PJP/toxo/bacterial); **fluconazole** pre-emptive
  if CrAg-positive asymptomatic; **CrAg reflex screening if CD4 <100.**
- Antibiotics per STG by syndrome (amoxicillin/amoxi-clav, ceftriaxone, macrolide, ciprofloxacin,
  gentamicin, piperacillin-tazobactam/meropenem for severe). Antihypertensives: HCTZ, amlodipine,
  enalapril/ACEi, atenolol/carvedilol per STG algorithm. Diabetes: metformin, sulfonylurea,
  insulin; SGLT2/DPP-4 access variable. Anticoagulation: **warfarin** is the mainstay (INR
  monitoring; DOAC access limited). Statins (simvastatin/atorvastatin).
- **Resource constraints that change decisions:** limited/rationed access to CT, MRI, echo, dialysis
  (slots — ration by reversibility/candidacy), primary PCI (thrombolysis is the default for STEMI at
  most levels), DOACs, and some specialist drugs. Delayed results turnaround — clinical decisions
  often precede confirmatory tests. Referral pathways (district → regional → tertiary) and transport
  delays shape timing. Blood product availability. High patient volumes.

**Notifiable medical conditions (NMC)** [EK/NDoH]: **TB, measles, meningococcal disease, cholera,
viral haemorrhagic fevers, plague, rabies, acute flaccid paralysis, food-borne outbreaks,
diphtheria, tetanus, typhoid, malaria, and others** — notify via the NMC electronic system. VHF/
cholera/measles trigger urgent public-health response and isolation.

**Medico-legal / practical touchpoints** [EK]: informed consent and capacity; **Mental Health Care
Act** for involuntary psychiatric-medical overlap; documentation of baseline neuro before sedation;
duty to notify (NMC); death certification and referral of unnatural/unascertained deaths to
forensic/medico-legal (procedural deaths, deaths within 24h of admission per policy); occupational
exposure (needlestick — PEP); confidentiality around HIV status; adherence and disability-grant
documentation influencing disposition.

**Classic SA-ward killers to always exclude** [EK]: hypoglycaemia, hyperkalaemia (AKI/drugs),
undiagnosed HIV with an OI, disseminated/miliary TB behind a "septic" picture, cryptococcal
meningitis (measure the opening pressure), TB pericarditis with tamponade, DKA behind an "acute
abdomen", and the drug/traditional-medicine cause (nephrotoxin, hepatotoxin, poisoning).
